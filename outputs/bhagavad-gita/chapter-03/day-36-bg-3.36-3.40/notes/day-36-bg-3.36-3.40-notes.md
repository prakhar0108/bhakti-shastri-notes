## Continuity & Recap from Preceding Classes (BG 3.1 – 3.35) [02:00 – 08:00]

- The teacher maps the chapter as four sections and places today's verses at the start of the last one.
- **First section:** Krishna encouraged Arjuna to perform _niṣkāma karma-yoga_, stating that work done in that spirit does not create bondage.
- **Second section:** for one unable to work without motive, Krishna's instruction was to perform regulated fruitive duty rather than to remain inactive.
- **Third section:** answering the possibility that Arjuna considers himself qualified for _jñāna-yoga_, Krishna stated the qualification — a purified heart, for whom there is no obligatory duty — yet still asked him to work for _loka-saṅgraha_, because example matters. This ran up to BG 3.29 and was concluded in BG 3.30 with work offered to the Lord.
- **BG 3.31–3.35 (previous class):** those who follow Krishna's instruction with faith and without envy become free from karmic bondage, while those who reject it out of envy lose their knowledge. Krishna then stated that forcible restraint does not work, since everyone acts according to his own acquired nature, and that one should act without falling under attraction and aversion, performing one's own duty imperfectly rather than another's duty perfectly.
- The bridge to today: Krishna has just warned against _rāga_. Attachment is itself lust, and lust is the direct obstacle to working without motive. So Arjuna's next question follows naturally — why does a person fall into attachment at all, and why, having fallen, does he act wrongly?

## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 3.36 [06:00 – 16:00]

<!-- verse:3.36 -->

**Context.** Arjuna asks what impels a person to sinful acts even when he does not desire them, as though engaged by force. The teacher calls this the most pertinent of Arjuna's questions in the Gita, because it is every person's question. [08:00 – 10:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Atha kena prayuktaḥ_: By what is one impelled — Arjuna is asking for the identity of the force, not merely for advice. [08:00 – 10:00]
- _Pāpaṁ carati pūruṣaḥ_: A person commits sin. [08:00 – 10:00]
- _Anicchan api_: Although not desiring it — in the ultimate sense the person does not want to do it, or does not want to do it to that degree. [08:00 – 10:00]
- _Balād iva niyojitaḥ_: As if engaged by force, as if someone were seizing him by the throat. [08:00 – 10:00]

#### The question is verifiable in ordinary experience [08:00 – 12:00]

- The teacher grounds the verse in the gross, observable level before treating it philosophically: a person makes his own plan and then breaks it himself.
- Examples he gives:
  - Someone with a habit of sleeping two or three hours in the day writes out a plan not to, then eats stuffed parathas and sleeps.
  - Someone who has formed a habit of misusing social media makes the plan, sets up the system, and still misuses it.
  - Someone installs parental controls on his own phone and still gets around them.
  - Someone loses his temper and says or does a great deal without planning it, and once the temperature drops, wonders why he was so angry and laments it.
- In every case no one else authored the plan and no one else broke it. That is precisely the force of _anicchan api_ and _balād iva_.
- He notes that this is a long-term reading: any thoughtful, moral, or spiritually inclined person does not want to act wrongly, and yet wrong action still occurs. [12:00 – 14:00]

#### What it is not [10:00 – 12:00]

- The teacher raises and rejects one candidate explicitly: the Supersoul does not inspire a person to wrong action.
- He attributes the point to Baladeva Vidyabhushana. [Attribution flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### The mechanism already given earlier in the Gita [12:00 – 14:00]

- Nothing happens without desire; desire must arise first.
- The teacher recalls the sequence described in BG 2.62–2.63: contemplation of sense objects, then attachment, then, as attachment grows, desire, and then the act.
- Because the cycle starts with contemplation, what runs in the mind matters enormously, and quality of thought is decisive over the long term.
- He restates the practical tension: a person's plan points in the direction of dharma, but he periodically moves in the exactly opposite direction, because the immediate desire becomes very powerful even when the ultimate intention is otherwise.

#### The rope between the senses and their objects [14:00 – 16:00]

- The teacher's central image for this verse: between the senses and sense objects there is an attachment, reinforced by _saṁskāras_ from previous lives — an invisible rope.
- It is not always pulling. A person feels bound only when it pulls, and that is the moment of _anicchan_ — "why am I doing this?"
- This is why commitments and vows are taken and then broken.
- The alcoholic and the liquor bottle are not literally tied together, yet on seeing the bottle the desire arises.
- The rope is made the way ropes were traditionally made — by intertwining several strands. Every indulgence in sense gratification twists in another strand, and the rope becomes stronger, and stronger again.
- Eventually the person feels genuinely weak and concludes that he will not be able to get out of it.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Arjuna's question is not abstract: we routinely break plans we ourselves made, which shows that some force other than our stated intention is operating. The binding is an invisible rope of attachment and past impressions between the senses and their objects, and every act of indulgence twists another strand into it. [06:00 – 16:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 3.37 [16:00 – 40:00]

<!-- verse:3.37 -->

**Context.** Krishna names the force: it is lust, born of the mode of passion, which transforms into wrath. The teacher calls this a verse with a very large and, in a sense, dangerous scope to explain, and treats _viddhy enam iha vairiṇam_ as the governing phrase — know this to be the enemy, here, in this world. [16:00 – 18:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Kāma_: The desire to enjoy any object; when frustrated by a boundary it turns into _krodha_. [16:00 – 18:00]
- _Rajo-guṇa-samudbhavaḥ_: Born of the mode of passion. A passionate lifestyle greatly increases the tendency to enjoy; in ignorance there is comparatively less activity. [16:00 – 18:00]
- _Mahāśana_: All-devouring — literally the great eater. [20:00 – 22:00]
- _Mahā-pāpmā_: Greatly sinful — it makes a person commit the greatest of sins. [22:00 – 24:00]
- _Vairiṇam_ / _iha_: The enemy, and specifically the enemy within this world. The teacher's conclusion is that we are in a war situation. [16:00 – 18:00; 24:00 – 26:00]

#### Desire as such is not the enemy; crossing the boundary is [16:00 – 20:00]

- Material desire exists in everyone. The teacher is careful not to equate all desire with _kāma_ in the condemned sense.
- He cites Krishna's later statement in the Gita that He is desire not contrary to dharma. [Reference flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- What is being named here is desire that crosses the boundary of dharma.
- Definitions he offers:
  - Pinpointed: the attraction between man and woman, which he calls the prominent case.
  - Extended: that all the wealth of the world and all its objects are for my enjoyment — leading to designs on others' property and others' partners.
- Because it is born of passion, the practical prescription follows: the more a person comes to the mode of goodness, the less his enjoying tendency crosses the boundary. He therefore stresses keeping lifestyle in goodness — regulation, and spiritual activity. [20:00 – 22:00]
- He observes two simultaneous pressures today: inner _vāsanā_ pulling from within, and the outer environment — billboards and the general situation — pulling from outside. Gadgets keep a person in passion for the whole day. [20:00 – 22:00]

#### _Mahāśana_ — it devours everything [20:00 – 24:00]

- The nature of the soul is service; the enjoying propensity eats that up completely, leaving nothing.
- Once the boundary is crossed, the teacher lists what stops being considered:
  - Social consequence — what people and society will think.
  - Financial consequence — how much money is going, and if there is none, theft.
  - Family responsibility — an addict's obligations to his children.
- _Mahā-pāpmā_ is illustrated by the most extreme cases: serial killers and extreme cases of sexual violence, which a person could not otherwise even imagine, and which no animal commits. The teacher's phrase is that it makes a man into a monster, placing him far below animal consciousness.
- He extends the point beyond attraction to the opposite sex, to greed and to power, naming Hitler and Stalin in that category, and Ravana, whose entire race was destroyed. [22:00 – 24:00]

#### Why it is called an enemy [24:00 – 30:00]

- **Perversion of love.** The teacher summarizes Srila Prabhupada's purport: the love that exists in the spiritual world becomes lust when it comes to the material world.
- **The hijacked capacity.** When the mind runs after satisfying the senses, a person becomes extraordinarily resourceful. He offers the security guard as a case of an apparently dull occupation, someone who sits under a tree and is paid simply for being there — yet the same person becomes intensely creative when the time for sense gratification arrives. His conclusion: this is a capability of the soul, meant to be directed toward Krishna's service, which material energy hijacks.
- **It promises pleasure and delivers pain.** Eat a little more, taste a little more — then overeating, oversleeping, digestive trouble, disease.
- **The proportion is dishonest.** There is just enough pleasure to keep us trapped — a brief, biological, hormonal release. Advertised as a ton, delivered as ten grams. [26:00 – 30:00]
- **What it leaves behind.** Three consequences are listed:
  1. Increased attachment, which pushes the person to repeat the act. The teacher's illustration is the chewing gum of earlier days — sweet briefly, then nothing, yet children buy it again and again.
  2. Karmic entanglement, since behind whatever sorrow we experience in this world there is some sin.
  3. Numerous practical problems alongside — disease from overeating, oversleeping and overindulgence, financial problems, and relationship complications.

#### How to deal with it: the four methods [30:00 – 40:00]

The teacher applies the traditional fourfold approach — _sāma, dāma, daṇḍa, bheda_ — and finds that only one works.

- **_Sāma_ (negotiation) fails.** You cannot talk lust into leaving you alone. His illustration is a pet dog that has contracted rabies: you have kept it lovingly for years, it is the only one who comes to welcome you, and now you must keep it away — and it will not understand. Consciousness habituated to enjoyment for a long time is the same.
- **_Dāma_ (paying it off, fulfilling the desire once and finishing) fails.** This is where _mahāśana_ applies: the more you give it to eat, the hungrier it becomes. Ordinary hunger decreases on eating; this does not.
  - His material illustration: first just a small house; then a two-wheeler is enough; then a four-wheeler; then a change in the variety of four-wheeler; then some furniture; then a change of interiors.
  - He connects this to the verse Srila Prabhupada quotes in the purport from the Mahabharata's Anushasana-parva: all the grain, gold, animals and women of the earth are not enough for one person. Rendered into present terms — all the food stores, the only jeweller holding all the gold, all the car showrooms. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]
  - He notes that similar statements occur in the Bhagavatam and can be seen in the life of Yayati Maharaja, and that such statements are stated principles rather than casual remarks.
- **_Bheda_ fails too** — you cannot easily convince the mind that this is the enemy.
- **Only _daṇḍa_ (punishment) works.** He gives two components:
  1. **Repeated hearing from śāstra** about how dangerous it is, and what happened to those who attempted to enjoy — Ajamila, Saubhari Muni, and the incidents of the kings discussed in class. Hearing this repeatedly is itself a form of punishment to lust.
  2. **_Kāma kṛṣṇa arpaṇa_** — taking whatever one is most attached to and engaging it in the Lord's service. Today the foremost object of attachment is wealth; the second he names is family.
- He identifies this as the reason some practitioners progress slowly: "Krishna sees what you are offering; Krishna also sees what you are holding back." A person chants on beads with one hand while keeping his attachment to sense objects fully intact, and then asks why the attachment does not go. [38:00 – 40:00]
- The principle he draws out is that prescription and prohibition must operate together: chanting done well and a simple life, combined with actually engaging the objects of attachment — wealth, family — in the Lord's service. Otherwise devotional activity continues while the anchor remains lodged in sense gratification.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The compelling force is lust, born of passion, which turns to anger when blocked. It is called an enemy because it promises a ton of pleasure, delivers ten grams, and leaves behind increased attachment, karmic bondage and practical ruin. It cannot be negotiated with or satisfied — only disciplined, by repeatedly hearing what it does and by engaging the very objects of one's attachment in Krishna's service. [16:00 – 40:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 3.38 [40:00 – 54:00]

<!-- verse:3.38 -->

**Context.** Having identified the enemy, Krishna describes how it operates: by covering. The teacher notes that every enemy has a method of attack — one uses a sword, another a gun — and this one covers the consciousness, the way a thief throws dust into a policeman's eyes so that he cannot see for a while. [40:00 – 42:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Dhūmena āvriyate vahniḥ_: As smoke covers fire. [42:00 – 44:00]
- _Yathā ādarśaḥ malena ca_: As a mirror is covered by dust. [42:00 – 44:00]
- _Yathā ulbena āvṛtaḥ garbhaḥ_: As the embryo is covered by the womb. [42:00 – 44:00]

#### Everyone is under attack; only the degree differs [42:00 – 44:00]

- The teacher's emphasis is that this verse establishes universality: no one has escaped. What varies is the thickness of the covering.
- As the covering increases, craving increases; as craving increases, the person becomes more imprisoned.
- His illustration is graded addiction: a drinker with children retains some sense of responsibility but it becomes dull; more addicted, he forgets family responsibility entirely; more addicted still, he also stops registering the financial loss.

#### The three degrees [44:00 – 52:00]

- **Smoke over fire — the thinnest covering, corresponding to general human consciousness.**
  - The fire is still visible behind the smoke.
  - As with a village stove, blowing air on it rekindles the flame, so removal is comparatively easy.
  - But it still needs expertise: someone who does not know how to draw out the smoke will blow in the wrong place and produce more smoke.
  - This is why the human birth carries an option. Under a guru's guidance, in a regulated life, a person can remove the covering and reignite spiritual life. The teacher notes Srila Prabhupada's purport speaks of a slight chance. [46:00 – 52:00]
- **Dust on a mirror — a thicker covering, given for animal species.**
  - Degrees within the example: a little dust and the face is not clear; more dust and only part of the face appears; a great deal and nothing is visible; extreme dust and one cannot even tell there is a mirror underneath.
  - An animal cannot be instructed out of its propensities, and almost no chance remains; animals operate on instinct, confined to eating, sleeping, fearing and mating.
  - The teacher allows for rare exceptions arising from a previous human life, mentioning Bharata Maharaja becoming a deer, while noting this is not the norm.
- **Embryo in the womb — the thickest covering.**
  - With fire, smoke is visible; with the mirror, something may still show; with the embryo, nothing is visible from outside at all.
  - Movement is extremely restricted, and he extends the comparison to plant species, which cannot move and grow in a single dimension, like grass.
- He is explicit that this verse gives the degrees of covering but does not yet say what is covered — that comes in the next verse. [48:00 – 50:00]
- He also applies the gradation within human life: coverings of lust differ from person to person, and a human so far overcome by lust becomes like an animal, or can go lower. [48:00 – 50:00]

#### The practical warning: even regulated enjoyment needs a higher goal [52:00 – 54:00]

- The enjoying tendency in the material world is dangerous even when regulated, which is why a higher goal must accompany regulated sense enjoyment — otherwise one gets stuck there.
- He retells the traditional story of the mouse who asks a sadhu to be made a cat because a cat is chasing him, then a dog, then a lion — and having become a lion, looks at the sadhu and announces it is his breakfast time, at which the sadhu says, "become a mouse again." His stated essence: material desires can be dangerous. [Story flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- The conclusion he draws is to accept one's situation and one's karma with gratitude: perform the duty of one's material circumstances, work the four to eight hours of a job, but not spend life trying to drastically alter material circumstances, and instead try to grow spiritually.
- The counter-example is the endless upgrade: Wagon R, then Creta, then Swift Dzire, then Innova, then Audi — with no limit, and life spent in that race.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Lust attacks by covering consciousness, and no one is uncovered — only the thickness varies. Smoke over fire is human consciousness, thin enough to be removed with expertise and guidance; dust on a mirror is animal life; the embryo in the womb is thicker still. The human form is the one position where removal is genuinely an option, which is why it should not be spent chasing material upgrades. [40:00 – 54:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 3.39 [54:00 – 66:00]

<!-- verse:3.39 -->

**Context.** This verse states what is covered — knowledge — and adds three qualifications of the enemy: it is eternal, it can never be filled, and it burns like fire. [54:00 – 56:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Āvṛtaṁ jñānam_: Knowledge is covered. The soul's natural condition is to be in knowledge, and that is what is covered — which is why we do wrong knowingly. [42:00 – 44:00; 54:00 – 56:00]
- _Nitya-vairiṇā_: The eternal enemy — a constant threat. [56:00 – 58:00]
- _Duṣpūreṇa_: Insatiable; it can never be filled. [58:00 – 60:00]
- _Analena_: It burns like fire. [60:00 – 62:00]

#### Why "eternal" is the operative word [56:00 – 60:00]

- The teacher contrasts this enemy with ordinary enemies: some become friends, some die, some cease to be enemies once you move away, and most have other work and do not pursue you continuously. This one never lets go.
- His supporting observation is that with time everything in this world deteriorates — the body ages, a house begins to decay after twenty or twenty-five years and needs repair, and the nature of the time factor is that everything moves toward destruction.
- Desires are the exception. He recites a verse describing a man whose limbs have given way, whose head is bald, whose teeth are gone, who walks with a stick in old age — and whose hope is still not given up. His comment: desires do not grow old by themselves. [Verse flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- He recalls Bhaktisiddhanta Thakur's illustration of a boat that does not move because the anchor has not been raised — material desires not given up — so that the effect of other activities is not visible. [Attribution flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- He relates a devotee's account of officiating matches at a local college for blind students, where the full range of wrong activity occurs, as an instance of how little this enemy depends on external capacity. [Anecdote retained as presented; see Verification Flags.]

#### Insatiable — so change the object, not the quantity [58:00 – 62:00]

- His instruction is direct: do not attempt to satisfy lust; attempt to surrender. Do not try to satisfy lust — try to satisfy Krishna.
- He points to the change in Srila Prabhupada's followers as evidence that such a transformation is possible: in India a man often cannot give up tea, while those followers gave up lifelong habits, broke every prior pattern, and many accepted sannyasa and became gurus.
- His explanation is that they did not merely perform external practice; they surrendered, and dedicated themselves to the mission. Where that transformation has not occurred, a person remains engaged in his own mission — chanting, ringing bells, doing everything, while the underlying prayer is that everything at home should continue pleasantly — and progress then moves only at the pace of material nature.

#### A fire that draws you closer [62:00 – 66:00]

- As age advances, the capacity to enjoy decreases, but the desire to enjoy does not.
- He distinguishes this fire from ordinary fire: fire means light and heat, and a person naturally runs away from heat. The fire of desire burns the person too, yet drives him closer to the object.
- Indulgence gives only temporary relief, and then fuels and fans the next craving. His salary illustration: a job at thirty to thirty-five thousand is enough, then fifty, then fifty-five, then seventy, then seventy-five — and the craving always grows.
- A warning for practitioners: sometimes it feels as though one has risen above lust. His formulation is that sometimes the enemy is down, but the enemy is not out, and a sudden attack follows.
- The genuine sign of progress he gives is different: as spiritual practice is done sincerely and attraction to the Lord develops, the enjoying propensity gradually decreases. Lust will still send proposals, but they become less frequent and less insistent — arriving with less force, so that one does not fall every time.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
What lust covers is knowledge, which is why we act wrongly while knowing better. Unlike everything else in this world, desire does not age or deteriorate, and it can never be filled — so the remedy is to change its object rather than its quantity. Progress shows not as the absence of proposals but as proposals that come less often and with less force. [54:00 – 66:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 3.40 [66:00 – 74:00]

<!-- verse:3.40 -->

**Context.** Krishna locates the enemy: the senses, the mind and the intelligence are its sitting place, and through them it bewilders the embodied being by covering knowledge. [66:00 – 68:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Adhiṣṭhānam_: The sitting place — the teacher renders it as the house where lust is seated. [66:00 – 68:00]
- _Indriyāṇi, manaḥ, buddhiḥ_: The senses, the mind, the intelligence. [66:00 – 68:00]
- _Etair vimohayati_: Through these it bewilders, covering knowledge. [66:00 – 68:00]

#### The decisive conclusion: the fault is not in the object [66:00 – 68:00]

- The teacher draws the main inference of the verse: lust is not in the external sense objects, and there is no fault in them.
- There is plenty of food in the world, many beautiful people, many attractive vehicles, many valuable things — the fault is not there. Lust is inside.
- His analogy for the internal situation: a man hires a spy, but the spy belongs to the enemy; he appoints a bodyguard, but the bodyguard is from the enemy's party. The whole internal software is corrupted.

#### How each of the three seats operates [68:00 – 72:00]

- **Senses.** The more impure the input given to the senses, the more impurity becomes their habit. His example is diet: someone habituated to meat-eating finds vegetarian food tasteless, while someone who has never eaten meat cannot conceive of eating it. Impurity becomes natural in proportion to what the senses are fed.
- **Mind.** The mind carries many _saṁskāras_ and begins to imagine — how much pleasure will be obtained, how enjoyable it will be. The teacher's formulation is that the mind takes what is X and simplifies it into 100X.
- **Intelligence.** Corrupted intelligence then plans how to fulfil the desire, because the function of intelligence is planning.
- The combined effect: impure senses supply the taste, the mind inflates the projected pleasure, and the intelligence builds the plan — and the person is thoroughly stuck.
- He notes the diagnostic contrast: the same mind that becomes utterly dull during chanting — complaining of tiredness and asking to be left alone — becomes a superman when a desire for sense gratification appears. [68:00 – 70:00]

#### The remedy follows the diagnosis [70:00 – 74:00]

- Because the problem sits in three places, training is required in all three.
- **For the senses — increase the dose.** The duller the mind is toward Krishna consciousness, the more intensely one should engage in it. He recounts a devotee telling Srila Prabhupada that he had been chanting sixteen rounds for many years and lust was not going, and receiving the reply that if a patient is not getting cured with the medicine, the dose should be increased. [Account flagged; see Verification Flags.]
  - He identifies the common self-deception here: asked why they do not read or chant more, people answer that they do not feel like it. His response is that the mind does not accept it initially because it is corrupted, and that the real issue is that the science is not understood.
  - His principle: first comes discipline, then comes relish. Do not discuss whether it feels good yet; build stamina and do it properly first.
  - He invokes Rupa Goswami's jaundice example: the patient finds sugar candy bitter, yet that is precisely the medicine, and it must be taken anyway. Waiting instead for a sudden morning of spontaneous interest is not how it works, and in the meantime the desire only grows stronger — which is also how people become depressed or addicted. [Reference flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- **For the mind — right contemplation.** Engaging the senses in pure things addresses the input; the mind then needs correct contemplation. He returns to his standing instruction to contemplate on each section while studying the Gita, and quantifies the difference: a person reading Srila Prabhupada's books for one hour gains a certain benefit; another investing the same hour but reading forty-five to fifty minutes, revising for two or three minutes, and contemplating and writing for seven or eight minutes gains several times more. The time investment is identical; the benefit is not.
- **For the intelligence — planning for the mission.** Since planning is the function of intelligence, it should be engaged in planning active service in Srila Prabhupada's mission. Everyone should consider, at his own level, what service he can actively render. Otherwise one's own life becomes the mission — this should happen, the car should come, that should be arranged — which he compares to a frog's well, and describes as not very satisfying.
- The class closes by directing students to read the purports, with BG 3.41–3.43 and the solution deferred to the next class. [74:00]

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Lust is not in the sense objects but in three internal seats: impure senses supply the taste, the mind inflates the promised pleasure, and corrupted intelligence plans the fulfilment. The remedy runs along the same three lines — engage the senses in pure things, contemplate rather than merely read, and put the intelligence to work planning service, since discipline precedes relish. [66:00 – 74:00]
</Callout>
