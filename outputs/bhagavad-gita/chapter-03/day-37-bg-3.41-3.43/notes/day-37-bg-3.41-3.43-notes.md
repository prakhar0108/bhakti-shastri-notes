## Continuity & Recap from the Preceding Class (BG 3.36 – 3.40) [02:00 – 22:00]

The teacher opens with an extended on-screen revision of the previous class, because today's three verses are the answer to the problem those five verses set up.

- **BG 3.36 — the question.** Arjuna asks who it is that makes a person commit sin. _Anicchan_ means the person wants to move toward dharma and wants to practise it, yet something drags him to adharma; _balād iva_ means it happens forcefully. The teacher restates the long-term reading established last class: the person does not actually want to act that way, or to that degree, and still does. [04:00 – 06:00]
- **BG 3.37 — the identity.** The force is _kāma_ — desire that breaks every boundary. Dharma and śāstra have set limits; this desire refuses to observe any of them, and when blocked it converts into _krodha_. [06:00 – 08:00]
  - _Mahāśana_ (all-devouring): the teacher's images are a python swallowing its prey whole, and an alcoholic whose addiction devours his money, his dignity, his relationships and often his job.
  - The more it is fed the hungrier it becomes — the more a drinker drinks, the more his system demands.
  - _Mahā-pāpmā_ (greatly sinful): nobody begins by planning to sin to that extent, but as addiction sets in the wrongdoing escalates.
  - His governing formula: the desire for pleasure, once **misdirected**, also becomes **disproportionate** — the direction goes wrong and the limit disappears, which is why a person can commit the greatest of sins. [08:00 – 10:00]
- **BG 3.38 — the reach and the strategy.** Everyone is under attack; no one is exempt, and only the degree differs. The strategy is covering, and three degrees are given: smoke over fire (human beings), dust on a mirror (animal species), and the womb covering the embryo (plant species). [10:00 – 14:00]
  - Applied within human life: the more a person indulges, the more his craving increases, and the more his covering increases.
  - The smoke covering is the one that carries an option — blow a little air and the fire rekindles — but it needs expertise, which corresponds to spiritual practice under guidance. Animals cannot give up enjoyment at all, and the child in the womb is almost entirely helpless with very limited movement. The more a person feels he is enjoying, the more helplessly he becomes stuck.
- **BG 3.39 — eternal and insatiable.** _Nitya-vairiṇā_: ordinary enemies stay for a while and then let go, but this one never does. As age advances the body's capacity to enjoy decreases, yet the desire to enjoy never grows old. _Duṣpūreṇa_: an ordinary hunger ends with two chapatis, but relief here is temporary, indulgence leaves impressions that push for more, and indulgence itself acts as fuel. _Analena_: it burns the person from within — tension and anxiety — whether the lust is for money, for fame or for anything else. Unlike ordinary fire, from whose heat a person runs away, this is a fire everyone wants to come closer to. [14:00 – 18:00]
- **BG 3.40 — where it sits.** The senses, mind and intelligence are the three faculties standing between the soul and the material world, and they are our entire knowledge-acquiring mechanism. The senses are outermost, the mind receives their impressions, and the intelligence functions on that input. Lust is lodged in all three, so all three are corrupted. [18:00 – 22:00]
- **The bridge to today:** having shown that lust sits in all three, the open question is how to deal with it — especially since the desire to enjoy independently and the devotional mood of loving service are exact opposites. BG 3.41–3.43 give that solution, and the teacher frames it as given "in the form of a hint." [20:00 – 22:00]

## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 3.41 [22:00 – 40:00]

<!-- verse:3.41 -->

**Context.** Krishna now concludes. Because this enemy is the enemy of the whole world, extremely dangerous, and everyone is under its control, He gives the starting instruction: regulate the senses first. [22:00 – 24:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Tasmāt_: Therefore — the marker that Krishna is drawing a conclusion from everything He has just said about lust. [22:00 – 24:00]
- _Indriyāṇi ādau niyamya_: First control the senses — first regulate them, before anything else. [22:00 – 24:00]
- _Ādau_: Both "first of all" in sequence and "as early as possible" in time. [32:00 – 34:00]
- _Pāpmānaṁ prajahi_: Destroy this sinful lust. [28:00 – 30:00]
- _Jñāna-vijñāna-nāśanam_: The destroyer of knowledge and of realization — _jñāna_ is knowledge, _vijñāna_ is realization or personal experience, and both are covered. [28:00 – 30:00]

#### Why the senses come first [22:00 – 24:00]

- The teacher's reasoning is positional. Working outward from the soul: soul, then intelligence, then mind and subtle body, then senses, and the senses are the outermost level at which we contact the material world.
- When an attack is coming, you stop it at the outermost point first.
- The senses are also simply the **easiest** to control, which is why Srila Prabhupada's regulative principles operate there.
- He is explicit that this is not a complete solution: the mind and intelligence must also be engaged. But the work starts with the senses.
- His illustration is chanting the Hare Krishna mantra: it is begun with a sense — the mouth speaks first — while the mind runs here and there. The mind will be absorbed gradually; a beginner should not complain that it is not absorbed at the outset. Engage the senses; that is the first step.
- His summary: sense regulation is not the ultimate solution, but without some arrangement of regulation for the senses, everything becomes very difficult.

#### Regulation means boundaries [24:00 – 28:00]

- Regulation happens through boundaries, and the teacher's reference case is Srila Prabhupada's four regulative principles — no meat-eating, no illicit sex, no gambling, no intoxication. Each is a limit drawn around an activity that continues: one still eats, but not meat; one still associates, but not illicitly.
- His principle: **the more protective boundaries we function within, the easier it becomes to deal with lust.**
- Not every boundary carries the same strictness, and the calibration is personal — different people need different limits in different areas. He identifies two categories where the boundary should be strict:
  1. **Where great danger is involved** — drugs and alcohol, where even trying is dangerous.
  2. **Where focus is required** — devotees who do not use the mobile during chanting, or during reading. Different devotees draw this differently, but "no cellphone during chanting" makes the practice noticeably easier.
- Boundaries should be set sense by sense. His examples:
  - **Eyes** — regulation of social media, and parental controls on every device.
  - **Mobile use** — a defined start and end; one should know where the boundary is.
- **Why the boundary does the work that argument cannot.** Two illustrations:
  - An alcoholic is in far greater difficulty when alcohol is also available nearby.
  - Someone who loves sweets, with sweets kept at home, can be lectured on sugar and disease — all of it true — and it will not help, because the lust is already inside, in the mind, the senses and the intelligence. The mind imagines wildly ("you cannot imagine how tasty it will be" — he puts the inflation at 100X), and the intelligence begins to justify ("that much won't matter, just walk it off").
  - If no sweet is kept in the house at all, the person is relatively saved. That is the boundary doing its work.

#### What lust destroys: knowledge, and then realization [28:00 – 32:00]

- _Jñāna-nāśanam_ repeats the earlier point — when a person wants to enjoy, his knowledge gets covered.
- _Vijñāna-nāśanam_ is the sharper claim: **even the person's own realized experience gets covered.** The teacher works this out through cases:
  - A thief steals, is caught, is beaten by the police through whatever degrees of interrogation, comes out with that experience — and steals again.
  - He recalls seeing students drink in college: severe intoxication, complete hangover, vomiting on the spot. That is direct realization, not theory — and the person drinks again.
- The mechanism he identifies is the thought _"this time there will be a difference"_ — last time was a mistake, this time it will not happen that way, this time it will be enjoyable. In other words, **a person rejects his own experience.** This is why people knowingly keep doing bad things, and why the teacher says lust makes a person helpless and turns him into the greatest fool.
- **A third casualty: the urge to know.** The human form carries a faculty that wants to understand — why there is suffering, what the deeper reason is. When a person becomes very lusty, that aspiration to go deeper and to know the truth itself dies out. [30:00 – 32:00]
  - He applies this to why such people feel no need for spiritual life at all: those who live in enjoyment and adjust comfortably into it conclude that they are already enjoying, so what is there to try for; devotees look to them like people who are suffering more.
  - The underlying trap is that dissatisfaction does not produce reorientation. Because lust is _duṣpūreṇa_ and _analena_, the person's conclusion is never "this direction is wrong" but "a better sense object will work, next time it will happen, I will try a little better" — so the dissatisfaction only increases the desire.

#### _Ādau_ — as early as possible [32:00 – 36:00]

- The second sense of _ādau_ is urgency: the moment a person understands that the enjoying propensity is this dangerous, he should begin.
- He connects this to Prahlada Maharaja's instruction that devotional service should be practised from childhood. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- The reason is structural: because lust is insatiable it only grows stronger with time, and the deeper a habit becomes the harder it is to leave. The earlier one deals with it, the easier it is.
- **Culture as an inherited boundary.** Culture used to install a great many boundaries automatically, and people were protected by them; the teacher observes that not much of it is left now. So the boundaries must be set deliberately, sense by sense: pure input for the eyes (regulated social media, parental controls), for the ears (what is being heard, what kind of music, keeping in touch with pure sound vibration), and for the tongue (prasada, and Srila Prabhupada's limits of no meat and no onion or garlic).
- **The cost of not regulating.** Without sense regulation, higher aspiration becomes very difficult — the person cannot even think about anything greater, and life runs almost like an animal's, with eating, sleeping and little else as its purpose. Only when regulation is applied at the level of the body can a person begin to think above it.
- He adds a qualification about helplessness: a person with deep wrong habits, pushed by his impressions, lives a very helpless life and does things knowingly — but he is not in fact completely helpless; lust only makes him feel so.

#### The water tank: purification has two halves [36:00 – 38:00]

- His image for the whole purification process is the rooftop water tank that has gone dirty. Cleaning it requires two things, not one:
  1. **Clean out the dirty water already inside.** In consciousness, this corresponds to the old impressions, which are gradually erased as spiritual activity reinforces spiritual impressions.
  2. **Ensure no more dirty water flows in.** The incoming supply must also be regulated — no further wrong impressions added.
- If dirty water keeps entering regularly, cleaning is pointless: the tank simply fills up dirty again. This is the teacher's justification for why regulation and positive engagement must run together, and he identifies it as exactly what Krishna is suggesting to Arjuna here.

#### The purport: lust as a perverted reflection [38:00 – 40:00]

- The teacher turns to the last paragraph of Srila Prabhupada's purport and draws out three points.
  1. **Lust is only a perverted reflection of love of God, which is natural to every living entity.** His gloss: it is 180° opposite — equal in magnitude, opposite in direction.
  2. **If one is educated in Krishna consciousness from the very beginning, that natural love cannot deteriorate into lust** — which is the ideal, and the argument for training from the start.
  3. **Once love of God has deteriorated into lust, returning to the normal condition is very difficult** — but not hopeless, because Krishna consciousness is powerful enough that even a late beginner can become a lover of God by following the regulative principles of devotional service.
- His analogy for the irreversibility: once milk has become yogurt, the yogurt will not turn back into milk.
- His conclusion is the practical one: do not become hopeless, but do begin working on it the moment you understand.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Krishna's first instruction is positional, not philosophical: start at the senses, because they are the outermost point of contact and the easiest to bound. Boundaries succeed where argument fails, because the mind inflates and the intelligence justifies once lust is already inside. Lust destroys not only knowledge but realization — a person rejects even his own experience with "this time it will be different" — and even the very urge to know. Purification therefore has two halves: erase the old impressions, and stop the supply of new ones. [22:00 – 40:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 3.42 [40:00 – 54:00]

<!-- verse:3.42 -->

**Context.** Having said "senses first," Krishna now gives the internal hierarchy — senses above dull matter, mind above senses, intelligence above mind, soul above intelligence. The teacher first re-situates the verse inside the section's architecture. [40:00 – 42:00]

**The section's architecture, as the teacher maps it** [40:00 – 42:00]

- **3.36** — Arjuna asks who makes a person sin.
- **3.37** — Krishna introduces the enemy and gives its identity: it is _kāma_.
- **3.38** — its power and reach: everyone is caught, only the degree differs.
- **3.39** — its strategy: it covers knowledge; and it has corrupted the entire knowledge-acquiring mechanism — senses, mind and intelligence. The situation is grave.
- **3.40** — what it destroys: both _jñāna_ and _vijñāna_.
- **3.41** — the first move: work on the senses.
- **3.42** — what lies inside and above them.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Indriyāṇi parāṇy āhuḥ_: The senses are said to be superior — superior to dull matter. [40:00 – 42:00]
- _Indriyebhyaḥ paraṁ manaḥ_: The mind is superior to the senses. [40:00 – 42:00]
- _Manasas tu parā buddhiḥ_: The intelligence is superior to the mind. [40:00 – 42:00]
- _Yo buddheḥ paratas tu saḥ_: And he — the soul — is superior to the intelligence. [40:00 – 42:00]

#### Why Krishna gives a hierarchy here at all [42:00 – 44:00]

- The teacher states the purpose directly: to control lust one must **start** at the senses, but starting is not sufficient.
- Above the senses is the mind, above the mind the intelligence, and finally the soul. Ultimately the _jīvātmā_ must become spiritually situated; only then is the solution permanent.
- His formulation: **the war with lust is a long war**, and sense regulation is its beginning, not its conclusion. Someone who thinks "the senses are regulated, that's done, the vehicle is running" has misread the verse.

#### Each step of the hierarchy, as he justifies it [42:00 – 46:00]

- **Senses above dull matter.** Dull matter will not come running to you. Standing in a sweet shop, the gulab jamun will not run over; the senses go to it. That movement is what makes the senses superior.
- **Mind above senses.** The senses are active only when a sense object is present — the eyes work when there is something to see, and are inactive otherwise. The mind, however, can be active in the **absence** of a sense object, because it imagines.
- **Intelligence above mind.** The mind runs on emotions and desires; the intelligence works on logic, and ideally dictates to the mind. His illustration: seeing a gold necklace in a shop, the mind says it is beautiful, take it; the intelligence says check your pocket first and see whether you are in a position to buy it.
- **Soul above intelligence.** Intelligence, mind and senses are all faculties — they are in action because of the soul. His computing analogy: the body is hardware, the subtle body of mind and intelligence is software, and the soul is the **user**. Software needs a user; therefore the soul is supreme.

#### The diagram: why the downward pull happens [46:00 – 50:00]

- The teacher draws the arrangement on screen, top to bottom: Krishna, then soul, then intelligence, then mind, then senses, then sense objects — the material world.
- Sense objects pull the senses downward. Ideally the soul should move upward toward Krishna.
- But the problem established in 3.40 is that lust has entered **all three** intermediate layers — intelligence, mind and senses. So instead of carrying the soul up, all three pull it down. That downward pull is the gravity the person is caught in, and the whole mechanism runs in reverse.
- **This is exactly why a boundary alone is insufficient.** The boundary prescribed in 3.41 is placed between the **senses and the sense objects** — at one point only. The mind still has lust in it, and so does the intelligence. Those must be worked on too, which is why the hierarchy is given here.

#### Three routes by which a person goes wrong [48:00 – 52:00]

Because lust sits in three places, the failure can start in any of the three:

1. **Through the senses.** A person is walking, something is seen on the wrong side — a sense object registers, the senses go toward it, and the mind follows behind.
2. **Through the mind.** A person is in a temple hall or some similarly protected place where no sense object is present at all, and the mind starts imagining anyway — and then the senses too move in the wrong direction.
3. **Through the intelligence.** The intelligence becomes corrupt and begins planning. His two examples:
   - Continuing the gold necklace case: the mind wants it, the intelligence knows there is no money, and concludes "then let's steal it" — and starts building the plan.
   - A student who wants to misuse social media: his intelligence works out how to crack or break the parental control lock.

- He applies this to a familiar pattern: students under regulation at school or with parents run fine, and change the moment they reach college — because the boundary has ended, while the mind was never healthy to begin with, and bad association then corrupts the whole system.
- **The chariot.** The soul is the passenger, and the vehicle should go according to the passenger. The senses are the horses, the mind is the reins, the intelligence is the charioteer. But in the present condition all of them are drunk — the horses have had a drink, the reins hang loose and swing about, and the charioteer has had a drink too. Where will that chariot go? The teacher's summary of the conditioned state is lusty intelligence, a fickle mind and uncontrolled senses. [50:00 – 52:00]

#### The practical corollary: strengthen the intelligence [52:00 – 54:00]

- The first conclusion is that **work is needed at every level**, because lust has entered everywhere.
- The second is specific: since the intelligence is higher than the mind, **strengthening the intelligence is especially important at the practitioner's stage.** If the intelligence is not strong, devotional practice becomes very difficult.
- The diagnostic he offers: when _sādhana_ is going well — reading, hearing — there is a strength present, so that the mind may imagine something wrong but the person does not act on it. When the intelligence weakens, the guard is gone and the person falls. He connects this to the statement on the destruction of intelligence in BG 2.62–2.63. [Reference flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- The mind also has to be absorbed in spiritual things, and that needs practice — but the intelligence needs **regular input**, and without it, it will get into difficulty. This is why the teacher places the emphasis on the intelligence here.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The hierarchy is given to prevent a premature conclusion: senses first does not mean senses only. A boundary is placed at one point — between the senses and their objects — while lust remains seated in the mind and the intelligence, so a person can fall through any of three routes: the senses meeting an object, the mind imagining without one, or the intelligence planning the fulfilment. Hence the chariot with drunk horses, loose reins and a drunk charioteer, and hence the emphasis on giving the intelligence regular input. [40:00 – 54:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 3.43 [54:00 – 68:00]

<!-- verse:3.43 -->

**Context.** The concluding verse of the chapter. Knowing yourself to be beyond the intelligence, steady the mind by deliberate intelligence, and conquer the formidable enemy in the form of lust. [54:00 – 56:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Evaṁ buddheḥ paraṁ buddhvā_: Thus knowing that you are beyond the intelligence — that the soul is beyond _buddhi_. [54:00 – 56:00]
- _Saṁstabhya_: To steady, to make firm. [54:00 – 56:00]
- _Ātmānam ātmanā_: The teacher notes that _ātmā_ carries many meanings in Sanskrit — it is used for the mind, the intelligence, the body and the soul. He reads the second line as _steady your mind by means of the intelligence_. [54:00 – 56:00]
- _Jahi śatruṁ mahā-bāho_: O mighty-armed Arjuna, defeat this enemy. [54:00 – 56:00]
- _Kāma-rūpam_: The enemy in the form of lust. [54:00 – 56:00]

#### Reversing the diagram [56:00 – 58:00]

- Returning to the drawing: lust is in the intelligence, in the mind and in the senses; the sense objects pull downward; a boundary has been placed at the senses.
- What 3.43 adds is the instruction to become **spiritually situated**. You are beyond the intelligence — so use the intelligence to steady the mind, and reverse the direction of the whole system.
- His phrasing: the pull that is going downward has to be turned into a pull upward; control the downward pull and connect the whole apparatus above.
- He names the situation an **inner tug of war**, running in the life of every conditioned soul.

#### Two ways to become spiritually situated [56:00 – 62:00]

The teacher distinguishes the path of knowledge from the path of devotion, noting that the practical application of this section will come in the _bhakti-yoga_ section later.

**1. Through knowledge — the _jñānī_'s method.** [56:00 – 58:00]

- One who follows _niṣkāma karma-yoga_ becomes situated in knowledge and thinks: I am not the body, I am the soul.
- The teacher is careful that this is genuine and consistent, not superficial: the _jñānī_ reads śāstra for ten, eleven, twelve hours, understands it, and contemplates it — _ahaṁ brahmāsmi_, I am spirit.
- His reasoning at that level: if I am not the body at all, then what do I get when the body receives some pleasure? Satisfying the senses will do nothing for me.
- **The kurta analogy:** I am hungry — and someone pours mango juice into the pocket of my kurta, and puts palak paneer in the other pocket. Will that satisfy me? It may satisfy the kurta. That is how the _jñānī_ regards sense gratification.
- But the teacher marks this as **difficult**, because it consists almost entirely of rejection: there is nothing positive to connect to.

**2. Through devotion — total inner reorientation.** [58:00 – 62:00]

- The same diagram, read upward: Krishna at the top, then the soul, then the intelligence, then the mind, then the senses, then the sense objects. At present the senses want to run to sense objects, the mind imagines pleasure from them, and the intelligence too runs downward.
- Being spiritually situated means **engaging all of them upward** rather than merely restraining them:
  - **Sense objects** are themselves offered to the Lord — a beautiful flower is taken and offered; something good seen in the market is bought and offered.
  - **Senses** are engaged in perceiving the Lord — the eyes in taking _darśana_, the ears in hearing about Him. All the senses, including the working senses, are placed in His service. [Verse recitation flagged; see Verification Flags.]
  - **Mind** contemplates and remembers — reflecting on the Lord's qualities, contemplating what is being read and heard.
  - **Intelligence** is put to the questions of how to serve Krishna and how to glorify Krishna.
- He contrasts the two paths directly: in the path of knowledge there is **only rejection** — the senses are not allowed down, the mind is not allowed to contemplate wrong things, and the intelligence is kept continuously in śāstra. In devotion, the practitioner knows he is not merely the soul — the Supersoul is also within him, and he is a part of the Lord. The teacher calls this **easier and more natural**. [Verse reference flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Why the mind cannot be skipped [62:00 – 64:00]

- If the senses are being engaged but nothing is entering the mind, stability stays very low, because the mind then constantly imagines wrong things.
- His practical instruments for the mind are remembrance, **writing and reflection**, which he identifies as very important.
- **The senses' gross function does not change.** Eyes will see something, good or bad; ears will hear something, good or bad. Arjuna is a _kṣatriya_, so he will fight — here, there, or in the forest, he will shoot arrows; a doctor will operate. The senses do their work regardless.
- What changes is **why** and **how** — and that is where the mind and intelligence enter. So the actual inner change depends far more on the intelligence and the mind than on the senses.

#### Values and desires: the two things that must change [64:00 – 68:00]

- The teacher assigns the two clearly: **the intelligence decides the value system**, and **the mind holds the prior desires.** Changing both is what matters.
- **The doctor case, worked through.** A doctor will perform surgery either way — but why is he doing it, to earn money or to serve? And how is he doing it, carefully or in a rush, sometimes leaving surgical instruments inside? The senses engage according to their function; values and desires decide the rest.
- **Two profiles emerge:**
  - Where **both** are corrupt — corrupt values and corrupt desires — the person's very purpose is to enjoy wrongly and break the system. He is entirely within lust's grip.
  - Where the person is a **practitioner**, the desires are still impure — everyone's mind carries wrong _vāsanās_ from a long time back, because there are a great many past impressions — but the **value system is sound**: he wants to act according to dharma and move toward Krishna.
- **This is why the intelligence must be strong.** A strong intelligence, informed by śāstra about what is right and what is wrong, regulates the wrong desires and keeps the person engaged in dharma. As he continues in that way and engages the mind in positive things, the desires themselves gradually become pure. The teacher marks that arrival as **success**.
- **The essence** he states twice: finally, everything is to be engaged in the Lord. Control the mind through the intelligence, and place the whole system in His service.
- **The closing principle.** As a person implements this in life, it gradually becomes a habit: _"we have to cultivate the habit of choosing Krishna."_ The war with lust is not won in a single encounter. The process is **gradual and incremental** — as the person applies it and moves in a positive direction, his confidence grows, the habit of choosing Krishna grows, and then he is able to overcome. [66:00 – 68:00]
- With this the Third Chapter, and the course's first unit, are concluded. [68:00]

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The chapter closes by inverting its own diagram. Knowing the soul to be above the intelligence, one uses the intelligence to steady the mind and turns the entire downward pull upward. The path of knowledge achieves this by rejection alone and is therefore difficult; devotion achieves it by reorientation — offering the sense objects, engaging the senses in perceiving the Lord, the mind in remembering Him, and the intelligence in how to serve Him. Since the senses will function either way, the real change is in values and desires, and it is won gradually, by cultivating the habit of choosing Krishna. [54:00 – 68:00]
</Callout>

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications

**1. Is the _jñānī_ situated at the level of the intelligence, and the devotee at the level of the soul? [68:00 – 72:00]**

- The teacher corrects the framing: the person on the path of knowledge **does** realize the soul — that is basic for him. The difference is one of **practice**, not of realization.
- The _sādhana_ of the path of knowledge is _vimarśanam_ — contemplation. He reads śāstra, thinks over its truths, and keeps the mind engaged in discriminating what is real and what is not.
- Its limitation, as he states it, is that there is **no practical positive engagement**: what is negative is given up, but there is nothing positive to connect with, which makes it difficult.
- Its second limitation is qualification: the prerequisite for the path of knowledge is a **purified _antaḥkaraṇa_**, and most people are not qualified for it.
- On the _aṣṭāṅga-yogī_: his method is different again — _yama, niyama, āsana, prāṇāyāma, pratyāhāra_ and the meditative limbs — and his goal is to realize the Supersoul, which is treated in the Sixth Chapter. He restrains the senses completely and meditates on the Supersoul, who also has a form, as described in the Third Canto's twenty-eighth chapter. [Caption ordering and reference flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- **These are different paths, not sequential stages.** In the yoga ladder they occupy different levels — above _niṣkāma karma-yoga_ is _jñāna-yoga_, above that _aṣṭāṅga-yoga_, above that _bhakti-yoga_ — but one does not pass through them in order. _Bhakti-yoga_ can be begun from the start, and even a small child can begin.

**2. Are the senses superior to the mind? [72:00 – 74:00]**

- A listener reports being confused by the two statements. The teacher restates the order cleanly: above the senses is the mind, above the mind the intelligence, above the intelligence the soul.
- The senses are superior to the **sense objects** — to dull matter — not to the mind. That is the statement that was being conflated.

**3. The soul is already pure, so what is being purified? And are the three levels connected? [74:00 – 78:00]**

- The teacher confirms the premise: what is purified is the lust lodged in the mind, the intelligence and the senses. This is what is called purification of the _antaḥkaraṇa_, which covers mind, intelligence and false ego.
- On the connection: there is **no exact one-way hierarchy** in which purifying the intelligence automatically purifies the mind and then the senses — but all of them are connected, and work at one level affects the others.
- The order he actually recommends is:
  1. **Create a boundary for the senses** — but this is only the beginning. He is pointed about its limits: refraining is not itself an achievement. Someone who has never drunk has not won a gold medal for it; that is only avoidance of the negative.
  2. **Engage the senses in positive things**, which is a step beyond avoidance.
  3. **Strengthen the intelligence** by reading śāstra, so that right contemplation becomes possible and the mind cannot pull in a negative direction. When the intelligence is strong and the mind does contemplate wrongly, it is noticed immediately and stopped.
- **How the mind then changes.** Taking a figure for illustration, suppose the mind holds a hundred impressions — in reality countless. With the intelligence controlling the system as the charioteer and keeping the right direction, spiritual impressions begin to fill the mind: from −100, then +1, +2, +3, +4, +5 — and gradually, with time, the negative impressions get erased. That is how the whole system is purified.

**4. Why is lust called the _eternal_ enemy in BG 3.39 if it ends as one progresses? [78:00 – 80:00]**

- "Eternal" is meant in the sense that **it does not end with time.** It ends only when a person evolves spiritually and love for Krishna is awakened.
- His contrasts: an ordinary enemy goes away with time. The body grows old and the power to enjoy decreases — but the desire to enjoy does not decrease. And an enemy you have in this world will not be your enemy in the next birth, whereas lust will still be there in the next birth.
- So long as the heart is not fully purified and love for Krishna is not awakened, it will not leave us. It remains until it is conquered.

**5. If _saṁskāras_ create the attraction, will they not obstruct the correction itself? [78:00 – 82:00]**

- The teacher grants the first half: past impressions do create problems and do push a person in the wrong direction. Whoever has drunk will be attracted on seeing alcohol; whoever has a habit of films or video games is pulled by that habit.
- **But the _jīva_ is not helpless.** The impressions pull; they do not completely control. That is the decisive point.
- Hence the sequence he has been giving: first place boundaries, then strengthen the intelligence on the basis of śāstra, then gradually inject positive _saṁskāras_ into the mind. It is a war, fought over time.
- **The force of the pull decreases measurably.** The more wrong action is taken, the stronger the impressions become, until saying no to them becomes impossible. The more one becomes spiritually strong, the weaker they become — the pull remains, but its force reduces. His illustrative figures: a pull of 100 newtons becomes 90, then 60, 50, 40, 30. The desires still arrive in the mind, but they no longer drag with the same intensity.
- **The comparison he draws:** for a person entirely outside devotional practice, a desire arrives like a strong wave in the ocean, and he feels he can do nothing — a helpless victim who must fulfil whatever desire comes. For a sincere devotee doing his reading, hearing and chanting well, the wave still comes, but it is handled comfortably in proportion to his practice — at twenty or thirty newtons. And it goes away as love for Krishna develops. It is a long journey.

**6. Atonement for a lapse [82:00 – 84:00]**

- Asked about rectifying a wrong act, the teacher notes he does not know the particulars of the case and answers on principle only.
- The principle: where there is a lapse against a rule, śāstra prescribes _prāścitta_ — atonement.
- The foremost atonement given in scripture, as he states it, is to **increase one's chanting of the holy name**, and additionally to perform _vaiṣṇava-sevā_ at a nearby temple.
- He adds the limit of the advice: one can give the solution, but no one can be compelled — everyone thinks differently.

**7. Course administration [84:00]**

- On the open-book question paper, students are directed to contact the organizing devotee, with a meeting announced at 7:30 the same evening on Zoom for all exam-related questions.

**8. Must one pass through _jñāna_ and _dhyāna_ before _bhakti_? [84:00 – 90:00]**

- **Not sequential.** One who follows the path of knowledge realizes the soul, and **that is perfection for him**; one who follows the path of meditation realizes the Supersoul, and that is perfection for him.
- The yoga ladder as he lays it out: _karma-kāṇḍa_ at the bottom; above it _niṣkāma karma-yoga_, in which one offers one's work; above that _jñāna-yoga_, whose prerequisite is a completely purified heart; above that _dhyāna-yoga_, for realizing the Supersoul; and _bhakti-yoga_ topmost — because it is the easiest, the most natural, and grants the topmost thing.
- **The asymmetry that matters:** one cannot take up _jñāna-yoga_ directly unless the heart is pure, and _dhyāna-yoga_ likewise has prerequisites. _Bhakti-yoga_ has no prerequisite at all and can be taken up directly, at any time, because it is itself powerful enough to purify the heart — purity is not expected in advance.
- He uses this to re-read the whole section: most of the principles in this part of the chapter about what one cannot give up and cannot do are addressed **mainly to _jñāna-yoga_**, which requires a prior qualification.
- **_Sādhana_ and _sādhya_ differ for each path**, and knowledge or meditation being _involved_ in devotion does not make devotion those paths:
  - _Jñāna-mārga_ — _sādhana_ is _vimarśana_ (contemplation); _sādhya_ is the realization that I am not the body but the soul.
  - _Dhyāna-yoga_ — _sādhana_ is _aṣṭāṅga-yoga_; _sādhya_ is realizing the Supersoul.
  - _Bhakti-yoga_ — _sādhana_ is _śravaṇam, kīrtanam_ and the rest of the ninefold practice: hearing about the Lord, chanting His name, performing _kīrtana_, remembering, and serving; _sādhya_ is realizing the Lord and love for Him.
- In devotion knowledge is certainly involved — scripture is being read right now — and meditation is involved in chanting, but the **primary method** is the devotional one.

**9. What about a devotee who cannot study well or does not know Sanskrit? [88:00 – 90:00]**

- The teacher's answer is that scriptural scholarship is **not a prerequisite**. Hearing and chanting do not require Sanskrit, and have no connection to it.
- One should try to understand śāstra according to one's own capacity. **The essence comes through to everyone** — that sense gratification is not to be pursued and that one is to engage in the Lord's service. Anyone who reads śāstra will understand that much, and can then practise devotion well.
- Technicalities may cause confusion, and some words may not be understood; that is not a significant issue. Lectures are available in Hindi; someone hearing properly is undergoing the process properly, and if he is trying to apply what he hears, it is perfect. Chanting the holy name is perfect.
- Not fully following an _ācārya_'s commentary is not a problem: whoever has the capacity should study it. **In devotion, the endeavour made lovingly for Krishna's pleasure is itself perfection.**
