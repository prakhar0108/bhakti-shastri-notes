- **Coverage:** BG 3.31–3.35 closes the third section of Karma-yoga. ## Continuity & Recap from Preceding Classes (BG 3.7 – 3.30) [02:00 – 04:00]

- Krishna's persistent aim through this chapter is that Arjuna remain active and not become inactive by taking shelter of the path of knowledge.
- The teacher traces the chapter's movement as a loop rather than a straight line: unattached duty, then regulated fruitive work, then the path of knowledge.
- BG 3.7 onward addresses the possibility that Arjuna considers himself qualified for _jñāna-yoga_. Krishna states the qualification, then still asks him to work, partly to set an example.
- BG 3.30 completed that argument by adding the element of offering: work performed without personal motive and dedicated to the Lord (_bhagavad-arpita niṣkāma karma-yoga_).
- With the section concluded, Krishna now states what follows for the person who has faith in all of this and what follows for the person who does not.

## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 3.31 [04:00 – 22:00]

<!-- verse:3.31 -->

**Context.** Krishna states the positive case first: the person who regularly follows His instruction with faith and without envy becomes free from the bondage of work. This directly answers what has been occupying Arjuna's mind — how to become free from karmic bondage. [04:00 – 06:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Me matam idam_: My opinion, My instruction — all that Krishna has stated so far. [04:00 – 06:00]
- _Nityam_: Eternal; the teacher connects the permanence of the instruction to the eternality of the speaker. [10:00 – 12:00]
- _Anutiṣṭhanti_: Not merely hearing, but carrying out; the teacher stresses that the words are meant to be lived and practised. [10:00 – 12:00]
- _Śraddhāvantaḥ_: With faith; explained as trusting that the words are potent. [12:00 – 14:00]
- _Anasūyantaḥ_: Without envy; explained as not becoming rebellious or resentful toward the instruction. [06:00 – 08:00]
- _Mucyante te karmabhiḥ_: They become free from the bondage of work. [18:00 – 20:00]

#### Faith operates even before full compliance [04:00 – 08:00]

- The teacher cites Baladeva Vidyabhushana: a person who has faith but cannot yet fully follow the instruction still becomes free, because faith carries the expectation of future practice.
- He recalls Giriraj Swami's account in his book on the history of the Juhu temple: Srila Prabhupada asked him to write it, and although the work was demanding, the principle he draws is that one may delay or postpone an instruction when something urgent intervenes, but should never simply disregard it.
- A devotee who hears class daily may feel nothing is changing. That feeling can turn negative and become the conclusion that none of this is meant to be applied.
- The two adjectives in the verse address exactly this: one may not be able to follow at the present level, but faith allows gradual capacity to develop. Rebellion, by contrast, blocks growth.

#### "Consciousness of being practical" as an obstacle [08:00 – 10:00]

- The teacher identifies the usual reason a person rebels against an instruction: it does not appear practical to him at his present stage.
- Examples he raises as things commonly dismissed as impractical: the recommendation that householders give a substantial portion in charity, rising early, fixed chanting, and the proper observance of Ekadashi.
- He labels this reflex "consciousness of being practical becomes a block" and treats it as a symptom of lust: I will do only what suits me.
- He contrasts _ātmendriya-prīti-vāñchā_ (desire for one's own sense pleasure) with _kṛṣṇendriya-prīti-vāñchā_ (desire for Krishna's pleasure) as the difference between lust and love. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Repairing a relationship changes the calculation [10:00 – 12:00]

- If a close relationship has broken because of your own fault, and a second chance appears, you do not evaluate the repair by whether it is convenient.
- The teacher's phrasing: in love, the question of practicality does not arise.
- Applied to bhakti: the relationship with Krishna is disturbed from our side, never from His. The desire to improve that relationship is what makes the instruction worth following.
- Because Krishna is the instructor, the connection between instruction and instructor matters. The Gita is special because its speaker is special.

#### Faith is the working currency of spiritual life [12:00 – 18:00]

- In material affairs some results are directly visible; spiritual reality is usually not directly perceived, so faith is needed all the more.
- The effectiveness of any devotional activity tracks the faith brought to it — chanting, circumambulating Tulasi Maharani, reading Bhagavatam and Srila Prabhupada's books.
- The teacher is explicit that this is not blind faith: it is reasonable, sensible, verifiable faith developed by reading and understanding the scriptures.
- He recommends taking some step, some risk, for Krishna. Risk normally denotes danger and uncertainty; when the object is Krishna, it becomes the safest thing, and the greatest risk of life is not taking any risk for Krishna.
- Material life is itself continuous risk — investments, relationships, health, sudden loss — yet people accept that risk repeatedly while calling devotional commitment impractical.
- He warns against the opposite pattern: performing the bare minimum of devotional activity with very little surrender while waiting helplessly for a mystical gift, such as expecting to wake up one morning with tears or a vision.
- Faith is ultimately a gift, but applying the instruction is a choice available to everyone, and faith grows through application in the same way trust in a medicine grows through taking it.
- Practically, he asks for regular favourable activities — hearing, chanting, remembering, and whatever service one can render — and says one should never conclude that one is already doing too much.

#### The purport's contrast: commentator versus ordinary person of faith [18:00 – 22:00]

Summarizing Srila Prabhupada's purport, the teacher draws out a comparison:

- Krishna's injunctions are the essence of Vedic wisdom, and as the Vedas are eternal, so is this truth; firm faith without envy of the Lord is required.
- Writing a commentary is not a small achievement, and there are a great many commentaries on the Gita — the teacher mentions a figure of more than five thousand. [Figure flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- A commentator without faith may sell books and earn money, but is not thereby freed from the bondage of action.
- An ordinary person with firm faith in the Lord's eternal injunction becomes free from that bondage even when unable to execute the order fully.
- The reason given is that such a person is not resentful of the principle and works sincerely without being stopped by defeat or a sense of helplessness. The teacher marks this line as the practical heart of the purport: one should not be discouraged at the beginning.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Faith plus absence of resentment is what carries a practitioner forward, not flawless compliance. Judge an instruction by its source rather than by whether it currently feels practical, apply it sincerely at your present level, and let capacity grow from there. [04:00 – 22:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 3.32 [22:00 – 26:00]

<!-- verse:3.32 -->

**Context.** Having stated the positive result, Krishna now states the negative one. Those who reject the teaching out of envy are described as bewildered in all knowledge, befooled, and ruined in their attempts at perfection. [22:00 – 24:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Abhyasūyantaḥ_: Out of envy; the disposition that makes a person refuse the instruction. [22:00 – 24:00]
- _Na anutiṣṭhanti me matam_: They do not carry out My injunction. [22:00 – 24:00]
- _Sarva-jñāna-vimūḍhān_: Bewildered despite all their knowledge. [24:00 – 26:00]
- _Naṣṭān acetasaḥ_: Ruined, with consciousness misdirected — the teacher glosses _acetasā_ as misdirected consciousness that stays turned away from Krishna. [24:00 – 26:00]

#### Choice is given, consequence is not [24:00 – 26:00]

- The teacher stresses that Krishna is not compelling anyone. The Lord states both options and their outcomes.
- Choosing to accept or reject the teaching is in our hands; the consequence attached to that choice is not.
- Envy and resentment are described as a live problem even after coming to devotional life, not only a condition of people outside it.
- Such a person may know a great deal and still remain bewildered, because consciousness stays fixed on material attraction and fruitive activity, which keeps him in the bondage of work.

#### Why faith and practice must go together [24:00 – 26:00]

The teacher summarizes the pair of verses as a single principle about the purpose of knowledge:

- First, have faith in what the scripture states.
- Second, actually follow it.
- Without faith, a person does not proceed to the next stage; with faith and practice, the Lord reciprocates, devotional taste develops, and the practitioner becomes willing to take further risk and offer further surrender.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Envious rejection of the instruction does not damage the instruction; it ruins the endeavour of the person who rejects it. Scriptural study is fruitful only when faith and practice accompany it. [22:00 – 26:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 3.33 [26:00 – 52:00]

<!-- verse:3.33 -->

**Context.** The teacher calls this a genuinely confusing verse and spends most of the class establishing its connection to what precedes. Krishna has just said that following or not following His instruction produces different results; He now says that even a knowledgeable person acts according to his acquired nature, and asks what repression can accomplish. [26:00 – 30:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Sadṛśam ceṣṭate svasyāḥ prakṛteḥ_: One endeavours in accordance with one's own nature. [32:00 – 34:00]
- _Jñānavān api_: Even a person possessing knowledge. [32:00 – 34:00]
- _Prakṛtiṁ yānti bhūtāni_: All living entities act according to the nature they have acquired. [32:00 – 34:00]
- _Nigrahaḥ kim kariṣyati_: What will repression accomplish? — _nigraha_ is explained as repression, distinguished throughout from regulation. [32:00 – 34:00; 38:00 – 40:00]

#### Why this verse appears here [28:00 – 32:00]

The teacher presents two linking questions drawn from Baladeva Vidyabhushana's commentary and from the flow of the argument:

- If the Supreme Lord's instruction is being disregarded, would such people not at least be afraid?
- More importantly: if an impure person somehow takes to the path of knowledge — if he is pushed into renunciation the way one might throw a person into a river expecting him to swim — will he not then be forced to follow?

Krishna's answer is that it does not work that way. Conduct follows acquired nature, so externally imposing an ashram or a set of rules does not create the qualification.

#### Nature persists despite knowledge [32:00 – 34:00]

- A thief knows he may be caught and imprisoned; after release he often returns to theft, because the sinful tendency remains.
- Knowing the rules of conduct does not by itself remove the desire for sense pleasure that produces the behaviour.
- The teacher therefore warns against artificially inspiring renunciation in an attached person: let such a person remain engaged, and let purification proceed gradually.
- He restates Krishna's instruction to Arjuna: do not do this yourself, and do not set that example for others.
- He is careful to scope the verse: this is stated primarily with respect to the path of knowledge. Its application to bhakti exists only at the level of principle. [32:00 – 34:00]

#### Repression is futile; regulation is essential [38:00 – 40:00]

- The teacher separates two things the verse could be taken to describe:
  - _Prakṛti_ — material nature in the sense of one's psychophysical nature, the brahminical, administrative, mercantile, or working disposition.
  - _Vāsanā_ — material desire formed by past impressions, including lust, anger, greed, illusion, pride, and envy.
- Psychophysical nature cannot simply be removed, but it can be kept within the boundaries of dharma and dovetailed in devotional service.
- Material desire that falls outside those boundaries must be regulated, and the verse must not be misread as licence: "my nature is like this, so what can be done" is explicitly rejected.
- Repression is described as futile because the path of knowledge withdraws everything at once without providing either a higher taste or practical engagement.

#### Dovetailing nature in Krishna's service [40:00 – 42:00]

- A person of administrative temperament can manage for the Lord; the teacher recounts Indradyumna Swami preaching in dangerous locations on Srila Prabhupada's instruction to preach fearlessly, an attack on the devotees, and the eventual initiation of the leader of the attackers, whom he names as Purusha Simha Das. [Names and details flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- A person of mercantile temperament can gather funds for the Lord.
- A person of brahminical temperament can teach.

#### The purport: education alone does not free anyone [42:00 – 46:00]

Summarizing Srila Prabhupada's purport, the teacher emphasizes:

- Without being situated on the transcendental platform of Krishna consciousness, a person cannot become free from the influence of the modes.
- Even a highly educated person on the mundane plane cannot get out of the entanglement of _māyā_ by theoretical knowledge alone.
- Many who present themselves as spiritualists remain under the control of particular modes.
- Long association with material nature keeps even a learned person in bondage; purity of heart has no correlation with academic degrees.
- Therefore one should not suddenly abandon occupational duty and become an artificial yogi or transcendentalist; it is better to remain in one's position and pursue Krishna consciousness under proper training.
- The teacher adds a distinction: an artificially renounced person is making plans for sense gratification, whereas a sincere practitioner is struggling against the same disturbances while knowing them to be wrong. Lust, anger, greed, and illusion trouble practitioners too; the difference lies in the direction of effort.

#### Two anecdotes on hearing without changing [34:00 – 38:00]

- At a de-addiction conference, a demonstration dropped insects into a jar of liquor and they died at once. Asked what he had understood, one listener concluded that the drink must kill the worms in his stomach.
- In a second story, one drinking companion phoned another in alarm after reading a detailed newspaper report on the harms of alcohol. The friend resolved to give something up — reading newspapers.
- The teacher connects both to Prahlada Maharaja's statement that attraction to Krishna does not arise by one's own effort, by another's instruction, or by their combination, for those who have effectively vowed to enjoy material life. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### An idle body with an impure heart is dangerous [46:00 – 48:00]

- Someone pushed suddenly into the path of knowledge has no permitted sense enjoyment and no practical engagement.
- Once a person becomes physically inactive, the mind becomes hyperactive; the teacher invokes the saying that an empty mind is the devil's workshop.
- With desires still present in the heart, that combination is described as a serious danger.

#### Why bhakti-yoga is treated differently [48:00 – 50:00]

The teacher gives two reasons why the restriction discussed here does not apply in the same way to devotional service:

- The path of knowledge depends on prior purity of heart, whereas devotional service purifies the person who takes it up, so an impure person entering bhakti is not thereby disqualified.
- Devotional life supplies practical engagement — management, accounts, physical service, and other responsibilities — rather than leaving a person unoccupied.

He nonetheless keeps the general rule for a change of ashram: in the absence of an exceptional teacher or an exceptionally situated candidate, let a person be purified in stages rather than abruptly, whether the movement is from student life or from household life. [46:00 – 48:00]

#### Association as the practical remedy [48:00 – 52:00]

- Baladeva Vidyabhushana's conclusion, as the teacher presents it, is that a person should take as much association of saintly devotees as possible.
- The reasoning follows the verse itself: reading and hearing about scripture alone do not remove one's acquired nature and the desires lodged in the heart.
- In good association, even a strong material desire weakens.
- The teacher cites a verse from the Eleventh Canto to the effect that an intelligent person should give up bad association and take shelter of saintly persons. [Reference flagged; see Verification Flags.]

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Forcible suppression cannot override an acquired nature, so do not manufacture renunciation in yourself or others. Regulate material desire, dovetail your actual temperament in service, and rely on association rather than on artificial withdrawal. [26:00 – 52:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 3.34 [52:00 – 72:00]

<!-- verse:3.34 -->

**Context.** The teacher frames two questions arising from the previous verse. If repression accomplishes nothing, are the scriptural instructions useless? And if Arjuna is to remain in the world, contact between the senses and their objects is unavoidable — will that not bind him? Krishna answers by relocating the problem from contact to attraction and aversion. [50:00 – 52:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Indriyasya indriyasya arthe_: The senses in relation to their objects. [52:00 – 54:00]
- _Rāga-dveṣau vyavasthitau_: Attachment and aversion are to be brought under regulation. [52:00 – 54:00]
- _Tayor na vaśam āgacchet_: One should not come under their control. [52:00 – 54:00]
- _Paripanthinau_: Stumbling blocks; the teacher glosses this as highway robbers who waylay a traveller, robbing the practitioner of the wealth gathered through _sādhana_. [52:00 – 54:00; 56:00 – 58:00]

#### The problem is not contact but attraction and aversion [52:00 – 56:00]

- Living in the world means seeing, hearing, and eating; sensory contact will occur.
- Krishna locates the danger in _rāga_ toward sense gratification and _dveṣa_ toward what is favourable.
- Drawing on Vishvanatha Chakravarti Thakura, the teacher describes the conditioned pattern: attachment forms toward whatever assists sense enjoyment, and aversion forms toward whatever obstructs it.
- Examples he gives of misplaced aversion: reluctance at the arrival of Ekadashi and its fast, checking the calendar with irritation, and avoiding hearing from saintly persons while still wanting their blessings, because one fears being told to give something up.
- Examples of misplaced attraction include association and conversation that scripture identifies as improper.
- He adds a general observation that correction is not palatable: instructions and corrections received in devotional life are often difficult to accept, while opportunities for sense gratification need no encouragement at all.

#### Why enjoyment reinforces attachment [54:00 – 58:00]

- Referring to the purport of BG 2.59 studied earlier, the teacher notes that although the sense objects may be given up, the taste for them remains.
- Every act of enjoyment leaves impressions and consequences, so indulgence strengthens _rāga_.
- Correspondingly, neglecting favourable devotional practice strengthens _dveṣa_, until even devotee association can begin to feel intolerable.
- Action and inaction should therefore not be driven by attraction or aversion, but regulated.
- He notes that excess also produces aversion — an item enjoyed past the point of satiation can become repellent — which is a further reason for regulation rather than extremes.
- He recalls Kunti Maharani's prayer asking that her bonds of affection be severed, as an example of a great soul seeking freedom from these bonds rather than comfort within them. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Strong emotions consume consciousness [60:00 – 62:00]

- Ordinary hunger does not absorb much of a person's awareness.
- But if a favourite preparation is announced — the teacher's running example is a rich gulab jamun served with rabri — the entire class can be spent thinking about it.
- Resentment works the same way: dismissing another devotee's instruction as worthless or impractical similarly consumes consciousness.
- The teacher's conclusion is that _rāga_ and _dveṣa_ matter because they occupy consciousness and thereby displace remembrance of Krishna.
- Practically, he asks the student to identify present attachments that are unfavourable to devotion — he names sport, YouTube, Instagram, and short-form video — to regulate them deliberately, and to expand reading, hearing, and chanting in their place.

#### The purport: regulation is the beginning, not the goal [62:00 – 70:00]

Summarizing Srila Prabhupada's purport, the teacher presents a graded argument:

- One fixed in Krishna consciousness naturally has no interest in material sense gratification; one who is not should follow the rules and regulations.
- Unrestricted sense enjoyment is the cause of material encagement, which is why scripture supplies regulations, illustrated in the purport by the licence of marriage.
- Even within such allowances, remaining propensities must be curbed, or they grow stronger.
- The principle behind all concessions is that one should accept only what is required to maintain life, not what is meant to satisfy the senses.
- As long as the material body exists its necessities are permitted, but within boundaries that are not to be crossed.
- Crucially, one should not rely on the control provided by such allowances: even a carefully regulated arrangement leaves a chance of falling, just as accidents can happen on the safest road.
- The teacher's applied example: deciding to take one sweet a week or watch one film a week is a beginning, but it is not something to depend upon and certainly not the goal.
- A person reduced from a packet of cigarettes to one per day has made progress, but treating one per day as the destination defeats the purpose of the programme.
- The purport's final movement is the positive one: attachment to Krishna consciousness and constant engagement in loving service detaches a person from sensory activity. The goal is attachment to Krishna; giving up sense gratification is the means, not the end. [70:00 – 72:00]

#### Comfort can become stagnation [70:00 – 72:00]

The teacher illustrates the risk of mistaking a regulated, well-ordered material life for the goal:

- A person whose work matches his natural temperament, whose family business is established, whose children's prospects are settled, and whose social circle is nearby.
- Everything is arranged, and precisely for that reason he becomes stagnant.
- The point drawn is that the material world is not meant for settlement, and that even a life in goodness needs a purpose beyond its own stability.

#### Impressions push but do not compel [72:00 – 74:00]

- Referring to Baladeva Vidyabhushana's commentary, the teacher explains that impressions accumulated over many lifetimes sit in the mind and are activated by particular conditions, producing a fitted system of likes and dislikes.
- These impressions push the living being but cannot force it, because free will continues to exist.
- This is precisely why scripture gives instructions at all, and why association and environment matter so much: free will exercised in good association is the way out.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Sensory contact is unavoidable; being controlled by attraction and aversion is not. Regulate both, treat regulation as a starting point rather than an achievement, and keep the actual goal — growing attachment to Krishna — in view. [52:00 – 72:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 3.35 [72:00 – 84:00]

<!-- verse:3.35 -->

**Context.** Krishna concludes the section: one's own prescribed duty performed imperfectly is better than another's duty performed well, and even death in the course of one's own duty is preferable, because another's path is dangerous. [72:00 – 74:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Sva-dharma_: One's own prescribed duty; here specifically the duty corresponding to one's psychophysical nature within _varṇāśrama_, not the general spiritual sense of being the Lord's servant. [74:00 – 76:00]
- _Viguṇaḥ_: With fault; performed imperfectly. [74:00 – 76:00]
- _Para-dharmāt sv-anuṣṭhitāt_: Than another's duty carried out very well. [74:00 – 76:00]
- _Sva-dharme nidhanam śreyaḥ_: Even death while performing one's own duty is better. [74:00 – 76:00]
- _Para-dharmo bhayāvahaḥ_: Another's duty is fearful or dangerous. [74:00 – 76:00]

#### The instruction to Arjuna [74:00 – 76:00]

- Arjuna is a kshatriya; fighting is his duty, and death in the discharge of it is preferable to adopting an idealized brahminical or renounced role in the forest.
- Krishna's directive is to perform the duties of one's own _varṇa_ and _āśrama_ in a spirit of non-attachment.
- The teacher notes that the person practising unattached duty offers the results but remains attached to the type of work itself, which is why the prescribed duty still matters at this stage.

#### A historical illustration [76:00 – 78:00]

The teacher recounts the succession after Maharaja Anga: his son Vena proved to be of wicked disposition and eventually claimed divinity, and the sages destroyed him; yet the sages themselves did not assume the throne, and Prithu Maharaja was brought forth from Vena's body. The point drawn is that in earlier times each order kept to its own occupation. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### In devotional service, varnashrama restriction does not apply [78:00 – 80:00]

- A devotee asked by his spiritual master to clean a toilet or clear plates does not refuse on grounds of family background.
- The teacher describes a single devotee's day passing through all four occupational registers: giving class in the morning, managing afterwards, collecting funds for that management, and joining the cleaning in the evening.
- Everything can be performed in the capacity of the Lord's servant. He warns that confusing the levels — applying an instruction meant for unattached fruitive duty to devotional service, or the reverse — distorts the teaching.

#### The purport: material duty, spiritual duty, and authorized direction [78:00 – 84:00]

Summarizing Srila Prabhupada's purport, the teacher brings out the following:

- One should discharge one's prescribed duty in full Krishna consciousness rather than take up duties prescribed for others; the teacher reads this as Srila Prabhupada again placing the verse within unattached, offered work.
- Two categories of duty are distinguished:
  - **Material duties**, determined by one's psychophysical condition.
  - **Spiritual duties**, ordered by the spiritual master for the transcendental service of Krishna.
- One should hold to the prescribed duty in both categories, even to the point of death; the standards of the two platforms differ, but the duty in each is to be followed.
- On the spiritual platform, the teacher identifies the sources of that duty as guru, sadhu, and shastra.
- The principle of following authorized direction is described as always good for the performer, and imitation of someone else's position is discouraged: a brahmana in goodness is non-violent, while a kshatriya in passion uses force for the establishment of dharma, so the kshatriya should not adopt the brahmana's non-violence.
- Performing one's own duty without personal motive gradually purifies the heart.
- Finally, one who transcends the modes and is fully situated in Krishna consciousness can perform anything and everything under the spiritual master's direction — teaching, management, fund collection, or service — because material distinctions do not apply on the transcendental stage.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Work with the nature you actually have, under authorized direction, rather than imitating a position you admire. In devotional service, the occupational distinction gives way to whatever the Lord's service requires. [72:00 – 84:00]
</Callout>

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications

### 1. Do BG 3.31–3.32 denote free will while BG 3.33 denotes destiny? [84:00 – 88:00]

- **Question:** How should free will and destiny be compared in the context of these verses?
- **Answer:** BG 3.31–3.32 ask a person to follow the instruction, which is an exercise of free will, and free will belongs to everyone.
- BG 3.33 can be connected with destiny: according to past action one has received a particular body and a particular psychophysical nature, and that nature cannot be entirely changed.
- The teacher distinguishes categories of karmic result — results still in seed form, results further developed, and _prārabdha_ results currently being experienced through the body as happiness and distress. [Terminology flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- BG 3.34 then addresses what remains in one's hands: contact with sense objects will occur, but attraction and aversion are to be regulated.
- He adds an important caveat: this whole section becomes impractical if read technically without devotion, which is why Srila Prabhupada's purports consistently place it within Krishna consciousness. Unattached work and purity of heart become achievable when a person is engaged in devotional service.

### 2. What is the difference between psychophysical nature and habit? [88:00 – 90:00]

- **Question:** Is a person's _svabhāva_ the same as his habits?
- **Answer:** Psychophysical nature is connected mainly to the modes and is categorized as brahminical, administrative, mercantile, or working disposition; it is an inclination, and its degree varies from person to person rather than being uniform within a category.
- Introvert and extrovert dispositions are given as a familiar example.
- When a person is engaged according to that nature, especially in work, the result is being internally comfortable and externally competent.
- Habits are different: they are set patterns formed by repeated practice — rising late, or sleeping immediately after eating.

### 3. Does a Srimad-Bhagavatam kept at home require prana-pratishtha before it can be read? [90:00 – 94:00]

- **Question:** Should the book be ritually installed, and would that restrict where it can be read?
- **Answer:** No such installation is required. Offer obeisances and read it.
- The teacher notes the opposite problem: where books are installed, they are sometimes never touched again. He recalls visiting a former student near Kanpur whose home shrine held every edition of the Gita that had been gifted to him, all still packed.
- The scriptures are non-different from the Lord and are therefore worshipable, and should be kept and handled respectfully.
- But reading them, contemplating them, and applying them is the principal worship, and is also how the debt to the sages is repaid.

### 4. Is the use of pesticides in farming acceptable? [94:00 – 98:00]

- **Question:** A householder engaged in farming uses pesticide to protect the crop; is this proper?
- **Answer:** The teacher invokes the comparison of fire and smoke, to be discussed in the Eighteenth Chapter: some fault accompanies nearly every activity in this world, and reaction does accrue.
- He does not recommend abandoning farming. Practicality must also be considered — the time an alternative method would take to establish, and the resulting yield.
- The working principle he gives is to find the least violent practicable way of doing the work.
- The second part of his answer is charity: a portion of what is produced should go to the Lord's service, otherwise the accumulated reaction becomes heavy.
- He generalizes the point: in most ways of earning, some principle is compromised somewhere. He recalls Arjuna in the First Chapter refusing a kingdom that would be stained with the blood of Bhishma, Drona, and his relatives, and compares sinful reaction to lentils mixed into rice — thoroughly combined with the activity by which we earn.
- Consuming the entire result for oneself is described as producing an unhealthy life, with difficulty in health, in work, and in devotional practice; offering a portion reduces that burden.

### 5. How should one accept scriptural statements that are beyond imagination? [98:00 – 104:00]

- **Question:** Some descriptions exceed what we can conceive — for instance the account that a river flowed at the place associated with Nanda Maharaja and Yashoda Mata, formed from tears. How can both faith and reason be maintained?
- **Answer:** The teacher describes three domains of scripture:
  - Statements that agree with modern science, including practical claims that science has subsequently confirmed.
  - Statements that do not match current science, notably cosmology, where the frame of reference differs and where the scientific theories themselves remain contested. He mentions a book on Vedic cosmology that explains how the distances given in scripture are consistent once the reference point is understood. [Author's name not identified in the captions.]
  - Statements that lie beyond the reach of science altogether.
- His central argument is that assuming science has a monopoly over the field of knowledge is mistaken: science is one branch of knowledge, dealing with gross matter, and the senses themselves are limited — many living entities are not visible to the eye.
- Similarly, treating whatever fits our mental framework as the limit of truth is unjustified.
- Two reasons are given for trusting scripture: statements recorded long ago that later investigation confirms, and the observable change in the lives of those who follow it.
- He relates Radhanath Swami's account of a discussion with a professor of religion who had studied extensively but had no faith in scripture. The response offered was a question about whom one should trust: selfless personalities who wanted nothing for themselves, or a contemporary debater absorbed in sense gratification.
- He also notes that faith is unavoidable even in science: a reader who accepts a stated count of stars in a distant galaxy is not in a position to verify it. [Account and details flagged; see Verification Flags.]

### 6. Is fulfilling one's daily commitment sufficient, or should one give best effort? [104:00 – 107:09]

- **Question:** Is meeting the daily commitment enough, or is "best effort" something more?
- **Answer:** Best effort does not mean abandoning everything and attempting twenty-four hours of devotional activity.
- Best effort is defined relative to one's actual situation — employed, married, unmarried, a brahmachari, or aspiring to become one — and determining it requires guidance.
- The teacher asks for progressive commitment: commit at the present stage, then grow incrementally and periodically.
- His distinction is between "full effort" in the abstract and the best that is possible within one's present responsibilities and available time, which also calls for planning and organization.
