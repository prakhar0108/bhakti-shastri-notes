## The Preface — Six Paragraphs [04:00 – 24:00]

#### Dedication and history [04:00 – 10:00]

- The teacher notes the volume is dedicated to Baladeva Vidyabhushana, from whose commentary Srila Prabhupada drew a great deal.
- **Why the first edition was cut.** When Srila Prabhupada went West he carried the First Canto of the Bhagavatam; the Gita commentary was written afterwards, running to around a thousand pages. No publisher showed interest, on the reasoning that the Gita already had innumerable commentaries and the author was unknown.
- He relates the incident by which it was published: two of Srila Prabhupada's men went to a large publishing house about an unrelated matter — a recording the accounts department wanted — and while they were there the chief editor happened to come out, saw them, and asked whether they had anything on the Bhagavad-gita, as the house wished to publish a commentary on it. The teacher treats the coincidence as the Lord's arrangement.
- When it was published, however, the manuscript was cut to under four hundred pages. Four years later, as appreciation and Srila Prabhupada's reputation spread, the complete edition was published. [Details and figures flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- He notes in passing that the Gita is among the most commented-upon books on the planet, with more than five thousand commentaries. [Figure flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Paragraph 1 — restoring the original manuscript [10:00]

Srila Prabhupada explains that the present effort restores the original manuscript with the complete _paramparā_ explanation, so that the Krishna consciousness movement may be more progressively and firmly established.

#### Paragraph 2 — four characteristics of the movement [10:00 – 12:00; 20:00 – 22:00]

The teacher isolates the sentence he treats as the Preface's most important, and asks the class to memorize its four terms. Because the movement is based on the Gita as it is, it is:

| Term                        | What the teacher draws from it                                                                            | Where it is explained |
| :-------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------- |
| **Genuine**                 | Presented without adulteration — the speaker's own words rather than the commentator's.                   | Paragraph 3           |
| **Historically authorized** | Coming directly from Krishna through disciplic succession; the _paramparā_ list is printed in the volume. | Paragraph 4           |
| **Transcendental**          | One who follows it transcends the cycle of birth, old age, disease, and death.                            | Paragraph 5           |
| **Natural**                 | The process of devotion is as natural as using the senses.                                                | Paragraph 6           |

#### The humility behind the title [10:00 – 14:00]

- The teacher raises the objection himself: a title meaning _as it is_ implies that other editions are not, which can sound like the work of a proud author.
- **His answer** is the end of the same paragraph, where Srila Prabhupada writes that if there is any credit in this connection it does not belong to him personally but to his eternal spiritual master.
- Paragraph 3 states the one credit claimed: that the Gita has been presented without adulteration.
- **What adulteration means.** Where something else has been mixed in — the commentator's own motives, ideas, and speculation. The teacher lists the resulting editions: the Gita for politicians, for patriots, for yoga teachers, each with its own supposed essence, and none presenting Krishna.
- His observation on what follows: the adulterated version is cheap and draws a crowd the way a vegetable market does, while the genuine article draws fewer people, like a jeweller's shop. But Srila Prabhupada's aim was to give the clear thing.
- He relates that when someone remarked that Srila Prabhupada's commentary resembled Ramanujacharya's and appeared copied, Srila Prabhupada treated that as the measure of success, since the task is to present the acharyas.
- **The cost of adulteration,** as Paragraph 3 states it: great harm followed, and the teacher's summary of the harm is that no devotees were produced. He refers to a lecture of Srila Prabhupada's in which he says openly that many came West to preach but not one was presenting the Lord's knowledge correctly, that he was not saying this out of pride but as his observation, and that the result was that nobody became Krishna's devotee. [Lecture title flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### On the apparent plainness of Srila Prabhupada's books [12:00 – 14:00]

The teacher notes that these books may not strike a reader as attractive — there is much repetition of sentences and of the maha-mantra — and treats that as inseparable from the absence of adulteration.

#### Why devotion is natural [22:00 – 24:00]

- The path of knowledge requires renouncing everything, which is not natural. The teacher's example from yoga is being told to stand on one's head.
- Devotion is as natural as using the senses. Anyone can chant, hear about the Lord, hear a kirtana, or take food offered to Him.
- **His supporting contrast:** there is no success story with maya — for unlimited time the living entity has been trying, and no one has obtained transcendental happiness here — yet maya convinces millions across millions of lifetimes that success is coming. And there is no failure story with Krishna; he points to BG 4.10 on how many have attained by taking His shelter. Even so, it remains difficult to believe.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The Preface's claim is deliberately narrow. Srila Prabhupada claims no originality — only that nothing was mixed in — and rests the movement's legitimacy on that one point. [04:00 – 24:00]
</Callout>

## Three Ways into the Gita's Structure [24:00 – 26:00]

The teacher says the Introduction is best approached after knowing three things:

1. The names of the eighteen chapters.
2. The seventeen questions Arjuna asks, on the basis of which the whole Gita is spoken. (Deferred to the next class.)
3. The threefold division: the first six chapters predominantly _karma-yoga_, the middle six predominantly _bhakti-yoga_, the last six predominantly _jñāna-yoga_ — with the emphasis on _predominantly_, since these are not discrete and the contents are mixed throughout.

His general principle: for any book one should first know the overview, the way one should know how many _kāṇḍas_ the Ramayana has or how many _parvas_ the Mahabharata has, even without having read the whole.

#### The teacher's asides on the sequence

- **On selective listening [28:00]:** when the mind is running in one direction it hears selectively, which is why Arjuna seized on "peace."
- **On why knowledge deepens [38:00]:** the Lord raises the level progressively because of the affectionate relationship between Him and Arjuna. His formulation — transcendental knowledge flows in an affectionate relationship — with relationship being very important for receiving knowledge.
- **On a good teacher [42:00]:** when one finds a good teacher, one takes the chance to get other things clarified too.

</Callout>

## Vishvanatha Chakravarti Thakura's Division [48:00 – 50:00]

The teacher gives three reasons from the commentary for placing _bhakti-yoga_ in the middle six chapters:

- **It is the most confidential secret**, so it is kept in the middle.
- **It gives life to both sides.** Placed in the middle it touches both, and _karma-yoga_ and _jñāna-yoga_ yield their result only when some devotion is involved in them.
- **The jewel box.** The Gita is compared to a box holding a jewel: the first six and last six chapters are the two covers, and devotion is the jewel between them, protected on both sides.

## The Introduction — Paragraphs 1 to 32 [50:00 – 70:00]

#### Why it is so long [50:00 – 52:00]

- The teacher notes it is almost a chapter in itself, running to sixty-five paragraphs before the Gita-mahatmya verses begin.
- **Srila Prabhupada's reason,** as he gives it: there was no confidence that people in this age would finish a book, so everything was put into the Introduction. Read it and nothing essential is missed.
- **His formulation of the mood:** Srila Prabhupada was writing books to save humanity, not to gain votes. The purports were written with urgency, not for applause — unlike the usual motive behind a lecture, a book, or a video, which is to attract approval.

#### On numbering the paragraphs [04:00 – 06:00; 70:00 – 74:00]

The teacher returns to this repeatedly and explains the mechanics:

- Count a new paragraph wherever the text is indented.
- Paragraph 1 begins after the opening prayers, at the statement that the Gita is also known as Gitopanishad.
- **Why it helps:** it lets you give each block a heading, so that the connectivity becomes visible. Across several paragraphs one theme is usually continuing; once you know the theme, the individual paragraphs connect.
- His worked example: paragraphs 2 through 9 all concern how to receive the Gita.
- He notes it also helps with ordinary purports, where the verse seems to go one way and the purport another.

#### The prayers [52:00 – 54:00]

- Srila Prabhupada gives the meanings of the opening prayers, which the teacher treats as valuable because we recite them without knowing the meaning. Knowing the meaning makes it far easier to fix the mind — what he calls _mantrārtha-cintanam_.
- He notes that this is the one place in the whole Gita where Radharani's name appears.

#### Paragraphs 2–9 — how to receive the Gita [54:00 – 62:00]

- **The medicine analogy.** One does not take medicine according to one's mood, but as the doctor prescribed. So the Gita must be accepted as the speaker gave it.
- **And the speaker is no ordinary physician.** The teacher extends the analogy: the most complicated operations on mind, body, and soul have been performed by Him, on patients as difficult as Hiranyakashipu and Ravana, and no operation has ever failed. The surgery is somewhat painful, but all of them succeeded.
- **The acharyas confirm the speaker's position.** Paragraph 2 cites Shankaracharya, Ramanujacharya, Madhvacharya, Nimbarka, and Chaitanya Mahaprabhu as accepting Krishna as the Supreme Personality of Godhead. [Names as captioned; see Verification Flags.]
- **Paragraph 3** quotes BG 4.1–4.3 on how the knowledge descends through succession.
- **Paragraph 4** gives the required attitude, from the Lord's own reason for speaking to Arjuna — that he is His devotee and friend. The paragraph ends by stating that one possessing Arjuna's qualities can properly understand the Gita.
- **Paragraph 5** describes the five kinds of relationship a devotee has with the Lord.
- **Paragraphs 6–8** show how Arjuna actually received it: accepting the Lord as Supreme, quoting BG 10.12–14; and then, in case this looks like a friend's flattery, citing Narada, Asita, Devala, and Vyasa as accepting the same.
- **Paragraph 9 concludes:** the Gita should be taken in a spirit of devotion; one should not think oneself equal to Krishna, nor think Him an ordinary personality, nor even merely a great one. The teacher highlights the practical concession in this paragraph — that one should _at least theoretically_ accept Krishna as the Supreme Personality of Godhead and approach in a submissive spirit. His comparison is an axiom in mathematics: a self-evident truth accepted in order to proceed.

#### Paragraphs 10–13 — what the Gita is for [62:00 – 64:00]

- The purpose of the Gita is to deliver humanity from ignorance. Arjuna is in difficulty, and everyone in this world is in the same perplexed condition — the teacher again points to the purport of BG 2.7.
- Arjuna was not actually in difficulty; the Lord used him to give us this knowledge, just as Parikshit Maharaja was used for the Bhagavatam. The teacher raises the same kind of question there — whether a great king could really not tolerate thirst — and answers that it was the Lord's plan.
- Paragraph 11 notes how few people actually have the desire to inquire.

#### Paragraphs 14–24 — the five subjects [64:00 – 66:00]

_Īśvara_, _jīva_, _prakṛti_, _kāla_, and _karma_. The teacher maps them onto the three topics of any Upanishad — _jīva_, _jagat_, _jagadīśa_ — and repeats that four are eternal while karma alone ends, since a liberated soul's karma is finished.

#### Paragraphs 25–27 — the impersonal aspect [66:00 – 68:00]

- The impersonal aspect is genuinely an aspect of the Lord, but it is not the complete manifestation. He refers to the description of the Absolute Truth being known in three features — Brahman, Paramatma, and Bhagavan. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- Paragraph 27 ends by stating that the complete knowledge of Vedic wisdom is found in the Gita.

#### Paragraphs 28–30 — infallible Vedic knowledge [68:00 – 70:00]

The four defects again — imperfect senses, the tendency to illusion, the tendency to mistakes, and the cheating propensity — and how Vedic knowledge, descending from the Lord to Brahma, stands beyond them.

**A reading tip the teacher gives here:** where a paragraph begins with "therefore," Srila Prabhupada is concluding a topic, which signals that the theme is about to change. Paragraph 30 concludes that one should not claim proprietorship over anything and should accept only what has been allotted for one's maintenance.

#### Paragraphs 31–32 [70:00]

These develop the principle of the first verse of _Śrī Īśopaniṣad_, that we are not proprietors.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The Introduction's first nine paragraphs are a single argument about how to read. Everything else depends on the concession asked for in paragraph 9 — accept the speaker's position at least theoretically, and approach submissively. [50:00 – 70:00]
</Callout>

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications

### 1. How many paragraphs are there, and how does marking them help? [70:00 – 74:00]

- **Question:** The count may differ depending on how one marks them, so what is the correct total — and what exactly does marking accomplish?
- **Answer:** Sixty-five paragraphs, after which the Gita-mahatmya verses begin, with a further paragraph or two at the end that he did not count. Paragraph 1 begins after the prayers.
- Count a new paragraph wherever the text is indented.
- **On the benefit:** without marking, one cannot see what subject matter is being discussed or how it connects. With marking, one can identify the theme running across a block — as with paragraphs 2 to 9 — and then the individual paragraphs connect and can be simplified. He notes this is essential for something as long as the Introduction, and useful even for ordinary purports.

### 2. The five relationships between devotee and Lord [74:00 – 76:00]

- **Question:** The description of one devotee being in a passive state and another active is confusing.
- **Answer:** These are the five _rasas_ — neutrality, servitorship, friendship, parental affection, and conjugal love — written in different language here.
- In the neutral relationship, something like grass is outwardly inactive but keeps itself soft, and serves the Lord in that state. In servitorship the devotee acts on the Lord's word. In friendship the mood intensifies, and the friends offer their food without being asked. In parental affection it is stronger still — Mother Yashoda feeds Him whether He wishes it or not. The topmost is conjugal.
- **His clarifying analogy:** bottles of two, five, and fifteen litres. All are full, and everyone in every _rasa_ is fully satisfied — but the capacity differs.

### 3. How should one understand the illustration on the Gita's cover? [76:00 – 80:00]

- **Question:** How should one view the picture of the Lord and Arjuna on the chariot, and the reins representing mind and intelligence?
- **Answer:** The teacher notes that different acharyas have commented differently — some describe the hand position as the gesture of knowledge, since the Lord is about to give Arjuna knowledge, and some read the fingers as representing the material and spiritual worlds.
- **His caution:** many such readings are allegorical, and it is difficult to call all of them authorized. He notes there is a great deal of allegorical commentary on the Gita.
- On why this image appears at all: the Gita's principal content is the conversation between Arjuna and Krishna, so the cover reflects it, as a book on the Lord's pastimes would carry His image.

### 4. How should one take notes? [80:00 – 84:00]

- **Question:** A participant explains that she takes very detailed notes, becomes tired, and still cannot frame them usefully for later recollection.
- **Answer:** The teacher gives three steps.
- **Notes should be neither a transcription nor a puzzle.** Not so complete that it becomes a transcript — in which case the mind is occupied only with writing — and not so short that afterwards one cannot tell what was being discussed. Writing matters because it keeps the mind focused.
- **He allows an exception:** for an informational class or a course such as this one, extensive notes are not wrong, since the material is to be taught later. But then a short version must also be made.
- **Divide the notes into headings.** Otherwise they remain one long narration, and revision becomes impossible — which defeats the purpose of having notes. Fifteen paragraphs should collapse under four or five headings, and the headings alone will then give the flow.
- **Make a flow.** Even after headings, reduce it to a very short flow: this topic has these five things, with any example or story held in a single line. That is what he calls the flow, and it is what one actually uses to give a class.
