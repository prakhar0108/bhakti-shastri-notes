## Topic-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### 1. Sambandha, Abhidheya, Prayojana — the frame for any body of knowledge [02:00 – 04:00]

**Context.** Before describing the Gita's contents, the teacher supplies the classification he will use throughout.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Sambandha_: The knowledge itself — what the relationships are. [02:00 – 04:00]
- _Abhidheya_: The process, the practice one undertakes on the basis of that knowledge. [02:00 – 04:00]
- _Prayojana_: The conclusion, the result one seeks to attain. [02:00 – 04:00]

The sequence is the natural order of learning anything spiritual: first acquire the knowledge, then attempt to practise what it prescribes, then expect a particular result. The teacher asks the class to identify all three for the Gita specifically.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Do not study the Gita as an undifferentiated mass of teaching. Ask of every section which of the three it belongs to — what relationship it establishes, what practice it asks for, or what result it promises. [02:00 – 04:00]
</Callout>

---

### 2. Sambandha — the five subject matters [04:00 – 06:00; 12:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- The five subjects are _jīva_, _kāla_, _karma_, _īśvara_, and _prakṛti_. [04:00 – 06:00]
- A simpler mnemonic form he offers is _jīva_, _jagat_, _jagadīśa_ — three words all beginning with the same syllable. [04:00]

#### Four are eternal, one is not [04:00 – 06:00; 12:00]

- The _jīva_ is eternal; the teacher forward-references the Second Chapter's description of the soul as unborn and everlasting.
- The Lord is eternal, which he treats as beyond question.
- _Kāla_, time, is present in both the material and the spiritual realms.
- _Prakṛti_, understood as the presiding potency of material nature, is a potency of the Lord and is also eternal.
- _Karma_ alone is temporary, in the sense that when a soul attains liberation its accumulated results are exhausted.

The teacher adds that the relationships _between_ these — between the _jīva_ and nature, between the _jīva_ and the Lord — are what the Gita actually discusses, and that this same set of subjects is what any Upanishad takes up.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
If asked in one sentence what knowledge the Gita contains, name the five subjects and note that only karma among them is temporary. [04:00 – 06:00]
</Callout>

### 3. Sambandha — the ladder of yogas [12:00 – 18:00]

**Context.** The second component of the Gita's knowledge is a graded sequence of approaches, rising as consciousness develops.

#### The rungs, from the bottom up [12:00 – 18:00]

- **Below the ladder — animalistic consciousness:** Those who simply want to enjoy without restriction and without following any authority.
- **Karma-kanda:** The person still wants to enjoy, but understands through the Vedas that the facilities for enjoyment are not under his own control, so he propitiates higher authorities through sacrifice. The teacher's comparison is an employee who must keep his manager satisfied in order to be comfortable himself.
- **Karma-yoga, with and then without personal motive:** The person begins offering something to the Lord, and gradually offers everything.
- **Jnana-yoga:** No desire for material enjoyment remains; the person has concluded that every attempt at enjoyment brings trouble. The teacher associates it with the method of negation and with impersonal realization, reaching mainly as far as peace.
- **Dhyana-yoga:** The senses are restrained through the eightfold discipline described in the Sixth Chapter, and attention is focused on a higher reality; the teacher names the realization here as Paramatma.
- **Bhakti-yoga:** Activity performed for the pleasure of the Supreme Lord — the highest rung in the hierarchy.

#### The problem with the karma-kanda level [14:00]

- Everything is worshipped except the Supreme Personality of Godhead; the teacher describes the pursuit as looking for a substitute for God.
- The goals sought are wealth, progeny, and learning.
- He cites the Gita's own two qualifications of demigod worship, in the Seventh and Ninth Chapters — that it is undertaken by the less intelligent, and that it is not the proper procedure — while stressing that this level is nevertheless far above unregulated enjoyment. [Terms flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Humans below the animal level [16:00 – 18:00]

The teacher explains his placement of modern human life below animalistic consciousness:

- Animals are regulated in eating, sleeping, mating, and defending. They eat when hungry and only as much as hunger requires; they rest when tired; they mate seasonally; they defend themselves for protection.
- Nothing they do is unnatural.
- In human life all four become artificial and superficial, so people can fall below the animal standard. Human life is an option that can go either up or down.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The Gita does not present one method and dismiss the rest; it arranges them as a ladder and establishes bhakti-yoga at the top after describing everything below it. [12:00 – 18:00]
</Callout>

### 4. Abhidheya — what the Gita asks you to practise [18:00 – 20:00]

**Context.** The practice the Gita teaches is how to deal with matter, the senses, the mind, and the self.

#### Four domains of dealing [18:00 – 20:00]

- **Matter:** Where does it come from, and is it meant for my enjoyment? The teacher points to BG 7.4–7.5 for the enumeration of the material energies proceeding from the Lord.
- **The senses:** How to engage them in the Lord's service; discussed in the Third Chapter, with further treatment in the Fifteenth and Sixteenth. He cites the Fifteenth Chapter's statement that the living entities in this world are the Lord's fragmental parts struggling with the mind and senses.
- **The mind:** Treated mainly in the Sixth Chapter; the teacher points to verses in the region of 6.5, 6.6, and 6.30, and to Arjuna's complaint that the mind is restless. [Verse references as spoken; see Verification Flags.]
- **The self:** Treated in the Second Chapter, which establishes that we are the soul.

He adds that the Gita's middle six chapters are described as its most confidential knowledge, because they state the soul's actual activity — pure devotional service.

#### Pleasure has levels [06:00 – 08:00]

Different living beings seek happiness at different levels, which the teacher lists as physical, mental, intellectual, egoistic, and — topmost — spiritual. The Gita teaches how to deal with each domain accordingly.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The Gita's practical instruction is not abstract: it addresses matter, the senses, the mind, and the self, each in identifiable chapters. Locate them and you can navigate the book. [18:00 – 20:00]
</Callout>

### 5. Prayojana — the result, and the central message [08:00 – 10:00; 20:00 – 22:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Dāso 'smi_: The realization that I am the Lord's servant, and that His service is the purpose of my life. [20:00 – 22:00]
- **Conditional duty:** Duty according to one's social position and circumstances, owed to various people. [20:00 – 22:00]
- **Constitutional duty:** Devotional service, which belongs to every living entity as such. [20:00 – 22:00]

#### How the result is recognized [08:00 – 10:00]

- Srila Prabhupada writes in the Introduction that the Gita should be heard as Arjuna heard it, and the result should be the result Arjuna obtained.
- The teacher identifies that result from Arjuna's concluding words: illusion destroyed, memory regained, and readiness to act on the Lord's word — in essence, that the Lord's mission has become his own.
- Stated positively, the result is the living entity's experience of loving devotional service.

#### The central message [20:00 – 22:00]

The teacher states it plainly: doing our duty without attachment, for the pleasure of the Lord.

- He observes that in India the popular answer to "what does the Gita teach" is usually half of one well-known verse about action and its fruits, which he does not treat as a problem.
- But the full statement of the central message includes the recipient — the Lord's pleasure — and the distinction between conditional and constitutional duty.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The Gita's purpose is not information but a change of position: the realization that one belongs to Krishna and that His service is one's actual duty. [08:00 – 10:00; 20:00 – 22:00]
</Callout>

### 6. The Gita-mahatmya — what study of the Gita yields [22:00 – 34:00]

**Context.** To fill out _prayojana_, the teacher turns to the glorification verses quoted in the Introduction. Each is treated briefly for the result it names. Because the captioned recitations are indistinct, the verses below are described by their content rather than reproduced.

#### Shankaracharya's statement [22:00 – 24:00]

One who follows the Gita's instructions can become free from the miseries and anxieties of life.

#### On serious, faithful study [24:00 – 26:00]

- The first verse quoted states that for one who reads the Gita with faith and seriousness, the reactions of past misdeeds cease to affect him.
- The teacher explains why this matters: accumulated sinful reaction makes any good undertaking difficult. He compares devotional practice to an uphill climb — difficult enough in itself, and much harder carrying a thirty-kilo weight.
- He points to BG 7.28 for the same principle: when sinful reactions are exhausted and pious activity has been performed, one can engage firmly in devotion.
- His study instruction here is practical: write down every verse number he mentions so that they can be studied later.

#### On surrender [26:00 – 28:00]

- The second verse quoted is the Gita's well-known concluding instruction on abandoning all varieties of duty and taking shelter of the Lord alone.
- The teacher spends some time on the inadequacy of the English word _surrender_ as a rendering of _śaraṇāgati_. Surrender suggests a last resort — the image he uses is combatants raising their hands only when the ammunition runs out, and waiting for the first chance to attack again.
- _Śaraṇāgati_ is entirely positive. His illustration: a small child frightened by a thunderclap runs to his mother and buries his head in her shawl. That experience is what taking full shelter of the Lord is like.
- So the Gita's purpose is that we give up the shelters of this world and accept the Lord's shelter.

#### On purification [28:00 – 30:00]

- The third verse contrasts daily bathing, which cleans the body, with a single bath in the nectar of the Gita, which removes the contamination of material existence.
- The teacher notes how refreshed consciousness feels after an ordinary bath, and asks what else could be used on a consciousness carrying the dust and wrong impressions of many lifetimes.

#### On sufficiency [30:00 – 32:00]

- The next verse asks what need there is for elaborate study of other scriptures when the Gita, which came from the Lord's own mouth, is carefully studied.
- Srila Prabhupada's illustration, as the teacher relates it: a person stranded from a shipwreck can carry only a bare minimum, the way one packs only essentials for a long journey because one has to carry them. A devotee in that situation would want chanting beads and one scripture — and if there is to be only one book, it has to be the Bhagavad-gita.
- The teacher also notes the claim that one who drinks Ganges water attains liberation, and the rhetorical question that follows about one who drinks the Gita. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### The cow and the milk [30:00 – 34:00]

The teacher treats this as one of the two verses the class should memorize:

- All the Upanishads are compared to a cow.
- The essence of a cow is milk.
- The milker is the son of the cowherd — Krishna.
- Arjuna is the calf, since a cow does not give milk without the calf present; anyone who has kept a cow knows the calf drinks first.
- The nectar of the Gita is the milk drawn.
- And as the calf drinks without the milk running out, so the Gita given to Arjuna remains available to all of us through him.

#### "If there is one scripture" [32:00 – 34:00]

The second verse the teacher asks the class to memorize states that there should be one scripture — the Gita, sung by the son of Devaki; one deity — Krishna; one mantra — which Srila Prabhupada identifies as the Hare Krishna maha-mantra; and one occupation — the service of that Lord.

His practical note: if you have to explain the Gita's distinction to someone, begin with these last two verses.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The Gita-mahatmya is not decorative praise. Each verse names a specific result — freedom from past reaction, the disposition to take shelter, the cleansing of consciousness, and sufficiency — which together describe the prayojana. [22:00 – 34:00]
</Callout>

### 7. Shruti and smriti — where the Gita belongs [36:00 – 42:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Śruti_: What comes directly from the Lord. [36:00 – 38:00]
- _Smṛti_: What the sages composed, on the strength of their study, so that _śruti_ could be understood. [36:00 – 38:00]
- _Apauruṣeya_: Not a product of human intellect; flawless knowledge descending through the line of hearing. [42:00 – 44:00]

#### The classification [36:00 – 40:00]

- Both _śruti_ and _smṛti_ are authoritative. The teacher notes that some schools treat _smṛti_ as less authoritative and rejects that position; the Puranas are equally authoritative, and are what make the difficult Sanskrit of _śruti_ intelligible.
- **Shruti** contains the Vedas. Originally one, they were expanded by Vyasadeva into Rig, Sama, Yajur, and Atharva. Each admits a fourfold division: _saṁhitā_, _brāhmaṇa_, _āraṇyaka_, and _upaniṣad_.
- **Smriti** contains the Puranas — eighteen principal and eighteen subsidiary — and the histories, Ramayana and Mahabharata.

#### The Gita is both [38:00 – 42:00]

- **As shruti:** It is called the _Gītopaniṣad_, for two reasons — it issues directly from the Lord's mouth, as _śruti_ does; and from the standpoint of subject matter it treats the three topics any Upanishad treats, the soul, the world, and the Lord.
- **As smriti:** From the standpoint of where the text is located, it falls within the Mahabharata — the Bhishma Parva, chapters 25 to 42 — and the Mahabharata belongs to _smṛti_.
- Beyond being one Upanishad among others, the Gita is the essence of them all, which is what the cow-and-milk verse states.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Remember the double answer: the Gita is śruti by its source and subject matter, and smriti by its location in the Mahabharata — and it is the essence of all the Upanishads rather than merely one of them. [36:00 – 42:00]
</Callout>

### 8. Why Vedic knowledge is flawless [42:00 – 48:00]

#### The four defects [42:00 – 44:00]

The teacher lists them in what he presents as a causal sequence:

1. **Imperfect senses** — the starting point.
2. **The tendency to fall into illusion**, which follows from imperfect perception.
3. **The tendency to commit mistakes**, which follows from illusion — his example is the appearance of water in a mirage.
4. **The cheating propensity** — the tendency to present the untrue as true.

As long as a person has these four, he cannot deliver perfect knowledge. The Vedas do, and the teacher's proof is that they do not change: the statement that we are not the body but the soul was made long ago and has never been revised, whereas scientific positions are revised every fifteen or twenty years.

#### Beyond common sense and beyond research [44:00 – 46:00]

- Much of Vedic knowledge can be understood by ordinary reasoning, but some of it lies beyond logic. The teacher's example is the scriptural statement about the purity of cow dung, and the false inference someone might draw by extending the logic further.
- It is also beyond material investigation — it has already been researched. His example is the Third Canto's description of the development of a child in the womb.

#### The attitude required [46:00 – 48:00]

- In material life a person can be intelligent, successful, and famous while living badly; in spiritual life that is not possible.
- He points to the second verse of the Srimad-Bhagavatam for two prerequisites — being free from envy, and good character. [Terms flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- He also cites the Fourth Chapter, where the Lord tells Arjuna the reason He is speaking to him: Arjuna is His devotee and friend.
- And BG 10.10, where the Lord gives understanding to those who are favourably disposed.
- The teacher labels the stance "until you prove it to me I will not accept anything" a demonic mentality, and contrasts it with the devotee's: let me try to see, let me follow what is reasonable, and understanding will come gradually.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Authority here rests on a source free of the four defects, received through an unbroken line — but receiving it also requires a disposition that is non-envious and willing to begin before everything has been proved. [42:00 – 48:00]
</Callout>

### 9. What makes the Gita itself special [48:00 – 52:00]

#### The setting [48:00]

The teacher lists what is extraordinary about the circumstances:

- It was spoken on a battlefield, without preparation, at a moment when a lecture would ordinarily be impossible — a lecture needs silence, and war was underway.
- It was not merely spoken but composed in _anuṣṭup_ metre. He demonstrates by counting the eight syllables in each line of the opening verse.
- So the Gita is literally a song sung by the Lord — and a topmost philosophical treatise delivered extempore in poetry, in the middle of a battlefield, to a depressed listener asking questions.

#### The speaker [50:00]

- The teacher refers to Sridhara Swami's benedictory verse on the Gita, which he recalls as contrasting the endless glorification of the Lord by Ananta Shesha through innumerable mouths, without repetition, with the still greater speciality of the Gita. He states he does not remember the verse. [Verse flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- He relays an acharya's observation about how remarkable Krishna's life is across the occupational orders: born into a kshatriya family, raised among the mercantile community, acting as a charioteer on the battlefield, and speaking the Gita, which is a brahmana's work.
- His conclusion: the setting is divine, the speaker is divine, and the hearer is divine.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The Gita's distinction is not only its content. Setting, metre, speaker, and hearer are each extraordinary, and understanding that shapes how one approaches the text. [48:00 – 52:00]
</Callout>

### 10. What makes Srila Prabhupada's edition special [52:00 – 60:00]

**Context.** The teacher reads the last two paragraphs of "Setting the Scene," identifying them as the two places where the commentary proper is announced; the rest of that section is the historical background.

#### Study habits [52:00 – 54:00]

Two recommendations for studying any book:

1. Keep one personal copy, marked and distinguishable from the several copies a devotee household usually holds — for noting, adding headings, and underlining.
2. Number the paragraphs of every purport and introductory section. This makes detailed reading and analysis of the purports considerably easier.

He mentions that verse-based questions drawn from the purports will be circulated as the course proceeds, and that working through them improves the depth of study.

#### The publisher's observation [56:00 – 58:00]

- The teacher asks the class to underline the line noting the general tendency of English translators of the Gita to set Krishna aside in order to make room for their own conceptions and philosophies.
- His restatement of the charge: they are not allowing Krishna to speak, while trading on His name.
- He connects this to the proliferation of the Gita adapted for particular professions and interests, all carrying heavy interpretation.
- The specific kind of misreading he names is allegorical: the five Pandavas taken to represent the five senses, or the Kauravas taken to represent bad qualities and the Pandavas good qualities. It sounds appealing and yields some application, but the Gita is historical fact from which lessons may be drawn, and treating it allegorically opens the door to further misinterpretation.
- Srila Prabhupada's position, as the teacher states it: Krishna is not a mythological or allegorical personality, and not even merely a historical one, but the Supreme Personality of Godhead — a term he notes Srila Prabhupada coined.

#### The proof of the claim [58:00 – 60:00]

- The final paragraph states that this translation and commentary direct the reader toward Krishna rather than away from Him.
- The teacher offers the worldwide network of centres and temples as evidence that this was not merely a claim: people read that edition and took shelter of Krishna.
- His counter-example is the number of Gita editions that produced no devotees, and some that took readers further away — he notes that the gravity of that is captured in the phrase _using Krishna to take people away from Krishna_.
- He also mentions the Fourth Chapter purport, around 4.2 or 4.3, where Srila Prabhupada explains that he had not intended to write on the Gita, having noted how many editions already existed, but found on reading them that not one was authoritative. [Reference as spoken; see Verification Flags.]

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The test of a commentary is its direction. A translation that sets the speaker aside to make room for the translator's views fails that test, however scholarly it appears. [52:00 – 60:00]
</Callout>

### 11. Structural facts worth knowing [60:00 – 68:00]

#### Comparison with the Gita Press edition [60:00 – 64:00]

The teacher works through two differences, both of which turn out to be non-issues:

- **Chapter One count.** Srila Prabhupada's edition has 46 verses; the Gita Press edition has 47. No verse is extra — the division differs. Some verses in the first chapter run to three lines; where Srila Prabhupada keeps such a verse as a single numbered unit, the other edition splits it. He points to 1.26 as a three-line verse.
- **BG 13.1.** This verse is present in Srila Prabhupada's edition and absent from the Gita Press edition, which is why both nevertheless total 700 verses. The Vaishnava acharyas had the verse in the text they read and commented on it; some other commentators did not. The teacher adds a contextual argument: the Twelfth Chapter is on devotional service, and the Thirteenth opens on an entirely different subject — the field, the knower of the field, nature, the enjoyer, knowledge, and the object of knowledge — so a question from Arjuna introducing those topics creates no logical problem. And if someone declines to accept the verse, nothing is lost, since the Lord proceeds to explain the same subject matter regardless.

#### Who speaks how much [64:00]

| Speaker       | Verses |
| :------------ | :----- |
| Krishna       | 575    |
| Arjuna        | 83     |
| Sanjaya       | 41     |
| Dhritarashtra | 1      |

The teacher's mnemonic is the initial letters in decreasing order — Krishna, Arjuna, Sanjaya, Dhritarashtra. Remember the first two figures and the rest can be worked out.

#### Names [64:00 – 66:00]

- 33 names are used for the Lord and 20 for Arjuna in the Gita. [Figures flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- Each name carries significance for the moment at which it is used. The teacher gives Partha, Kaunteya, Bharata, Dhananjaya, and Parantapa as addresses to Arjuna, and Madhusudana and Hrishikesha as addresses to the Lord, with the import of the name supplying the particular encouragement intended at that point.

#### Commentaries and further reading [66:00 – 68:00]

- **Gaudiya Vaishnava commentaries** most often referred to: Vishvanatha Chakravarti Thakura's _Sārārtha-varṣiṇī_ and Baladeva Vidyabhushana's _Gītā-bhūṣaṇa_.
- **Other commentaries** mentioned: Ramanujacharya, Sridhara Swami, and Bhaktivinoda Thakura. Translations of some of these are available through Gita Press.
- **English translations** of the Vishvanatha Chakravarti and Baladeva Vidyabhushana commentaries are by Bhanu Swami. The teacher notes the Sanskrit cannot be fully rendered in English, but the additional material still helps considerably.
- **Recommended companion volume:** _Surrender Unto Me_ by Bhurijana Prabhu, which he praises for explaining the flow of the Gita in simple, beautiful language, and describes the author as a senior Vaishnava scholar who also wrote on the practice of chanting. [Titles and attributions flagged; see Verification Flags.]

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Know the structural facts — verse counts, the 13.1 question, who speaks how much, and which commentaries exist — so that textual variations do not become a source of doubt. [60:00 – 68:00]
</Callout>

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications

### 1. Shlokas simply do not stay in my memory — what should I do? [68:00 – 72:00]

- **Question:** A participant explains that meanings are retained but the verses themselves are not, and asks for a method, both for the examination and generally.
- **Answer:** The teacher offers a concrete procedure and asks the class to report back in twelve days.
- The course involves roughly forty to forty-five verses overall, and nobody is expected to memorize them all at once.
- Take ten at a time and keep a printed sheet of them with you.
- Read them through with the word-for-word meanings, at least well enough to paraphrase what each verse says; knowing the word-for-word meaning is better still. Read them two or three times.
- Then recite the sheet three times a day, following the traditional pattern of three daily periods, using whatever breaks arise.
- Keep this up for at least ten to twelve days.
- The essential condition: while reciting, know the meaning, so that some meditation on the meaning accompanies the recitation.
- Do not take on the tension of memorizing. If after twelve days nothing has stuck, the matter can be reconsidered — but he expects it will work.
- Asked about other students' anxiety over pronunciation, his advice was to keep a safe distance from that conversation and simply try the method.

### 3. Did Vyasadeva put the Gita into metre, or did Krishna speak it in metre? [72:00 – 74:00]

- **Question:** A participant with an interest in poetry describes the rules of _anuṣṭup_ metre and asks whether Krishna spoke in that form directly or Vyasadeva arranged it afterwards.
- **Answer:** The teacher declines the speculative framing and answers directly: the Lord spoke it as it is.
- Vyasadeva was _trikāla-darśī_, seeing past, present, and future; he composed the Mahabharata, with the Gita within it, knowing what was to occur — just as Valmiki had already composed the Ramayana beforehand.
- The same applies to the Bhagavatam, which Vyasadeva composed and later Shukadeva Gosvami narrated; the narrated discussion is what is written, and those events also took place.
- He encouraged the questioner to write poetry on the Gita, in _anuṣṭup_ or another metre, as a form of meditation on it.

### 4. Why do sincere devotees face difficulties? [74:00 – 80:00]

- **Question:** Advanced devotees remain undisturbed in hardship, but devotees around them become disturbed and ask why such a person should suffer. The questioner's usual answer — that Krishna removes most of one's karma and leaves a little for purification — is not easily accepted by others.
- **Answer:** The teacher first notes that not everyone's capacity to absorb an explanation is the same.
- The principle he gives is that time acts in three ways:
  - **As a punisher**, for the ordinary person engaged in forbidden and improper activity.
  - **As a purifier**, for the practitioner — past reactions still have to be worked through, and the Lord wants to purify him, so some treatment is required.
  - **As a glorifier**, for pure devotees. The Pandavas are his example.
- He acknowledges that telling people the hardship is purification will often not be accepted, and may simply disturb them further.
- His larger point is that suffering itself is an important element of life, and for some people the lesson has to be driven into their consciousness. He recounts Kamsa killing Devaki's children, and the appearance of the goddess who told him that the one destined to kill him had already taken birth — the first moment, he says, when any sense entered Kamsa.
- What actually convinces observers is the reaction they see: a devotee in distress who still chants, still prays, and does not blame the Lord. Problems come in devotees' lives too; the difference is the attitude toward them.
