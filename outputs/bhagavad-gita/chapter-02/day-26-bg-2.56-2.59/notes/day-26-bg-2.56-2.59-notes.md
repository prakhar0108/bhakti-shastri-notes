## Class Snapshot & One-Line Argument Map

- **Class Snapshot:** Arjuna's second and third questions are answered. BG 2.56 – 2.57 describe how the _sthita-prajña_ "speaks" — that is, what runs in his mind when happiness and distress arrive; 2.58 opens the section on restraining the senses with the image of the tortoise; and 2.59 supplies the decisive distinction between mere abstinence and actual freedom — the _higher taste_.
- **One-Line Argument Map:** He is neither agitated in distress nor elated in happiness, and is free of attachment, fear and anger -> having neither affection nor aversion for whatever good or evil comes, since in the material world something is always going on -> when unwanted sense objects appear he withdraws the senses as a tortoise withdraws its limbs -> but abstinence alone leaves the taste intact | the taste itself ceases only on experiencing something higher.

## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 2.56 [04:00 – 22:00]

<!-- verse:2.56 -->

**Context.** The answer to "how does he speak" — which is really a question about the inner commentary.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Duḥkheṣv anudvigna-manāḥ_ [04:00]: not agitated in mind amid the threefold miseries.
- _Sukheṣu vigata-spṛhaḥ_ [08:00]: without hankering when happiness comes.
- _Vīta-rāga-bhaya-krodhaḥ_ [14:00 – 16:00]: free of attachment, fear and anger. **Attachment** to the objects of sense gratification; **fear** that what gratifies the senses will be lost; **anger** at whoever takes it away.
- _Muni_ [14:00]: from _manana_, deliberation — but here, as the purport specifies, not one who merely deliberates on anything; one who has arrived at the conclusion that Vāsudeva is everything.

#### What he does _not_ say [04:00 – 06:00]

Not "why has this happened to me", not "the Lord has done this to me", not "so-and-so caused it." He understands: **this is my own past activity; I have brought it, and I must go through it.**

- He does not refuse practical remedy — if he falls ill he goes to a physician; he works to counteract distress. **He simply does not blame anyone.**

#### The bull and Mahārāja Parīkṣit [06:00 – 08:00]

From the First Canto: Mahārāja Parīkṣit finds a man in dark clothes beating a bull and draws his sword, but first asks the bull who has caused him this suffering, promising immediate punishment even if the offender were a demigod. **The bull replies that he does not know.**

- Some say it is due to activity in a former life; some say the instrument is only an instrument; some point to past time; some to destiny. He himself gave distress to someone in a previous life — but he does not know whom, so how can he name anyone now? Mahārāja Parīkṣit recognises that this is religion personified.

#### Nobody can know why [10:00 – 12:00]

- From the First Canto again: Bhīṣmadeva, lying on the bed of arrows, sees the Pāṇḍavas' suffering and asks aloud why it should come to them — sons of the lord of religion, who never did anything wrong, always followed religious principles, with such a mother. **Great sages were present, and everyone turned to him for the answer.**
- **The Lord's master plan cannot be understood.** The more one tries to know exactly why something is happening, the more bewildered one becomes.
- **What we can know is what to do** — how to practise devotion in the situation, how to take shelter in it.
- **The dengue analogy:** a report comes back positive and one demands to be shown the mosquito responsible. The mosquito is not going to be found. **Begin the treatment.**

#### The devotee's level, one step beyond [08:00 – 10:00]

- The _sthita-prajña_ understands that it is his own past activity. **The devotee understands that Kṛṣṇa's mercy is hidden in it** — that Kṛṣṇa knows what is best, is giving exactly that, and that the situation is to be used to grasp His lotus feet more firmly.
- **The image the teacher uses:** when one begins devotional life, the quota of happiness and distress passes from the hands of the illusory energy into the hands of the Lord, and He administers portions of it as needed — because the practice is slack and that is what is required.
- The purport is explicit: when difficulty comes, the devotee thinks it is some past activity and that by the Lord's mercy he is being given only a reduced measure of it; **when good times come he does not credit his own activity** but credits the Lord, thinks himself unworthy, and increases his service. **Grateful in distress; increasing his service in happiness.**

#### Śrīla Prabhupāda as the illustration [12:00 – 14:00, 20:00]

- Seen only through his years in India, the record looks like failure — no support in business, no support at home, his copy of the Gītā at one point exchanged for household necessities, no one assisting the preaching.
- **Thirty years without success, and he never said** "how could my spiritual master leave me with such an instruction and depart himself." He did not stop.
- Then in the West came a success nobody had anticipated, with figures of great public standing coming to him. **He was distracted by neither** — he stopped for neither the failure nor the success.
- **The contrast:** Sugrīva, who until a moment before had been living in austerity on the mountain, received a kingdom and forgot everything. And in distress we also forget — a person becomes agitated and irritated.

#### Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura's gloss [16:00]

He is never **disgusted** — never asking why such a calamity should come into his life — and never over-excited when something succeeds.

#### The verse Śrīla Prabhupāda called the guideline for a devotee's life [16:00 – 18:00]

The Bhāgavatam verse on one who, experiencing distress, sees it clearly as the Lord's mercy, as the result of his own activity, offers obeisances from the heart, remains grateful, and goes on waiting hopefully for the Lord's mercy — **that person becomes eligible for liberation.**

#### Why a higher goal is the precondition [18:00 – 20:00]

- Most people's lives run in exactly the opposite way **because there is no goal in them.**
- Where there is one — the _sthita-prajña_ experiencing the self, the mystic experiencing the Supersoul, the devotee experiencing the higher taste — happiness and distress can be borne. Without it, it is very difficult.
- **The ordinary person runs two projects:** maximising happiness (the next promotion, the festival bonus, the next increment in profit, a hundred and eight plans in the mind) and **reducing misery to zero.** Neither is within his control: the happiness cannot be increased by a paisa beyond what is fixed, and the misery cannot be brought to zero. The human lifetime passes between the two.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Steadiness is not stoicism. The *sthita-prajña* takes practical remedy but blames no one; the devotee goes further and reads the Lord's hand in it. [06:00, 08:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.57 [22:00 – 30:00]

<!-- verse:2.57 -->

**Context.** The same principle from another angle.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Anabhisnehaḥ_ [22:00]: without affection — meaning without attraction _to material things_. **Not that he is devoid of affection:** his affection for Kṛṣṇa and for the devotees is very great.
- _Tat tat prāpya śubhāśubham_ [22:00]: obtaining this or that, good or evil — profit or loss, something gained or gone, honour paid or insult offered.
- _Na abhinandati na dveṣṭi_ [22:00]: he neither praises nor resents it.

#### Why elation and depression are the same symptom [22:00 – 24:00]

- One who becomes very excited in success is generally the one who becomes very disturbed in misery. **Both are one symptom**, and progress is difficult for such a person, because he is riding on the chariot of the mind.
- One who knows that success also came by the Lord's mercy knows in failure: _I gave my best; it was not the Lord's will; something else is intended._
- **Śrīla Prabhupāda's phrase:** don't blame the instrument.
- **And the safeguard is practice** [24:00]: this consciousness is possible only where hearing and chanting hold the mind. Otherwise the whole of a lifetime — not part of it — goes into these two projects.

#### The nature of this world [24:00 – 26:00]

- The purport's first line: in the material world there is always something going on, which may be good or evil.
- **The teacher's illustration:** a man lacks money and works so hard earning it that his health breaks; the health is addressed and someone else at home falls ill; if nothing else, a marriage has to be arranged — and before the marriage there is distress that it has not happened, and after it there may be distress that it has.
- The purport continues that one who is not agitated by such material affairs is to be understood as fixed in Kṛṣṇa consciousness.
- **The application** [26:00]: the morning and evening programme and one's practice are not to be compromised for any of it. "An examination came up", "I was travelling", "something happened at home", "there is construction going on", "I had to arrange my brother's admission" — **these will never stop happening.** They are not to be offered as excuses.

#### Prahlāda Mahārāja and Queen Kuntī [26:00]

- Prahlāda Mahārāja underwent enormous suffering and **never once prayed for it to be removed.** When Lord Nṛsiṁhadeva offered him any benediction, he neither asked for material happiness nor accepted the benediction offered — only that his consciousness remain turned towards the Lord.
- **Queen Kuntī went a step further** and asked for calamities — because in calamity, at least, He comes.

#### Good and evil are not absolute [26:00 – 30:00]

- The same situation a person longs for, he curses a little later. A man is unhappy because he has no job; ask him after he has one, and he is unhappy because of the job. A man is distressed that his marriage has not been arranged, and after it is arranged he may be distressed that it was.
- **The cultural preference for a son** is cited as another example: if the daughter rises to high office and the son turns out badly, which was the auspicious birth?
- **The visa, the settlement abroad** — good or bad depends entirely on what is done there.
- **Even the menu:** whether a rich dish is good or bad depends on whether one is judging by taste or by health.
- **Lord Caitanya's principle** is cited: these designations of good and bad are mental concoction.
- **The conclusion the teacher draws:** if you want to enjoy materially, anything will turn bad; **if you want to serve, even a bad situation has hope.** The example given is Śrīla Prabhupāda's own account of his marriage — that as a young man he had wanted a beautiful wife, and his father's remark that it would make the later stage of renunciation easier.
- **So what is good?** Whatever helps us take shelter of Kṛṣṇa.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Since something is always going on, waiting for conditions to settle before practising is a guarantee that practice never begins. [24:00, 26:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.58 [30:00 – 44:00]

<!-- verse:2.58 -->

**Context.** The third question — _kim āsīta_, how does he sit — that is, how does he restrain the senses. The answer runs from 2.58 to 2.63.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Kūrmaḥ_ [32:00]: the tortoise.
- _Saṁharate aṅgāni sarvaśaḥ_ [32:00]: draws in all its limbs.
- _Indriyāṇi indriya-arthebhyaḥ_ [32:00]: the senses from the objects of the senses — here specifically the **unwanted** ones: a sight that should not be seen, food that should not be accepted.

#### Why the tortoise [32:00 – 34:00]

- The tortoise's head and limbs are out; at the first sign of danger everything goes inside and only the shell is visible. **The shell is extraordinarily strong** — it is said an elephant could stand on it — **while the limbs themselves are very delicate.**
- **The parallel:** our senses are our weak point. And in this world, weakness is exactly where one is attacked. **The boxing maxim: hit where it hurts.** Once a punch draws blood at one spot, every subsequent punch is directed at that same spot. The illusory energy knows our weakness and defeats us there.
- **Therefore each person must know his own weak sense** and be cautious about it. One who knows his eyes are his weakness must be careful accordingly; one who is quick to anger should stay away from quarrels, because he is already vulnerable there.

#### Out of sight, out of mind [36:00 – 38:00]

- **The billboard:** an advertiser does not spread his hoardings evenly across a city; he places the same one every few metres along a road. That is not foolishness, it is psychology. Seen once, twice, ten times, fifty times, **it registers, and then we act on it.**
- **The principle:** every contact of a sense with a sense object affects us; every taste has an impact and leaves something in the consciousness. The casual attitude — "a little of this is fine", "a little news just to stay updated", "an old film is harmless" — misses this.
- **The scriptural examples the teacher lists:** Bharata Mahārāja _looked a little_ at a deer, and his story occupies several chapters, followed by several more as Jaḍa Bharata; Arjuna _looked a little_ at the army, and the whole Gītā had to be spoken; Ajāmila looked; Kaikeyī listened.

#### Two wrong conclusions [38:00 – 42:00]

1. **"Detachment alone is the whole answer."** It is not. The tortoise is only one part; engagement is the other part, which comes in the verses ahead. The well-known maxim about seeing, hearing and speaking no evil covers only this half.
2. **"Let me test my spiritual strength by exposing myself to sense objects."** — _How will you know your character if you never test it?_ The teacher's reply: **many are martyred in the testing.** This is not to be done.

#### What restraint actually achieves [40:00 – 42:00]

1. **Safety.** One who follows idle curiosity — this must also be seen, that must also be heard — becomes unsafe, and somewhere a mistake certainly occurs.
2. **Conservation of energy.** **Will-power is limited.** If something wrong is opened and then closed by an exertion of will, that will-power has been spent on saying _no_ — and the same will-power could have been spent on saying _yes_. **If all the energy goes into saying no, no energy remains for saying yes.**
   - **The study analogy:** someone studying with conversation nearby, a film playing, notifications arriving and advertisements appearing on the screen spends energy dismissing each one. Close the network and the whole of that energy goes into the study.
   - **Hence one places oneself in a right culture** rather than relying on resistance.

#### The purport [42:00 – 44:00]

- **The test of a _yogī_, a devotee or a self-realised soul is how far he can control his senses according to his plan.**
- **The senses are like serpents** — they want to act loosely, without restriction, and are not easily caught.
- **But** one in Kṛṣṇa consciousness knows the senses belong to the master of the senses; they are to be engaged in His service and kept from everything else.

### Bhagavad-gita 2.59 [44:00 – 60:00]

<!-- verse:2.59 -->

**Context.** Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura's objection, and the answer that defines the whole of devotional practice.

#### The objection [44:00]

What is so remarkable about withdrawing the senses? **A foolish man who falls ill also abstains.** A patient with a fever also avoids fried food and eats only plain, clean fare. **Where is the difference between him and the _sthita-prajña_?**

#### The answer [44:00 – 48:00]

- **The ill man abstains _rasa-varjam_ — the objects go, but the taste does not go.** He is forbidden sweets and the relish for sweets remains.
- **The teacher's contemporary example:** a person who comes to Kṛṣṇa consciousness having eaten meat for years gives it up, and yet from the smell alone can still identify exactly what is being cooked. The object has been given up; **the taste has not.**
- **The _sthita-prajña_ is the one in whom the taste itself has ceased** — and the verse says how: _paraṁ dṛṣṭvā_, **by experiencing something higher.**

#### The practical principle [46:00 – 50:00]

- **We can give up sense objects exactly to the degree that we have experienced Kṛṣṇa** — to the degree of the mind's absorption in His name, form, qualities and pastimes.
- **And not to be discouraged:** it is not zero. It is precisely because it is not zero that we are chanting, honouring the Lord's food, and in the association of devotees at all.
- **But at the beginning the senses have to be engaged somewhat forcibly** — more chanting, more reading of Śrīla Prabhupāda's books, more service. Otherwise, free time simply becomes sense gratification, and **the taste never leaves the other side.**
- **If a person wants to give up the taste for sense gratification, he must engage in devotion.** The teacher's blunt point about quantity: a man gives ten or twelve hours to his work and a fixed share to his family; **in devotion we cheat ourselves — because Kṛṣṇa cannot be cheated — by expecting attachment without giving time.** First give the time; sit there; something will happen.
- **The principle of favourable acceptance** [50:00]: accept with resolve what is favourable to devotion and reject with resolve what is unfavourable — and keep oneself inside a devotional culture. **Whoever comes near a fire feels some warmth**; one entirely engaged in material things cannot expect the higher taste to arrive.

#### The purport [50:00 – 56:00]

- **Unless one is transcendentally situated it is not possible to cease from sense enjoyment.** After so many lifetimes of it, it does not simply fall away.
- **The patient analogy from the purport:** a physician restricts a sick man's entire diet down to plain gruel — and the patient neither likes the restrictions nor loses his taste for the forbidden food.
- **The famous line that follows:** one who has tasted the beauty of the Supreme Lord in the course of advancement in Kṛṣṇa consciousness no longer has a taste for dead material things.
- **The three stages of the senses** [52:00]: impure, semi-pure, pure. In the impure stage the taste for material things is present; practice carries one gradually to the semi-pure stage; in the pure stage the taste is gone.
- **Why people will not let go** [54:00]: the scriptures say the sense object is like a five-rupee note and what is offered in devotion is a cheque for five crores — and still it is not released, because the living being is by nature pleasure-seeking, and every renunciation feels like a fast. _What will become of life if this goes?_ Even a simple restriction placed on a device produces anxiety.
- **And the honest difficulty** [54:00]: after becoming a devotee the taste for gratification does not go, and the taste in devotion does not arrive quickly either — reading gives little relish, hearing gives little, chanting feels like austerity, and whether the spiritual world exists at all is not yet known. **Progress is made by trusting the scripture and travelling a little of the road.** The teacher cites campus preaching: a few sessions and a two-day camp, and the change in young people's lives is considerable.

#### Real wealth and false wealth [56:00 – 60:00]

- **This is not blind faith but reasonable.** A man clinging to an old, ruined car will let it go at once if a new one is offered. The scripture says something far greater is available; therefore give up what is wrong.
- **The difference between false and real wealth:** false wealth does not bring satisfaction even after it is obtained; real wealth, once obtained, leaves no further hankering.
- **The Biblical phrase quoted:** strive for the wealth that thieves cannot steal, that moths cannot destroy, that fire cannot burn.
- **The bigger the man, the bigger the beggar:** the beggar at the railway station needs a few rupees; the businessman needs lakhs or crores. **Who is the greater beggar?**
- **The illustration of real wealth** [58:00]: the account in Śrīla Prabhupāda's biography of Shailee Agarwal, in whose home he stayed in America, who said he was **the best guest she could have had** — because she never had to attend to him at all. He cooked for himself, chanted, kept his morning programme; she would go out about her work and he would be chanting; she would return and he would be chanting.
- **The definition:** the truly wealthy man is not the one who has the most but **the one who needs the least.**
- The Sixth Chapter's statement is cited — that on gaining this, one thinks there is no greater gain than this.

#### The three efforts required [60:00]

1. **Effort to give quantity of time** to Kṛṣṇa consciousness.
2. **Effort to remain in the fire of congregational chanting** — especially the morning programme, chanting and reading. **And at the practitioner's stage, do not say "my mind is not in it."** Arjuna's mind was not in it either.
3. **Effort to be attentive** — because until the mind comes to a thing, the thing does not happen.

Done regularly, these begin to yield glimpses of the higher taste.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Abstinence and freedom look identical from outside and are opposite inside: the patient keeps the taste and loses the object; the *sthita-prajña* loses the taste because he has found a higher one. [44:00, 46:00]
</Callout>

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications [62:00 – 66:00]

**Q1 — With the tortoise principle, how do we decide when to withdraw the senses? Often we go somewhere believing it is a safe space — especially in family and social life — and only on arriving discover it is not.** [62:00 – 64:00]

- **The barometer is the refinement of the consciousness itself.** The better spiritual life is practised, the more alert a person becomes; anything that takes one away from Kṛṣṇa is grasped immediately as not right.
- Gross parameters are already known to everyone; **the subtle element depends entirely on how refined the consciousness has become.** The more refined, the more quickly even a small danger is sensed.
- **And the definition differs from person to person.** The analogy: two buckets of refuse tipped into a refuse truck make no difference; two or three scraps of paper in a clean room or a temple hall _are_ refuse. **As consciousness becomes refined, we become correspondingly more careful in dealing with the world, precisely in order to protect it.**

**Q2 — About the point that one very elated in success is also more disturbed in difficulty: will that not simply improve with time and practice?** [64:00 – 66:00]

- It can come with time, but **mainly it comes as the consciousness develops.**
- **The root of it:** for such a person, material things carry very great value in the mind. Because that importance is there, he is affected more.
