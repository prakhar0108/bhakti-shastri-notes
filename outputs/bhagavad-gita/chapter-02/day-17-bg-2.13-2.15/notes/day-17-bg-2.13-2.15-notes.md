## Class Snapshot & One-Line Argument Map

- **Class Snapshot:** The class moves from theory to practice. BG 2.13 stated the principle; BG 2.14 gives its first practical application — tolerance of the dualities that arise from sense contact; BG 2.15 states the result — one who is steady in both happiness and distress becomes eligible for liberation. BG 2.16 is then introduced, distinguishing the non-existent from the eternal.
- **One-Line Argument Map:** Happiness and distress arise only from the contact of the senses with their objects, and they come and go like the seasons -> so they are to be tolerated rather than resented, because tolerance is what makes the discharge of one's duty possible -> and one who remains steady through both becomes eligible for liberation -> since in the end the perishable cannot be preserved and the eternal can never be destroyed.

## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 2.13 — the principle restated [10:00]

<!-- verse:2.13 -->

**Context.** Briefly revisited as the theory on which the next verse builds: nobody weeps when infancy gives way to youth or youth to old age, so the transition to another body should not bewilder the sober.

**A further exchange the teacher reports** [18:00]

- Arjuna's reply to that principle: this is the law of nature — but You are the master of nature and can change the law, as the Ninth Chapter states that material nature works under the Lord's direction.
- The Lord's answer, contained in the next verse: **I have not come to change the law of nature; I have come to change you.**
- The principle for us: the world will not change. Changing oneself is the real project.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
BG 2.13 is the theory and BG 2.14 is its first application — and the answer to "why not change the situation?" is that the Lord has come to change the person, not the law of nature. [18:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.14 [10:00 – 30:00]

<!-- verse:2.14 -->

**Context.** The first practical instruction of the Gītā. Having established what the self is, the Lord tells Arjuna what to _do_ about the feelings that will inevitably arise.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Mātrā-sparśāḥ_ [10:00]: sense perception — the contact of the senses with their objects.
- _Śīta-uṣṇa-sukha-duḥkha-dāḥ_ [10:00]: giving cold and heat, happiness and distress.
- _Āgama-apāyinaḥ_ [10:00]: appearing and disappearing.
- _Anityāḥ_ [12:00]: non-permanent.
- _Titikṣasva_ [12:00]: just tolerate them.

#### Why the same thing gives pleasure and then pain [12:00]

In summer everyone wants to bathe in cold water — the pool, the river, whatever is available, and it is a pleasure. A few months later, in winter, that very same cold water becomes a source of distress. The experience reverses because it was never a property of the object; it arose from contact, and contact changes.

#### The three levels at which tolerance is required [14:00 – 16:00]

- **The body:** heat and cold. Someone who becomes agitated makes it the subject of endless conversation — "such heat this year", and when the rains come, "such rain this year". The teacher's illustration: when a train halts, the entire compartment turns it into a discussion about the whole railway system.
- **The mind:** favourable and unfavourable circumstances — the preparation one likes or does not like, someone's pleasant or unpleasant behaviour; sometimes the mind is willing for reading, chanting or service, and sometimes it is not.
- **The false ego:** honour and dishonour. Sometimes one is garlanded; sometimes "purification" happens instead. The teacher's own phrase for walking past a group praising you versus a group discussing you otherwise: glorification and purification.

#### Why tolerance is not mere endurance [16:00 – 22:00]

- Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura is cited: tolerating these is our _dharma_.
- Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa gives the mechanism: tolerate the present dualities and continue to discharge your duty; from that discharge knowledge arises, and that knowledge leads to liberation. So tolerance is not pointless suffering.
- **Two names in the verse are the argument** [20:00]: Arjuna is addressed as _Kaunteya_ and _Bhārata_ — connected to two great families. On the mother's side, Kuntī, who tolerated a great deal for the sake of _dharma_; on the father's side, the Bharata dynasty. Both lines endured much for religious principles, and Arjuna is being reminded of that inheritance.
- **Tolerance is not torture** [20:00]: in torture a person is overwhelmed and can do nothing positive; tolerance exists precisely so that something higher can be done. The formulation given: **tolerance gives rise to focus**.
- **Why this is needed at all:** the material world is characterised by constant and unpredictable change. If one learns to tolerate, one keeps moving; otherwise one stops at every disturbance.
- **A cultural note** [20:00]: modern society emphasises rights; Vedic culture emphasises responsibility. Tolerance belongs to the second frame and is often read negatively in the first.

#### Both happiness and distress have to be tolerated [22:00 – 30:00]

- Distress obviously distracts. So does pleasure — and both pull one away from the goal.
- **Jayadratha** [28:00]: when the sun appeared to set and Arjuna prepared to enter the fire in keeping with his vow, Jayadratha became excited and began to celebrate — and came forward, at which the Lord withdrew the covering of the sun and Arjuna killed him. Pleasure distracted him fatally.
- In devotional life the same holds: very comfortable circumstances also make it difficult to take shelter. And the loss of a material pleasure — the power going out, the cooler or air-conditioner failing — should be understood as the loss of one pleasure, not of all pleasure.
- **The driver and the student** [22:00]: a man on his way to an important meeting, or a student going to an examination, does not stop the car to fight about someone's bad driving; the focus makes the small thing small.

#### Prabhupāda's purport and the teacher's application [22:00 – 28:00]

- The purport's opening principle: in the proper discharge of duty one has to learn to tolerate the non-permanent appearance and disappearance of happiness and distress. Two examples are given there — bathing in the morning even in the cold months of January and February, and cooking in the kitchen in the heat of May and June. In both cases the duty is performed despite the discomfort.
- The second paragraph explains the two names, _Kaunteya_ and _Bhārata_.
- **"Keep small things small"** [24:00]: where there is no larger purpose, trivial matters expand to fill a life — the endless discussion in a household about who said what. Let one family member be admitted to intensive care and all of it stops at once, because something big has arrived. The positive version is to have something genuinely big: pleasing _hari_, _guru_ and _vaiṣṇava_, and making this human life successful.
- **The wrong turn** [26:00]: if the GPS shows that a wrong turn has added two kilometres, one does not stop the car to argue about it; one takes the U-turn, because arguing only wastes more time.
- **The mind's auto-repeat mode** [26:00]: when something unpleasant happens and there is nothing bigger in life, the mind replays it continuously, and then it gets narrated to ten other people, until the matter has grown far beyond its original size.
- **Some things cannot be changed** [26:00 – 28:00]: the family one lives among, the close relations one has — resenting them permanently stops one's progress; they have to be accepted, tolerated and worked with, because the important thing is still fully available: the opportunity to serve Kṛṣṇa remains intact.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Happiness and distress come and go like the seasons, so they are to be tolerated — not as pointless endurance but because tolerance is what keeps a person focused on a purpose that outlasts them. [12:00, 20:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.15 [30:00 – 50:00]

<!-- verse:2.15 -->

**Context.** The result of the previous instruction. Having said "tolerate", the Lord now says what such tolerance produces.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Puruṣarṣabha_ [30:00]: best among men — the address chosen for Arjuna here.
- _Na vyathayanti_ [30:00]: they do not distress him.
- _Sama-duḥkha-sukham dhīram_ [32:00]: equipoised in distress and happiness, sober.
- _Amṛtatvāya kalpate_ [32:00]: becomes eligible for liberation, for the eternal.

#### Attachment to sense contact ends in suffering [32:00]

The teacher recounts the five classic examples of creatures destroyed through a single sense:

- The bee, drawn by fragrance into the lotus, trapped when it closes.
- The moth, drawn by light, destroyed in the flame.
- The elephant, captured through the sense of touch.
- The deer, drawn by the hunter's music through the sense of hearing.
- And where fish are concerned, the tongue.

The human being has all five senses strong at once — so one who is not attached to the five sense objects is qualified for liberation.

#### What a _dhīra_ actually does [32:00 – 38:00]

- He does not try to change circumstances, knowing they cannot be changed; he changes himself.
- Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura's point: as one keeps tolerating, the same things gradually stop causing distress.
- **The air-conditioner example** [34:00]: for one accustomed to sleeping in air-conditioning, a failure is a serious difficulty; for one whose house has none, it is nothing at all — many sleep with a fan, and many sleep on a station platform.
- **Rising early** [34:00 – 36:00]: for one who sleeps late, rising early is a punishment; for devotees in whom the habit is formed, it is a pleasure. Similarly with fasting: missing a meal is distressing to most, but on Ekādaśī devotees do not experience it as distress.
- Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa's definition of the _dhīra_ [38:00]: one who has settled his intelligence in the conviction "I must practise _dharma_, and whatever must be tolerated for it, I shall tolerate." That resolve is what a practitioner needs — acting on the authority of scripture even before full realization.

#### Why nobody else is to blame [38:00]

No one in this world can actually harm us; what returns to us is our own previous activity. Hence the instruction not to criticise others. Practical arrangements against heat and cold may be made, but one should not become absorbed in elaborate arrangements — for the body, for food, for comfort — because that absorption itself pulls a person off the path.

#### The reward that is waiting [42:00 – 48:00]

- BG 2.13 is the theory, BG 2.14 the practical application, and BG 2.15 the result: what is waiting for one who tolerates and performs his duty.
- **Nothing great is achieved without sacrifice** — and sacrifice, in practice, is simply the tolerance of many small things.
- **The examination-hall analogy** [44:00]: going to an unfamiliar school for a board examination, nobody notices the gate, the architecture, the furniture or the lighting — the focus eliminates everything else. Without an examination, one notices all of it.
- **The comparison that stings** [46:00]: in material work a person tolerates everything — sleeping at one in the morning and still reaching the office on time, accepting any amount of rebuke from an employer with "yes sir, no problem, thank you sir" — while the same person misses the morning programme and cannot absorb a fraction of that treatment from devotees.
- **Watch the whole film** [48:00]: in the Mahābhārata the Pāṇḍavas lose at dice and go to the forest — but do not stop there; they are victorious, they regain the kingdom, and they follow the Lord at the end. Sudāmā begins in poverty and his village is transformed. The one who takes shelter of irreligion shines for a short while, like a firefly, and gains nothing further.
- **When life makes no sense** [48:00]: circumstances will sometimes be impossible to interpret. Even then, Kṛṣṇa remains, the purpose of life remains available, and the destination is reserved.
- **Homework given to the class** [42:00]: a verse from the Tenth Canto (referred to by the teacher as 10.14.8) on the devotee who patiently tolerates the reactions of his own past deeds and offers respects to the Lord — such a person is the rightful claimant to liberation.
- The Bhāgavatam's statement by Ṛṣabhadeva is also cited: this is the austerity by which existence is purified and by which one attains unending spiritual happiness — tolerance undertaken for _dharma_, not for its own sake.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Tolerance is the beginning, not the goal — its fruit is _amṛtatva_: one who is unmoved by the dualities produced from sense contact becomes eligible for the eternal. [32:00, 44:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.16 — introduction [50:00 – 56:00]

<!-- verse:2.16 -->

**Context.** Introduced at the end of the class; verses 2.17 and 2.18 elaborate it, and the detailed treatment is taken up next.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Na asataḥ vidyate bhāvaḥ_ [50:00]: of the non-existent — the body — there is no endurance.
- _Na abhāvaḥ vidyate sataḥ_ [52:00]: of the eternal there is no cessation.
- _Tattva-darśibhiḥ_ [52:00]: by the seers of the truth, who have studied the nature of both.

#### The simple form of the argument [52:00 – 54:00]

- **The body cannot be saved** by anyone — if not the war, then disease, or an accident, or old age.
- **The soul cannot be killed** by anyone.
- Therefore lamentation has no object: one would grieve if something savable were being lost, or if the soul could be destroyed. Neither is the case.
- **The extension** [52:00]: if one's own body cannot be preserved, neither can the bodies of those connected with it — parents, spouse, children — nor the house and property built around them.
- **Vidura's words to Dhṛtarāṣṭra** in the First Canto are cited: when time turns its glance on a person, it takes away what is dearest — life itself — and everything else along with it. The teacher's comparison: a tax raid leaves something behind; time leaves nothing.

#### What the purport adds [54:00 – 56:00]

- There is no endurance for the changing body — even medically, its cells are constantly replaced — while the soul always remains. This is the difference between matter and spirit.
- The distinction between the soul and the Supersoul is also maintained: as energy and the energetic differ, so the living entity is always subordinate, the servant, and the Lord always the master.
- The practical conclusion: this is a place where everything scatters, however much one tries to hold it together — but the soul remains, the Lord remains, and the relationship between them is eternal. That is what deserves work.

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications [56:00 – 72:00]

**Q1 — Hearing some classes leaves one feeling that even after millions of births nothing is achievable. And with family members who may themselves become devotees later, are we not committing offences at every step?** [56:00 – 62:00]

- **On offences to devotees:** yes, in one sense an offence registers even if the person was not yet a devotee — Dhruva Mahārāja's case is the example the questioner herself raised. So care in dealings is needed, because those around us who are attempting devotional service will gradually become advanced devotees.
- **Intention is what makes an offence serious.** Where there is no deliberate ill intent — as in ordinary family dealings — it does not fall into the category of a grave offence, just as there is a difference between a sin and an offence. An offence in the full sense involves a wrong motive, deliberately causing pain.
- **Third, keep practising devotional service.** Once one's account has been placed in the Lord's hands, He manages it — this life's and past lives' reactions alike — and He can reduce it to nothing. The Bhāgavatam's statement is cited that for one who takes shelter of the Lord's lotus feet, the vast ocean of material existence is reduced to the size of the water in a calf's hoofprint.
- The settled practice: remain careful never to offend Vaiṣṇavas intentionally, never to criticise them, and continue in devotional service — the Lord will make the best arrangement.

**Q2 — If facilities are available, what does forced tolerance even mean?** [62:00 – 64:00]

Do not manufacture hardship. The purpose is what matters: if there is an air-conditioner, use it — but use the comfort to chant more rather than to sleep more. And if it fails in the middle of chanting, do not stop chanting. That is the whole point.

**Q3 — How do we tell where our own effort should stop and providence begins?** [64:00 – 68:00]

- The teacher first notes how much the question is a modern one. In earlier times devotional life was simply a way of life — shops opened and closed at fixed hours, villagers gathered in the evening for _kathā_ and _kīrtana_ daily; now even thirty minutes of reading has to be planned and tracked on a card and feels like austerity.
- The practical principle: **do your duty, but do not over-endeavour.** The Nectar of Instruction lists over-endeavour among the six things that destroy devotional service.
- Over-endeavour produces two problems: one becomes over-attached to the result, so success breeds greed and further over-endeavour, while failure produces total dejection. Performing a duty without that over-attachment avoids both.
- **The farmer** sows, and the rain may be excessive or scanty and the harvest large or small — but his devotional practice continues, and it acts as a stabiliser. Without a stabiliser, a fluctuation in voltage destroys the device.
- So fix limits: this much time is reserved for devotional service; beyond that, the Lord will arrange.

**Q4 — How to deal with people who work by an entirely different method from ours without becoming irritated?** [68:00 – 70:00]

**The tennis analogy:** when you are at the serving end you can place the ball where you like and set your conditions; when you are at the receiving end you must respond to where the other person hits it. Dealings in life work the same way — recognise which end you are at.

**Q5 — The purport to 2.16 says this knowledge is clear and easy to understand. Are we above ignorance if we grasp it?** [70:00 – 72:00]

There is a difference between information and knowledge. At present we are receiving information — that we are servants of the Lord. The Fourth Chapter's standard for knowledge is that having attained it one never again falls into illusion. Information becomes knowledge when it is applied: if I am a servant, then I should actually serve.

## Last-Page Revision Sheet

### High-Yield Facts & Terminological Anchors

- **The structure of these three verses:** 2.13 theory, 2.14 practical application, 2.15 result.
- **_Mātrā-sparśāḥ_** — happiness and distress arise from the contact of senses with sense objects, not from the objects themselves; hence the same cold water pleases in summer and pains in winter.
- **Three characteristics of the dualities:** _āgama_ (they come), _apāyinaḥ_ (they go), _anityāḥ_ (they are impermanent). Therefore _titikṣasva_ — tolerate.
- **Three levels of tolerance:** body (heat and cold), mind (favourable and unfavourable), false ego (honour and dishonour).
- **_Kaunteya_ and _Bhārata_** — Arjuna is reminded of two dynasties that tolerated a great deal for _dharma_.
- **Tolerance is not torture; tolerance gives rise to focus.** Purpose is what makes small things stay small.
- **Both pleasure and pain distract** — Jayadratha's celebration is the example.
- **The _dhīra_** does not try to change circumstances but changes himself, and settles his intelligence in the resolve to practise _dharma_ whatever must be tolerated.
- **Result:** _so 'mṛtatvāya kalpate_ — eligibility for liberation.
- **The five creatures destroyed each by one sense** — bee (smell), moth (sight), elephant (touch), deer (hearing), fish (taste); the human being has all five strong at once.
- **BG 2.16:** the perishable cannot be preserved and the eternal cannot be destroyed — so lamentation has no object.
- **Information versus knowledge:** knowledge is information applied; by the Fourth Chapter's standard, after real knowledge one never again falls into illusion.

### Core Analogies & Metaphors

- **Cold water in summer and in winter** — the same contact producing opposite results.
- **The train that halts, and the whole compartment's commentary** — agitation over the unavoidable.
- **Glorification and purification** — walking past praise and past criticism.
- **The driver going to an important meeting; the student going to an examination** — focus that shrinks obstacles.
- **The examination centre nobody looks at** — what a clear goal does to distraction.
- **The wrong turn and the GPS** — take the U-turn, do not argue about the turn.
- **The mind's auto-repeat mode** — a small slight replayed until it becomes large.
- **Someone admitted to intensive care** — how a big thing silences small talk.
- **The air-conditioner, rising early, fasting** — what tolerance does to distress over time.
- **Watching only half the film** — judging the Pāṇḍavas by the dice game, or Sudāmā by his poverty.
- **The firefly** — the brief shine of one who takes shelter of irreligion.
- **The stabiliser** — devotional practice steadying a life of fluctuating voltage.
- **Serving end and receiving end in tennis** — knowing which position one is in.
