## Class Snapshot & One-Line Argument Map

- **Class Snapshot:** The core of the soul section. BG 2.16 states the two truths; 2.17 expands the second — the soul is indestructible and pervades the body by consciousness; 2.18 expands the first — the body is perishable, therefore fight; 2.19 removes the notion of a slayer and a slain; 2.20 lists the six bodily transformations that never touch the soul; and 2.21 asks how one who knows this can kill or cause killing. The class also explains why the same point is repeated so many times.
- **One-Line Argument Map:** Of the body there is no endurance and of the soul no cessation -> the soul pervades the whole body by consciousness and can be destroyed by no one, while the body is perishable and immeasurably outlasted by its owner -> so neither the idea of a slayer nor of a slain is knowledge, since the six transformations that govern the body never occur in the soul -> and therefore one who actually knows this neither kills nor causes anyone to be killed, and Arjuna must fight as an instrument in a war of principle.

## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 2.16 [06:00 – 08:00]

<!-- verse:2.16 -->

**Context.** The verse on which the next several rest: two statements, each of which the following verses take up in turn.

- **_Na asataḥ vidyate bhāvaḥ_** — of the non-existent there is no endurance; the body has no stability, and nobody can preserve it.
- **_Na abhāvaḥ vidyate sataḥ_** — of the eternal there is no cessation; the soul is never destroyed.
- The seers of the truth arrived at this conclusion by studying the nature of both.

The teacher notes the structure explicitly: **2.17 explains the second half** of this verse, and **2.18 explains the first half**.

---

### Bhagavad-gita 2.17 [08:00 – 10:00]

<!-- verse:2.17 -->

**Context.** Expanding "of the eternal there is no cessation": the soul pervades the entire body and cannot be destroyed by anyone.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Avināśi tad viddhi_ [08:00]: know that to be imperishable.
- _Yena sarvam idaṁ tatam_ [08:00]: by which this entire body is pervaded.
- _Avyayasya_ [10:00]: of the imperishable.
- _Na kaścit kartum arhati_ [10:00]: no one whatsoever is able to bring about its destruction.

#### How something so small pervades the whole body [10:00]

- The purport explains that the soul pervades the body **through consciousness**, and that it is by this that pleasure and pain are experienced throughout the body.
- The measurement given in the purport, from the Upaniṣadic verses quoted there: take the tip of a hair, divide it into a hundred parts, take one of those and divide it again into a hundred — the soul is of that magnitude.
- **The sunshine analogy:** the sun occupies one place, yet its light is present everywhere. In the same way the soul is localised and its influence extends throughout the body.

### Bhagavad-gita 2.18 [10:00 – 14:00]

<!-- verse:2.18 -->

**Context.** Expanding "of the non-existent there is no endurance": the body is perishable, the soul immeasurable — therefore, fight.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Antavantaḥ ime dehāḥ_ [12:00]: these bodies are perishable — they have an end.
- _Śarīriṇaḥ_ [14:00]: of the embodied one; the soul is the actual owner and possessor of the body.
- _Anāśinaḥ_ [12:00]: never to be destroyed — the same attribute as _avināśi_ in the previous verse.
- _Aprameyasya_ [12:00]: immeasurable — it cannot be measured by any scale or instrument.
- _Tasmād yudhyasva_ [12:00]: therefore fight.

#### The two-sided argument [12:00 – 14:00]

- Śrīla Prabhupāda's purport states it plainly: the material body is perishable by nature — it may perish immediately or after a hundred years; it is only a question of time, and there is no possibility of maintaining it indefinitely.
- So the conclusion holds from either direction: **the soul cannot be killed**, hence no cause for grief; **the body cannot be saved**, hence no cause for grief.
- As long as the soul is within, the person is considered alive — and the soul, not the body, is the owner of it.
- **A new attribute is added here:** _aprameya_, immeasurable. The previous verse said it cannot be destroyed; this one adds that it cannot even be measured.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The argument is closed on both sides: nothing can destroy the soul and nothing can preserve the body, so lamentation has no object from either direction. [12:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.19 [14:00 – 20:00]

<!-- verse:2.19 -->

**Context.** Neither the one who thinks the living entity a slayer nor the one who thinks it slain is in knowledge.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Ya enaṁ vetti hantāram_ [16:00]: one who thinks this to be the killer.
- _Yaś cainaṁ manyate hatam_ [16:00]: and one who thinks this to be killed.
- _Ubhau tau na vijānītaḥ_ [16:00]: both of them are without knowledge.

#### How the verse must and must not be read [16:00 – 20:00]

- That the soul is not killed is already clear from the previous verses. The half that needs care is that the soul does not kill.
- **The misuse the teacher warns against:** a murderer might quote this verse in court — the Lord Himself said the soul neither kills nor is killed. The answer is that a verse cannot be lifted out of its level and its context.
- **The hen analogy** [18:00]: it would be absurd to cut away the part of a hen that has to be fed while keeping the part that lays eggs; the two go together. Likewise scripture also says that violence must not be committed — one cannot keep half of scripture and discard the other half.
- **The two principles operating here:**
  1. Everything is happening under the superintendence of material nature, which itself works under the Lord's direction.
  2. The Lord is speaking at the level of self-knowledge — distinguishing body from soul. At that level the body is already destined to die and the soul can never die, so the question of who kills whom does not arise in the way Arjuna imagines.
- **The duration of a life is fixed by nature, not by us**, and nature acts under the Lord's superintendence. Arjuna is therefore being urged to a war of principle, not to violence; and as the Lord's instrument he is not the killer.
- Śrīla Prabhupāda's purport closes the point: Arjuna is being engaged on the Lord's order for the establishment of religious principles — which is precisely why no sinful reaction attaches. The verse is not a licence to harm animals or anyone else.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The verse denies both "I am the slayer" and "he is slain" — but only at the level of self-knowledge, and only for one acting on the Lord's order; it is not a general permission for violence. [18:00, 20:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.20 [20:00 – 32:00]

<!-- verse:2.20 -->

**Context.** The famous description of the soul. At first reading it looks repetitive — unborn, eternal, ever-existing — and the commentaries explain why each word is needed.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Kadācit_ [22:00]: at any time — the purport's word-for-word expands this as past, present or future.
- _Ajaḥ_ [22:00]: unborn.
- _Nityaḥ_ [22:00]: eternal.
- _Śāśvataḥ_ [22:00]: permanent.
- _Purāṇaḥ_ [26:00]: the oldest — yet, as the teacher adds, ever fresh.

#### The six transformations of the body [22:00 – 30:00]

Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa's commentary, which Śrīla Prabhupāda also reproduces in the purport, explains that the verse is denying of the soul the six changes that every body undergoes:

1. **_Jāyate_** — it takes birth.
2. **_Asti_** — it remains in existence for some time.
3. **_Vardhate_** — it grows.
4. **_Vipariṇamate_** — it produces by-products, such as offspring.
5. **_Apakṣīyate_** — it dwindles and deteriorates.
6. **_Vinaśyati_** — it is finally destroyed.

Read against that list, nothing in the verse is redundant:

- _Na jāyate_ denies the first — it is never born.
- _Na ayaṁ bhūtvā bhavitā vā na bhūyaḥ_ denies the second — it is not that the soul was non-existent, came into being for a period, and will come into being again.
- _Ajaḥ_ reinforces that there is no birth at all, which is why there is no repetition of birth.
- _Nityaḥ_ and _śāśvataḥ_ deny dwindling and destruction — there is no deterioration in it.
- _Purāṇaḥ_ — it is the oldest, yet no ageing or by-product arises from it, and it remains ever new.

The teacher's summary for revision: **the six changes that take place in the body do not take place in the soul.**

#### Why _ajaḥ_ is not redundant [28:00]

A child in the womb has not yet taken birth, but will. _Ajaḥ_ rules out even that: there was never a stage at which the soul was pending existence. Its existence has no beginning at all.

#### The purport's supports [30:00 – 32:00]

- The Kaṭha Upaniṣad verse quoted there — Yamarāja's instruction to Naciketā — is almost identical to this verse, particularly in its closing lines.
- The soul is full of knowledge and eternally blissful, and the symptom by which we detect it is **consciousness**.
- **The clouded sun** [30:00]: we may not see the soul directly, just as the sun may be hidden behind clouds — but the presence of daylight tells us the sun is there. Consciousness in the body tells us the soul is there.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Nothing in this verse repeats itself: each word denies one of the six transformations that the body must undergo, so the whole nature of matter is systematically excluded from the soul. [24:00, 30:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.21 [32:00 – 38:00]

<!-- verse:2.21 -->

**Context.** The conclusion drawn from the description: one who truly knows the soul in this way neither kills nor causes anyone to be killed.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Veda_ [32:00]: one who knows — and the teacher stresses that this means one actually situated on the platform of the soul, not one who merely knows the theory.
- _Ajam avyayam_ [32:00]: unborn, immutable; it cannot be cut or divided.
- _Kaṁ ghātayati hanti kam_ [32:00]: whom does he cause to be killed, and whom does he kill?

#### What Arjuna is worrying about [32:00 – 36:00]

Arjuna's fear is that the Lord will be the one who causes the killing and he will be the one who kills. The verse answers: one established in knowledge of the soul does neither.

- **The distinction that matters** [34:00]: "one who knows" here does not mean one who has the theory — all of us have the theory. It means one who is actually situated at the level of the soul, the liberated soul, not one identified with the body. Without that distinction the verse can be badly misapplied.
- **The judge and the executioner** [34:00 – 36:00]: a judge orders an execution and an executioner carries it out; both are paid by the government for those respective roles — yet neither incurs the blame of a murderer. The Lord's direction to Arjuna is of that order.
- **The Lord's point to Arjuna** [36:00]: do not think that I am having your teachers killed through you, or that you are the killer; this is a war of principle, and I am impelling you to _dharma_, not to violence.

#### Against the "the Gītā teaches non-violence" reading [36:00 – 38:00]

- Some well-known commentaries argue that the Gītā's purpose is non-violence, and present the Lord as merely goading a reluctant Arjuna.
- Śrīla Prabhupāda's purport opens by establishing the principle instead: everything has its proper use, and one must know where a thing is to be applied — violence included.
- **The examples from the purport:** a judge awarding capital punishment where it is necessary — for if such a man is not stopped, ten others will be killed — and the Manu-saṁhitā's position that a murderer punished in this life is thereby relieved of the reaction that would otherwise follow him, while others are warned by the example.
- **The surgeon** [38:00]: a surgical operation is not performed to kill the patient but to cure him. The Lord's instruction to Arjuna, given in full knowledge of the self, is of that character — and therefore no sinful reaction attaches to it.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Not merely "the soul cannot be killed" but "one who knows does not kill": the Lord is answering Arjuna's fear of becoming a killer, and the judge, the executioner and the surgeon show how an act of principle differs from violence. [34:00, 38:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.22 — introduction [38:00 – 42:00]

<!-- verse:2.22 -->

**Context.** Introduced at the close of the class; taken up in full next time.

- Arjuna's remaining objection: granted that no one kills and no one is killed, these embodied souls — Bhīṣma, Droṇa and the rest — will nevertheless have to give up their bodies, and we shall be the cause.
- The Lord's reply, in the imagery of the verse: as a person gives up worn-out garments and accepts new ones, the soul gives up worn-out bodies and accepts new ones. Where is the loss?
- **The teacher's application** [40:00]: Bhīṣma is an old man in very old clothes; he will receive a new body. Nobody weeps when new clothes are worn — children wait for the festival precisely so they can put on new clothes. The Lord is saying the same thing as before, but now in an engaging, almost conversational way.

## Why the same point is repeated so many times [42:00 – 44:00]

The teacher pauses over an obvious feature of this section: from 2.16 onward, the same two statements — the soul does not die, the body is not saved — recur in verse after verse.

- The repetition is deliberate, because the point being made is not small: it is the single greatest challenge in spiritual life.
- Most of us live in bodily consciousness, and that consciousness is extremely strong; such a concept does not enter on one hearing. So the Lord repeats it until something of it penetrates.
- **The illustration** [42:00 – 44:00]: after hearing Śrīla Prabhupāda repeat "we are not this body, we are the soul", some listeners concluded that _he_ must be special — that he was not a body while they were. And when he said on one occasion "you are not your body", the question came back: "if I am not my body, then whose body am I?"
- Thinking of oneself as something other than the body is simply not easy, which is why the principle has to be stated again and again — and its practical applications are many.

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications [44:00 – 60:00]

**Q1 — When we commit a sin, does the reaction apply to the body or to the soul? The body ends with this life, and karma is carried forward.** [44:00 – 50:00]

- The teacher first recounts a story from a book by a senior devotee — _If Attacked I Will Chant Hare Kṛṣṇa_ — in which the author, drafted into military service, asked his weapons instructor whether they were aiming at the body or at the soul. Told "the body", he pointed out that the body is dead matter and the living one is the soul; told "the soul", he pointed out that the soul cannot be killed. The class was dismissed for the day — and the instructor eventually became his disciple.
- **The answer in principle:** the word _jīva_ means the _embodied_ soul. Because the soul identifies itself with the body, it experiences the consequences carried from one body to the next. For the liberated soul, situated at the level of the self, there is no such effect — which is exactly the distinction drawn in BG 2.21.
- So as long as there is misidentification with the body, new bodies are taken and the account of pious and sinful activity is experienced through them. Śrīla Prabhupāda's illustration is rainwater: pure as it falls, gradually contaminated as it descends.
- The subtle body — the mind with its accumulated impressions and desires for enjoyment — is what necessitates the next body, so that those desires may be fulfilled. Once the living entity is situated in its own constitutional position — to serve the Lord — no further body need be accepted.
- Hence the recurring term in the purports: the **embodied** soul.

**Q2 — Why has the Lord not given us enough sense to accept death as we accept the sunset or the regrowth of vegetables, rather than grieving for years — some people even keep wax statues of departed parents and serve them?** [50:00 – 54:00]

- The capacity **has** been given. The human form is specifically designed with the ability to come to the platform of the self.
- But free will is also given. **The government analogy:** one might as well ask why a government builds prisons instead of only schools — laws exist, and so does the freedom to break them.
- The principle behind the question needs care: the Lord is _acyuta_, He never errs, never fails, and His knowledge is never defeated — the Fourth Chapter says He knows past, present and future. If that is not accepted, scripture itself becomes useless.
- So the right form of the question is not why the Lord did not arrange it, but **why we do not utilise what has been given**. The human body is described as special; study of scripture should be directed at using that capacity properly.
- **Follow-up, on stories where the wrong person is said to have been taken by mistake:** where such a thing appears, no fault attaches to the Lord — the Lord may do such things to expand His pastimes. In Kṛṣṇa's pastimes He steals and He speaks untruth, and behind every such pastime there is a principle to be understood; He remains faultless.

**Q3 — Where does the third gender come from, if creation was arranged in pairs?** [54:00 – 56:00]

- The Third Canto describes how the species came about, but tracing exactly which activity produces which species is extremely difficult — as the Third Chapter of the Gītā says, the intricacies of action are very hard to understand.
- The teacher recounts asking a scholar-devotee about dinosaurs, and the principle given in reply: according to a particular kind of activity a living entity receives a corresponding body, and nature's factory manufactures it accordingly. The general principle can be grasped; the full variety cannot be catalogued by us.

**Q4 — The purport to 2.20 speaks of two kinds of soul. Please explain.** [56:00 – 60:00]

- The purport quotes the Kaṭha Upaniṣad on the two — the individual soul, described as atomic, and the Supersoul, described as all-pervading. Both are present within the same body, and they are distinct.
- **The two birds in one tree:** on a single tree two birds are sitting; one is eating the fruits, the other is simply watching and waiting. The tree is this body; the bird eating the fruits is the individual soul, wishing to taste the varieties of this world; the witnessing bird is the Supersoul, waiting for the living entity to turn towards Him. When the soul does turn and takes shelter, it is freed from the material body.
- The Fifteenth Chapter states the complementary truth: the living entities are the Lord's eternal fragmental parts — not newly made fragments — and the Lord is the origin and seed-giving father of all. Both facts stand together: we are eternally existing, and He is the origin.

## Last-Page Revision Sheet

### High-Yield Facts & Terminological Anchors

- **BG 2.16 is the hinge:** of the non-existent there is no endurance, of the eternal no cessation. **2.17 expands the second half; 2.18 expands the first.**
- **The soul pervades the body by consciousness;** the measurement given in the purport is one ten-thousandth of the tip of a hair. The sun is in one place, its light is everywhere.
- **2.18 adds _aprameya_** — immeasurable — to _anāśī_, indestructible; and the body is _antavanta_, having an end.
- **"It may perish immediately or after a hundred years; it is only a question of time."**
- **2.19 must not be misapplied.** It is spoken at the level of self-knowledge and for one acting on the Lord's order; scripture equally forbids violence, and one cannot keep half of scripture.
- **The six transformations of the body:** _jāyate_, _asti_, _vardhate_, _vipariṇamate_, _apakṣīyate_, _vinaśyati_ — none of which occurs in the soul. That is the whole content of 2.20.
- **_Kadācit_ = at any time — past, present or future.** _Purāṇaḥ_ = the oldest, yet ever fresh.
- **2.21's "one who knows"** means one actually situated on the platform of the soul, not one who knows the theory.
- **Judge, executioner, surgeon** — three illustrations that an act of principle is not violence.
- **Why the repetition:** bodily consciousness is extremely strong, and this concept does not enter on one hearing.
- **_Jīva_ means the embodied soul** — reactions are experienced because of identification with the body, not by the pure soul.
- **Two birds in one tree** — the individual soul enjoying the fruits and the Supersoul witnessing and waiting.

### Core Analogies & Metaphors

- **The sun in one place, its light everywhere** — how an atomic soul pervades a whole body.
- **The hen that must be fed and the eggs it lays** — why half of scripture cannot be kept and half discarded.
- **The judge and the executioner drawing government salaries** — responsibility for an act of law.
- **The surgeon's knife** — an incision that is not an attack.
- **Old clothes exchanged for new** — BG 2.22's image, and the children waiting for new clothes at a festival.
- **"If I am not my body, then whose body am I?"** — why the principle must be repeated.
- **Rainwater pure at its source and contaminated as it descends** — the soul's condition through identification.
- **Prisons built by a government that also builds schools** — capacity and free will together.
- **Two birds on one tree** — _ātmā_ and _Paramātmā_ in one body.
