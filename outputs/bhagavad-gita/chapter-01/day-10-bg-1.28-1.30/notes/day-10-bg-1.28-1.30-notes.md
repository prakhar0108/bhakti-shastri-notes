- **Central questions:** Why does the same person who called them "the evil-minded son's supporters" now call them "my own people"? What is the difference between soft-heartedness and weakness of heart? Why is a compassion that looks admirable actually a problem?

## Continuity & Recap [06:00 – 14:00]

- Recap of the previous section: Arjuna's surprising request, the Lord halting the chariot before Bhishma and Drona, and the gathering of five generations that overwhelmed him in verse 26.
- The teacher notes that what follows is the Lord's own construction of the Gita's setting. Everything was about to begin; He changed it suddenly, which also draws everyone's attention.

#### Direct perception is not reliable [08:00 – 10:00]

- Most people take decisions on the basis of what the senses perceive, but the teacher's formulation is that direct perception has a great deal of capacity to drag a person into illusion.
- His maxim: maya comes through the eyes, Krishna comes through the ears — often, though not always.
- His illustration is a supermarket, where one always buys more than intended, as against the small shop where one asked for the three things one needed and left with them.

#### Krishna as bhakta-vatsala — the humility of the Lord [10:00 – 12:00]

Completing the previous section, the teacher isolates the quality the Lord has just displayed:

- Whatever qualities scripture names, the Lord Himself represents, and one very special one is that He is an extremely humble personality.
- He recounts Bhrigu Muni's test of who is greatest: he failed to show respect to Brahma, entered into verbal conflict with Shiva, and kicked the Lord in the chest in Vaikuntha — whereupon the Lord took hold of his feet and expressed concern that His hard chest might have hurt the sage's tender feet. Tears came to Bhrigu's eyes and he understood who was greatest. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- He adds a modern instance he attributes to a lecture of Radhanath Swami: a very large industrialist dancing alongside his own driver during guru-puja, taken as a sign that the community would certainly flourish, because a person had set aside his false ego to serve. [Names flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Why daily hearing is necessary [12:00 – 14:00]

The teacher's formulation: maya's plan is to confuse us daily, which is why we should hear daily. He observes that when a practitioner's hearing and chanting decline, confusion sets in and material plan-making multiplies — business ideas and investment schemes arrive in proportion as sadhana weakens.

#### Arjuna's five reasons — the CDEFI acronym [14:00 – 16:00]

Four of the five appear in this chapter; the fifth comes at BG 2.6. The Lord answers each of them subsequently.

- **C — Compassion.** Today's section. The teacher notes its answer comes at BG 2.11–2.30, the section on the soul.
- **D — Destruction of family tradition.**
- **E — Enjoyment**, since with everyone killed there is no one to enjoy with.
- **F — Fear of sin.**
- **I — Indecision.** The teacher treats this as characteristic of material analysis generally: we analyse a situation and end up confused, because material problems have no complete solution at the material level.

## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 1.28 [16:00 – 26:00]

<!-- verse:1.28 -->

**Context.** Arjuna begins to speak. Having seen his own people arrayed and eager to fight, his limbs tremble and his mouth dries. [16:00 – 18:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Dṛṣṭvā_: Having seen — the teacher stresses the weight this word carries. [16:00]
- _Svajanam_: One's own people. [16:00 – 18:00]
- _Yuyutsum_: Eager to fight. [18:00 – 20:00]
- _Sīdanti mama gātrāṇi_: My limbs are failing. [18:00]
- _Mukhaṁ ca pariśuṣyati_: And my mouth is drying up. [18:00]

#### The decisive word is "my own" [16:00 – 20:00]

- The teacher contrasts this with what Arjuna said only a few verses earlier — _the evil-minded son of Dhritarashtra_. The same person is now saying _my own people_.
- His point: in life, nearly everything rests on what we accept as ours. Once accepted, a person becomes special, and so do all their connections. If someone accepts Krishna as his own, life becomes successful.
- He illustrates with a reported hospital case of newborns exchanged without the parents knowing, who nonetheless raised the child as their own.
- Duryodhana does not think this way. It is compassion that produces the thought in Arjuna.

#### Why seeing changes everything [16:00 – 18:00]

The teacher's illustration: hearing of a relative's death has one impact; actually going there and seeing the surroundings has a much greater one. Arjuna had known perfectly well who would be fighting, but seeing it was a different matter.

#### These are not ordinary symptoms [20:00 – 22:00]

- Trembling limbs and a drying mouth indicate emotion strong enough to overpower a person.
- The teacher is careful about the cause: Arjuna is not trembling from fear. He is thinking of the consequence — that some of those visible now will not be visible afterwards.
- He notes that Arjuna possesses full spiritual knowledge and had been with Krishna throughout. Knowledge was not the deficiency.

#### The purport: soft-heartedness, not cowardice [22:00 – 24:00]

- Srila Prabhupada writes that a devotee of the Lord naturally possesses the quality of compassion, and that Arjuna, being His devotee, had it.
- Nearly Arjuna's whole extended family was present; though it is not explicitly stated, the purport observes it can be inferred that he was also weeping.
- **The distinction the teacher highlights:** these symptoms were not due to weakness but due to softness of heart. He notes that the purport supports this by citing the description of one who has unalloyed devotion to the Lord and thereby possesses all good qualities. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- He also notes that Srila Prabhupada later characterizes the condition as weakness of heart, and treats that as a development rather than a contradiction.

#### With Krishna but not connected to Krishna [24:00 – 26:00]

- Everything had been planned for a long time; when he arrived, emotions overpowered him.
- The teacher's formulation: Arjuna is with Krishna, but at this point he is not connected to Krishna. He feels more connection with the material relationships, and is disturbed accordingly.
- The Lord arranged this deliberately, and will now turn the battlefield into a classroom — which the teacher treats as the Gita's central practical message: the world is itself like a battlefield, and one is not meant to wait for conditions to settle before beginning devotional life. He lists the usual postponements — career pressure, a family problem, house construction — against the Gita's point that everything material is temporary.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The whole reversal turns on one word. Nothing about the situation changed between verse 23 and verse 28 except what Arjuna accepted as his own — and that reclassification was enough to disable him. [16:00 – 26:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.29 [26:00 – 40:00]

<!-- verse:1.29 -->

**Context.** The symptoms intensify: his whole body trembles, his hair stands on end, his bow slips from his hand, and his skin burns. [26:00 – 28:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Vepathuḥ_ and _roma-harṣaḥ_: Trembling, and hair standing on end. [26:00]
- _Gāṇḍīvaṁ sraṁsate hastāt_: The Gandiva bow slips from his hand. [26:00]
- _Tvak caiva paridahyate_: And the skin is burning. [26:00 – 28:00]

#### Emotional paralysis [28:00]

The teacher's comparison is to paralysis: a physically strong person who suddenly cannot lift anything with the same arm. Emotions can produce the equivalent — a person who knows what to say and cannot say it because emotion has taken over the capacity to act.

#### The significance of the bow slipping [28:00 – 34:00]

This is the part the teacher develops at length, because the detail is not ordinary:

- A warrior's weapon is like a limb of his body; nobody gives up a limb unless it is diseased. A warrior does not let go of his weapon.
- Arjuna had taken a vow concerning the Gandiva: that he would kill anyone who told him to give it up, with certain exceptions.
- **The seventeenth-day incident.** Yudhishthira was badly wounded in an encounter with Karna and withdrew to the camp. Arjuna, hearing of it, left the battlefield to see him. Asked whether he had killed Karna, he said he had not — he had simply come to see him. Yudhishthira, angry, said a great deal, and among it asked what use there was in bearing the Gandiva if Karna had not been killed, and told him to give it to someone else.
- Bound by his vow, Arjuna raised his weapon against Yudhishthira. Krishna intervened, pointing out that there are many ways of killing, and that for a kshatriya dishonour is equivalent to death.
- Arjuna then criticized Yudhishthira — and the teacher notes how much had gone unsaid until then, through the gambling match and everything that followed, which indicates the level of respect Arjuna held for him. Once it began, all of it came out at once.
- Afterwards Arjuna was so ashamed of having dishonoured his elder brother that he turned his sword on himself, having also vowed to kill anyone who insulted his brother. Krishna again intervened: there are ways of killing oneself too, and praising oneself is, according to scripture, equivalent to killing oneself. So Arjuna began to glorify himself, and the situation resolved.
- The teacher's comment on the sequence: people take vows without consulting the Lord, and Krishna can resolve every problem in the entire creation when nobody else can.

#### What the slipping bow represents [38:00 – 40:00]

- Following Baladeva Vidyabhushana: the slipping indicates Arjuna's unsteadiness. A warrior's weapon represents his determination, and here that determination is wavering.
- The teacher's general principle: an outer attack does not affect us as strongly as an inner attack of the emotions. He returns to the saying that wars are won in the general's tent, distinguishing physical fighting from fighting spirit.

#### Two kinds of hair standing on end [36:00 – 38:00]

Srila Prabhupada's purport distinguishes them: one is spiritual, occurring at the stage of devotional emotion, which is a very high level; this one is material.

#### Emotion is not the measure — dedication is [36:00 – 38:00]

The teacher develops a point he treats as important:

- He relates a devotee asking why tears sometimes come while chanting and at other times the mind wanders everywhere.
- His answer: dedication is the common denominator. What are we doing for Krishna, and for His mission?
- **The foundation of any relationship is dedication.** A mother's love for her child is evidenced by rising at midnight to clean up after him, not by the many people who pick up a well-dressed child and make a fuss of him. That is not a demonstration of love; dedication is.
- Applied: emotions should be examined by how much they help us move toward Krishna. Otherwise the whole thing remains theory. He lists the emotions people readily feel — for parents, for country, at a film, at a lost cricket match — as evidence that emotion by itself settles nothing.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The bow slipping is the chapter's most precise symptom: a warrior's weapon stands for his determination, and Arjuna's determination is visibly leaving his hand. Emotion, not opposition, is what disarms him. [26:00 – 40:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.30 [40:00 – 60:00]

<!-- verse:1.30 -->

**Context.** Arjuna says he can no longer stand, his mind is reeling, and he sees only adverse omens. [40:00 – 42:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Na ca śaknomy avasthātum_: I am unable to stand here. [40:00]
- _Bhramatīva ca me manaḥ_: My mind is reeling. [40:00 – 42:00]
- _Nimittāni_: Omens — the teacher notes there was a science of these. [42:00 – 44:00]
- _Viparītāni paśyāmi_: I see the reverse of what should be. [44:00]

#### Nothing is physically wrong [40:00 – 42:00]

The teacher makes the point directly: Arjuna has not sprained an ankle. There is no physical problem whatsoever. The emotions have become strong enough that he cannot handle them.

#### The mind that cannot find its footing [42:00 – 44:00]

- _Bhramati_ indicates that he cannot focus — the teacher's phrase is that he is feeling lost.
- He draws an extended comparison with depression, from cases he says he has seen directly: highly capable graduates with professional experience who, once depression sets in, give up their work and find even simple things impossible, because once the mind goes down it concludes nothing further is possible.
- He notes the characteristic of the mind repeating the same content endlessly, like a damaged cassette, and how difficult trauma in a family is to deal with for the same reason.
- He adds that some who become devotees are saved in such situations, because they at least obtain Krishna's shelter and something positive in life.

#### Reading the omens [44:00 – 46:00]

- Arjuna says every omen appears adverse. The teacher explains that there was a recognized science of omens, and mentions the familiar instances — a cat crossing one's path, or the twitching of a particular side of the body being auspicious for men and for women respectively.
- The application: before beginning something significant, people look for an auspicious start. A warrior entering battle expects indications of success, and favourable omens encourage him.
- His illustration: a cat crosses your path, and then the tyre punctures — after which the day feels wrong.
- _Paśyāmi_ — "I see" — is taken as Arjuna saying he sees into the future: he sees no point in the war, because he anticipates the reverse of whatever is achieved.

#### The purport: attachment destroys equilibrium [46:00 – 48:00]

Srila Prabhupada writes that excessive attachment for material things puts a person in a bewildering condition of existence. The teacher develops it:

- If there is no spiritual element in a person's life at all, the life being lived is unsteady, because the mind cannot be stable when everything it rests on is changing.
- Every material relationship is subject to change — death, disease — and we identify with a body in which anything may happen.
- Connection with the Lord, by contrast, is an unchanging one: He does not age, does not fall ill, never misunderstands us, and never leaves. That connection is what keeps a person mentally stable.

#### Real self-interest [48:00]

- The purport's conclusion, as the teacher reads it, is that a person's real self-interest lies in Vishnu, or Krishna; the conditioned soul forgets this and is therefore forced into material questions. Arjuna concluded that even victory could only be a cause of grief to him.
- The teacher's application: we generally act on the calculation of what benefits us, and when no benefit appears, frustration follows.

#### Why material life is frustrating by nature [48:00 – 52:00]

His formulation: when we experience frustration at the material level, we have to raise our consciousness to the spiritual. Material life is frustrating because three things are absent:

1. **No perfect situation.** Everyone thinks a change of situation will settle matters — a larger vehicle, an owned house instead of a rented one. But whenever we are placed in a material situation, its complexity is revealed after a while. Someone without work has one set of problems; placed in work, he learns what problems work has.
2. **No perfect person.** We expect someone to understand us completely, and no one can, except Krishna.
3. **No unadulterated happiness.** There is no happiness here that lasts, increases, and holds at every time; distress is concealed within it.

#### Where consciousness actually goes [50:00 – 52:00]

- A major portion of our consciousness is occupied with changing or upgrading material life — the teacher's example is house construction absorbing two or three years of a person's attention, after which another material project takes its place.
- What should happen instead is that material life is dealt with while the major portion of consciousness goes to spiritual life — because spiritual life is not predetermined.
- **His governing distinction:** material enjoyment comes according to past action, and cannot be increased by effort, whereas the Lord is obtained by desire. So the one thing that is in our hands is the thing we do not work at, on the assumption that sixteen rounds is already enough.

#### The dead end [52:00 – 56:00]

- Materialism reaches a dead end. His example is a wealthy industrialist with a serious illness, several sons, and a great deal of property, and no idea what to do with it.
- His observation: the conflict over property begins before the parents have even gone, and is certain afterwards. So what has actually been given to the children is conflict — and in any case each person arrives with his own fortune and will receive what is due to him.
- **Loss of purpose** is itself a form of suffering, and the one Arjuna is experiencing. It comes at the last phase of a purely material life, when the material purpose has ended and there is no answer to what comes next.
- He connects this to suicides among students and after relationship breakdowns: the definition of life supplied to them was material, so when that definition fails there is nothing left. He notes that parents often supply the wrong definition.
- Arjuna, by contrast, used the situation to take shelter of Krishna. His conclusion: controlling a material situation is almost impossible, but the option of taking the Lord's shelter is available to everyone, and no one can say it is unavailable to them.

#### What material compassion is [56:00 – 60:00]

Following Vishvanatha Chakravarti Thakura, the teacher notes that _nimitta_ indicates Arjuna contemplating the end result, and being bewildered by it. This is material compassion:

- Externally it looks admirable. Arjuna appears to be renouncing a kingdom so that his relatives will not die.
- But it is material compassion, not spiritual compassion, and the Second Chapter will explain the difference.
- **Its practical form for us:** it is often exactly what prevents devotional practice. Someone stops coming to the temple because parents object; someone withdraws from devotee association because a spouse objects.
- The teacher's principle: anything in this world can be given up for Krishna, but Krishna cannot be given up for anything. He adds that parents who are not directing their children spiritually are themselves in ignorance, and that intelligence has to be used here.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
A compassion that looks like sacrifice can be material compassion, and material compassion is one of the commonest reasons devotional life gets set aside. Anything here may be given up for Krishna; Krishna may not be given up for anything. [40:00 – 60:00]
</Callout>

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications

### 1. What is special about Krishna acting as a charioteer, when every warrior had one? [60:00 – 62:00]

- **Question:** Every chariot had a driver, and every warrior communicated with his own driver somehow. Why is so much made of Krishna receiving Arjuna's foot?
- **Answer:** The teacher grants the premise and relocates the point. The other charioteers occupied that position according to their own background and were doing what was appropriate to it.
- What is remarkable is not the role but who accepted it. His comparison: if a head of state were to become someone's driver, the remarkable thing would not be that the driver obeys the owner — it would be that the head of state had become the driver.
- Krishna is the Lord of innumerable universes and the king of Dvaraka. From every angle His position is extraordinary, and He nevertheless accepted a humble role.
- He adds that this registers properly only when one reads the Bhagavatam — he points to Brahma's prayers and the prayers of the personified Vedas in the Tenth Canto — at which point one understands whose instruction Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva cannot refuse. [References flagged; see Verification Flags.]

### 2. On local residents addressing the Deity familiarly at a holy place [62:00 – 64:00]

- **Question:** A participant relays having heard that local people at a temple address the Lord aloud with complaints about their own affairs, and asks whether that is how it is done.
- **Answer:** The teacher says he has no information about that particular account.
- His general principle: those living in a holy place are there because the Lord has admitted them, and they have their own relationship with Him. We follow the etiquette ourselves, but we do not enter into or comment on that relationship, which is beyond our jurisdiction.

### 3. Is the point that attachment can bewilder even the best of persons? [64:00 – 70:00]

- **Question:** Does Arjuna's bewilderment show that the power of attachment is such that it can bewilder even a personality of his stature, or is this simply a pastime?
- **Answer:** Yes — even the best of persons can be put into a bewildering situation because of attachment. The subject is material compassion, and its solution comes in the Second Chapter.
- **His question back to the class:** how much energy do we put into family and into all of this, and how much into the Lord? He calls this genuinely worth reflecting on.
- On the status of relationships: at the external level these are material relationships. When one becomes a devotee, one spiritualizes them — discharging one's duties within them and trying to connect those people to the Lord — while knowing that one's core relationship is with the Lord, and asking how much energy is going there.
- **Why material things affect us so strongly,** in three parts:
  - They are immediately accessible, essential, and enjoyable. With material relations there are exchanges of feelings and of service.
  - Spiritual things appear remote and non-essential. The Lord has not appeared despite all the chanting; when there is a problem, it is family who come. And the need is not felt as acutely — a quarrel at home stops the meals, whereas a quarrel with the Lord seems to carry no consequence, since He is merciful.
  - Connecting deeply with Krishna requires sacrificing something immediate, and we do not want to pay that price. His measure: at least ten per cent of what is done for the family.
- His conclusion: as the body needs physical nutrition, the soul needs its own, which it receives only on connecting with the Lord. Without dedicating time and energy, there will only ever be excuses, and a relationship will not be established.

### 4. Was Arjuna's treatment of Yudhishthira proper? [70:00 – 72:00]

- **Question:** Both are religious and great personalities, yet Arjuna criticized his brother and became aggressive. Could it have been dealt with better, and if so what was missing?
- **Answer:** The teacher notes it was what Krishna Himself suggested in that situation, and therefore was the best thing.
- His general principle: what is best in any given situation is something we can never fully comprehend; only Krishna knows. This is why the parameters for decisions in life should be guru, sadhu, and shastra, and why the benefit of a decision becomes visible only in the long term.
- His formulation: we see our present and ask for things in the future accordingly; Krishna sees the future and arranges things in the present accordingly. He always knows better.

### 5. How does one distinguish soft-heartedness from weakness of heart in practice? [72:00 – 74:00]

- **Question:** The line seems very fine, and most of the time what we have is weakness of heart.
- **Answer:** The teacher says the distinction is easy to state and hard to act on.
- **Attachment** means it controls you, and you give up dharma in order to display love. His example is Bharata Maharaja becoming attached to the deer and giving up even his chanting.
- **Affection** means you are in control and you act according to dharma.
- Acting on the distinction varies with each person's situation, so what he recommends is working on the positive side: gradually increasing time for Krishna, increasing service, increasing dedication.
- He observes of Srila Prabhupada's early Western disciples that many had broken every regulative principle, and points to the dedication they subsequently showed. The Lord sees surrender. As one practically dedicates, attachment gradually transfers to Krishna — and until one dedicates, the feeling does not come. The feeling comes to those who dedicate.

### 6. Negative interactions keep disturbing the mind — how should that be handled? [74:00 – 78:00]

- **Question:** Some people constantly create difficulty; we avoid it as respectfully as possible, but the mind is still disturbed afterwards and past events resurface. Recovery gets faster with time, but how should one hold the consciousness that there is actually no solution and one should just move forward?
- **Answer:** The teacher notes that thinking is itself affected by the situation, so this is not simply a matter of adopting a thought.
- **The practical answer is to increase positivity**, which he calls the only solution for dealing with negativity: more time discussing and speaking about Krishna, reading, chanting the holy name, and serving.
- Where a close relationship requires listening, listen; where something can be avoided, avoid it; where it can be cut short, cut it short — because little will come of it.
- His recurring recommendation: write a reflection on each completed section and share it with other devotees. He treats this as spiritual sharing that increases positive energy, and as the permanent solution rather than a coping method.

### 7. We emphasize hearing over seeing, yet seeing has such impact [78:00 – 82:00]

- **Question:** Spiritual life focuses on hearing, and seeing is said to be for ordinary people — yet seeing evidently has great impact.
- **Answer:** Direct perception has a great capacity to drag us into illusion, which is precisely why we hear: so that our vision changes.
- **Three kinds of eyes:** the eyes of flesh, the eyes of scripture or knowledge, and the eyes of love. One who only sees and does not hear has an entirely material thought process. Hearing gradually moves a person to scriptural vision, and as love for the Lord develops in the heart, to the eyes of love, which belong to pure devotees.
- Vision nonetheless matters greatly, because whatever we see impacts consciousness deeply. The teacher extends the principle to all the senses, referencing the instruction against over-accumulation in _The Nectar of Instruction_.
- **His practical rule:** if something is neither yours nor to be engaged in Krishna's service, do not look at it for long, because prolonged looking produces attachment. He cites Ajamila, and observes that the entire advertising industry is built on repeated visual exposure producing attachment.
- The general principle he draws: no sense should be over-engaged where there is no need, since all sensory input integrates in the mind.
