## Continuity & Orientation: How to Study Chapter One [02:00 – 14:00]

#### Overview before detail [02:00 – 04:00]

- The teacher distinguishes _viṅgāvalokana_ (overview) from _sīmāvalokana_ (close reading), and insists the overview comes first.
- Without knowing what each section of a chapter contains, close study of individual verses becomes disorienting.
- He mentions Gauranga Darshan Prabhu's _Subodhinī_ as an example of a work that supplies the chapter overview first.

#### Three things to keep in your personal Gita [04:00 – 06:00]

The teacher repeats his earlier request to maintain one marked personal copy, and specifies the minimum it should contain:

1. The breakdown of each chapter, written in.
2. Every word in a verse whose meaning you do not know, underlined — with the corresponding word-for-word meaning highlighted.
3. Whatever you find important in Srila Prabhupada's purport.

#### Breakdown of Chapter One [04:00 – 08:00]

Chapter One contains 46 verses in Srila Prabhupada's edition and functions as the problem definition for the whole book. The teacher's division, noting that different authors divide it slightly differently without any real disagreement:

| Verses | Section                                 | Sub-division                               |
| :----- | :-------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------- |
| 1–27   | Observing the armies (_sainya-darśana_) | 1–11: preparation for war                  |
|        |                                         | 12–20: the signs of victory                |
|        |                                         | 21–27: Krishna as _bhakta-vatsala_         |
| 28–46  | Arjuna's lamentation (_arjuna-viṣāda_)  | The five reasons he does not want to fight |

- On verses 12–20 the teacher notes that the omens already settle the outcome in the first chapter: the Pandavas will win.
- On verses 21–27 he stresses the wonder of it — the Lord of innumerable universes follows Arjuna's instruction to place the chariot where Arjuna asks.

#### Arjuna's five reasons — the CDEFI acronym [06:00 – 08:00]

- **C — Compassion:** He is moved by pity for those before him.
- **D — Destruction of family tradition:** He fears the dynasty's tradition will be destroyed.
- **E — Enjoyment:** With these people killed, how will he enjoy the kingdom?
- **F — Fear of sin:** Killing so many will incur heavy sinful reaction.
- **I — Indecision:** After all his arguments, he ends up confused.

#### How to work with verses 1–11 this week [10:00 – 14:00]

The teacher recites BG 1.1–1.9 with the class and then sets two pieces of homework:

- Read through the verses and underline every word you do not follow; highlight its word-for-word meaning in the purport apparatus. He estimates this takes about five minutes.
- Then recite. Do not set out to memorize — that only creates tension in the mind. Reciting verses 1–11 takes about a minute, so three to five recitations fit into five minutes a day.
- Keep this up for eight to ten days and portions will settle in by themselves. He frames the activity as service to _śabda-brahma_ rather than as a memory exercise, and observes that people who try hardest to cram usually retain least.

#### Where the Gita sits in the Mahabharata, and when Sanjaya spoke [14:00 – 18:00]

- The Gita occupies chapters 25–42 of the Bhishma Parva of the Mahabharata.
- The war lasted eighteen days; Bhishma was commander-in-chief of the Kaurava side for the first ten, and fell — not died — on the bed of arrows. Sanjaya then returned from the battlefield to Dhritarashtra.
- The teacher raises the resulting puzzle: was Sanjaya narrating live, or reporting after ten days had passed? Acharyas differ.
- He cites Madhvacharya's _Mahabharata-tatparya-nirnaya_, a work written to resolve apparent contradictions in the Mahabharata, as holding that there were in effect two Sanjayas — one fighting on the field and one narrating live to Dhritarashtra. [Attribution flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- Whichever position one takes, the settled point is that Sanjaya narrated the Gita to Dhritarashtra, having received the necessary vision by Vyasadeva's mercy — the teacher compares it to a camera that could go anywhere and zoom in at will.

## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 1.1 [18:00 – 34:00]

<!-- verse:1.1 -->

**Context.** Sanjaya has already described both armies. Dhritarashtra now asks what his sons and the sons of Pandu did after assembling. This is the only verse in the entire Gita spoken by Dhritarashtra, and the whole conversation follows from it. [18:00 – 20:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Dharma-kṣetre kuru-kṣetre_: At the field of dharma, which is Kurukshetra. [18:00 – 20:00]
- _Samavetāḥ_: Assembled. [18:00 – 20:00]
- _Yuyutsavaḥ_: Desiring to fight. [18:00 – 20:00]
- _Māmakāḥ pāṇḍavāḥ_: My sons and the sons of Pandu — the teacher treats this pairing as the verse's decisive word. [32:00 – 34:00]
- _Kim akurvata_: What did they do? [18:00 – 20:00]
- _Kṣetra_: A field, as in a field under cultivation. [28:00 – 30:00]

#### Why Kurukshetra is a field of dharma [20:00 – 22:00]

- Scripture describes Kurukshetra as a place of sacrifice even for the demigods. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- King Kuru ploughed the land there with a golden plough and performed sacrifice; many sages performed austerity there.
- Pleased by his sacrifice, Indra was asked to grant that anyone leaving the body there would attain the heavenly realm. Indra considered that too broad and granted it to those who die there while fighting or performing austerity.
- Kurukshetra is also known for _samanta-pañcaka_ — the five ponds associated with Parashurama, who rid the earth of the warrior class twenty-one times and afterwards performed penance, wishing the place to be established as holy. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Why Dhritarashtra asks at all [22:00 – 28:00]

The teacher reconstructs the exchange after Vishvanatha Chakravarti Thakura's commentary:

- Sanjaya could object that the king has already answered his own question — he said they assembled _desiring to fight_, so obviously they would fight.
- Dhritarashtra's reply is that this is a field of dharma, and a holy place exerts an influence. Something might have changed.
- The teacher illustrates the influence of place in both directions: someone who does not drink may feel the urge inside a bar, and someone who never chants may feel like joining kirtana inside a temple.
- His anxiety runs both ways. If Yudhishthira's mind changed, that would trouble him; if Duryodhana's mind changed, that would trouble him equally.
- The reason is that a compromise would settle nothing. Either side could renew hostilities later, so he wants a decisive war that leaves him an unchallenged kingdom.

#### Names carry meaning [24:00 – 26:00]

The teacher explains that in the Vedic period a person's name, form, qualities, and activities tended to correspond:

- **Dhritarashtra** — _dhṛta_, to hold on to; _rāṣṭra_, kingdom. One who clings to the kingdom, and by extension to material name, fame, wealth, and home, no matter the cost.
- **Duryodhana** — a bad fighter, in the sense of one who sets principles aside to win.
- **Duhshasana** — _śāsana_, discipline; one who is bad at disciplining himself.
- **Yudhishthira** — _yudhi_, in battle; _sthira_, steady. One who stays rock-steady on the battlefield of life, because he always follows dharma.

He adds Dhundhukari from the Bhagavatam's glories as another example of a name matching its bearer.

#### Sarasvati speaking through him [28:00 – 32:00]

A second explanation the teacher gives from the acharyas:

- Dhritarashtra knows Krishna is the Supreme Personality of Godhead, and knows the odds.
- Yet the words he chooses announce the verdict: it is a field of _dharma_. The one who upholds dharma is Yudhishthira, the son of Dharma, and the father of dharma is Krishna Himself.
- So the king himself declares, through Sarasvati speaking by his mouth, that his sons cannot win there.
- He proceeds anyway, because the throne remains his as long as the war lasts.

Srila Prabhupada's purport develops the agricultural image:

- A farmer weeds his field so that the intended grain can grow.
- Here Krishna is the farmer, the Kauravas are the weeds to be removed, and the Pandavas are the crop that will flourish.

#### The purport's opening paragraphs [28:00 – 34:00]

The teacher walks through the purport paragraph by paragraph, recommending that readers write a heading above each paragraph where Srila Prabhupada explains a particular word:

- **Paragraph 1** — the special position of the Bhagavad-gita and how it should be studied. The Gita contains what other scriptures contain and much besides; the way to read it is the way Arjuna, its original student, received it.
- **Paragraph 2** — Dhritarashtra's apprehension that one side or the other might compromise.
- **Paragraph 3** — the significance of _dharma-kṣetra_, and separately of _māmakāḥ pāṇḍavāḥ_.

On that last phrase the teacher's comment is blunt: both parties descend from the same dynasty, but the king divides them into _my_ sons and Pandu's sons. Once that mentality appears, war follows.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The opening verse exposes attachment rather than describing a battle. Attachment is the inability to say no whatever the cost, and the very words the attached king chooses announce the outcome he is trying to avoid. [22:00 – 34:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.2 [34:00 – 38:00]

<!-- verse:1.2 -->

**Context.** Sanjaya begins his reply, and begins it with Duryodhana. Seeing the Pandava army drawn up in formation, the king approaches his teacher and speaks. [34:00 – 36:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Dṛṣṭvā_: After seeing. [34:00 – 36:00]
- _Pāṇḍava-anīkam_: The army of the Pandavas — _anīka_ meaning army, rendered in the purport as the soldiers of the Pandavas. [36:00 – 38:00]
- _Vyūḍham_: Arranged in military formation. [36:00 – 38:00]
- _Ācāryam upasaṅgamya_: Approaching the teacher, meaning Dronacharya. [36:00 – 38:00]
- _Rājā_: The king — the teacher notes Duryodhana was not actually king, but had adopted the mentality of one. [36:00 – 38:00]
- _Vacanam abravīt_: Spoke words. [38:00]

#### Why Sanjaya turns the camera to Duryodhana [34:00 – 36:00]

Summarizing the purport:

- Dhritarashtra was blind from birth and, unfortunately, also deprived of spiritual vision.
- He knew his sons were equally blind in matters of religion, and was confident they would never compromise with the Pandavas.
- Sanjaya, knowing the father wants to hear about the son first, begins there.

#### The shock and the calculated speech [36:00 – 38:00]

- Duryodhana had been reckoning on his own eleven divisions against the Pandavas' seven. Seeing how the smaller army was arranged, he was shaken.
- Frightened, he decides the thing to do is approach his teacher.
- The teacher draws attention to the apparent redundancy in "spoke words." Following Baladeva Vidyabhushana, he reads _vacanam_ as marking a few words carrying deep import — a deliberate combination of words, not ordinary speech.
- Duryodhana is presented as a highly expert speaker, politically and diplomatically skilled in the way that makes a politician's real intention impossible to read from what he says.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Fear, not confidence, drives Duryodhana to his teacher. The narrative marks his speech as deliberately crafted, which is the key to reading everything he says next. [34:00 – 38:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.3 [38:00 – 46:00]

<!-- verse:1.3 -->

**Context.** Duryodhana asks Dronacharya to behold the great Pandava army, arranged by Drona's own intelligent disciple, the son of Drupada. Every element of the sentence is chosen to provoke. [38:00 – 42:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Paśya_: Behold — an imperative addressed to the teacher. [40:00 – 42:00]
- _Mahatīm camūm_: The great military force. [40:00 – 42:00]
- _Vyūḍhām_: Arranged, referring to the arrangement of the formation. [40:00 – 42:00]
- _Drupada-putreṇa tava śiṣyeṇa dhīmatā_: By the son of Drupada, your very intelligent disciple. [40:00 – 42:00]

#### The scale of the setting [40:00]

The teacher pauses on what has been assembled: by his account 64 crore participants, and not ordinary people — administrators and warriors gathered from across the world, with no children, women, or elderly among them. He frames the Gita as the greatest lecture ever delivered to an assembly of leaders, arranged by the Lord so that Arjuna would become confused just as the fighting was about to begin. [Figure flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### The Drona–Drupada history behind the provocation [42:00 – 46:00]

The teacher recounts the background that Srila Prabhupada gives in the purport:

- The Pandavas' ball fell into a well; a passing Dronacharya retrieved it by firing arrows that lodged one behind another. The astonished children reported this to Bhishma, who identified him and appointed him their teacher.
- Earlier, Drona and Drupada had studied in the same gurukula — one the son of a poor brahmana, the other the son of a king, because in that system both studied together regardless of wealth. They became friends, and Drupada promised Drona half his kingdom.
- When the impoverished Drona later approached the now-royal Drupada, Drupada did not recognize him. The teacher reads this as a matter of social status: the king found the claim of old friendship humiliating in front of his court, and insulted Drona severely.
- Drona resolved on redress. When the time came for _guru-dakṣiṇā_, he asked that Drupada be brought to him captive. The Kauravas went first and were routed. Arjuna went alone, defeated Drupada's army, and brought him bound.
- Drona's response was to remark that Drupada had forgotten his promise — memory loss happens — and then to return half the kingdom as a gift.
- Drupada in turn resolved on revenge. The teacher's comment: revenge is not a good system, because it simply continues. Drupada performed a sacrifice from which Draupadi and Dhrishtadyumna appeared, Dhrishtadyumna being born for Drona's destruction.
- Drona knew this and trained him anyway, because the boy was qualified.

Duryodhana knows every bit of this. His sentence therefore means: your own disciple has taken your full training and used it to draw up this army against you. The teacher paraphrases the unspoken message as an accusation of foolishness, delivered precisely to provoke anger. The purport's closing point is that Duryodhana pointed out this weakness so Drona would stay alert in battle and not compromise.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
A single sentence can be a weapon. Duryodhana selects the one fact that will sting his teacher most, not to inform him but to guarantee he fights without hesitation. [38:00 – 46:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.4 – 1.6 [46:00 – 52:00]

<!-- verse:1.4 -->

<!-- verse:1.5 -->

<!-- verse:1.6 -->

**Context.** Duryodhana continues by naming the Pandava commanders — heroes, mighty bowmen, and great chariot fighters. [46:00 – 52:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Atra śūrāḥ_: Here are heroes. [46:00 – 48:00]
- _Maheṣv-āsāḥ_: Great bowmen — those who wield a mighty bow. [46:00 – 48:00]
- _Bhīmārjuna-samā yudhi_: Equal to Bhima and Arjuna in fighting. [46:00 – 48:00]
- _Mahā-ratha_: The teacher gives the graded classification as _rathī_, _atirathī_, and _mahā-rathī_ — one who fights from a single chariot, one who can fight many thousands, and one described as able to fight ten thousand warriors alone. He attributes the definitions to Baladeva Vidyabhushana and Vishvanatha Chakravarti Thakura. [48:00 – 50:00]
- _Nara-puṅgavaḥ_: A hero among men. [50:00 – 52:00]
- _Saubhadraḥ_ and _draupadeyāḥ_: The son of Subhadra — Abhimanyu — and the sons of Draupadi. [50:00 – 52:00]
- _Ca_: And — used, the teacher notes, because not everyone could be named; Ghatotkacha and many others are covered by it. [48:00 – 50:00]

#### Why Bhima is named first [48:00]

- Arjuna is the principal fighter, but Bhima is the one lodged in Duryodhana's mind, and what occupies the mind comes out in speech.
- The teacher lists the vows that made Bhima fearsome: to kill all of Dhritarashtra's sons, to break Duryodhana's thigh, and to tear open Duhshasana's chest.
- He adds that Bhima fought so ferociously that even those on the Pandava side found it frightening, and that Duryodhana had taken a good deal of physical punishment from him since childhood.

#### Names with histories behind them [50:00 – 52:00]

The teacher's summary of the name lists: there are names in history, and there is history behind names. Scripture names a great many kings, elaborating the history of some — such as Dhruva Maharaja, a pure devotee — and leaving others as names that the original audience would have recognized.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The catalogue of warriors is not filler. Which name comes first, and which are grouped under "and others," reveals the speaker's fear and his sense of proportion. [46:00 – 52:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.7 – 1.8 [52:00 – 60:00]

<!-- verse:1.7 -->

<!-- verse:1.8 -->

**Context.** Duryodhana realizes he has praised the opposing army too well, and turns to his own commanders to repair the damage. [52:00 – 54:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Asmākaṁ tu viśiṣṭāḥ_: But our especially powerful ones. [52:00 – 54:00]
- _Tān nibodha_: Take note of them — the teacher contrasts this with _paśya_ ("behold") used for the enemy: for his own side, mere information suffices, since the names alone are enough. [52:00 – 54:00]
- _Dvijottama_: O best of the brahmanas, addressed to Drona. [52:00 – 54:00]
- _Nāyakāḥ mama sainyasya_: The captains of my army. [52:00 – 54:00]
- _Samitiṁ-jayaḥ_: Always victorious in battle — the teacher glosses it as never having been defeated in any engagement. [56:00 – 58:00]

#### A calculation about who to name first [54:00 – 56:00]

- Duryodhana is actually the one who is afraid, and the teacher offers a principle: the world is a mirror reflecting your own image, so a frightened man assumes everyone else is frightened.
- He weighs whether to name Bhishma first as commander-in-chief, then decides to name Dronacharya first because Drona is a brahmana and might take offence at being placed second, whereas Bhishma is stable and would not mind.
- The teacher's reading is that the stable people were never going to react; it is Duryodhana who is unstable. He generalizes: one who tries to satisfy everyone ends up satisfying no one.

#### Why Karna is named when he is not on the field [56:00 – 60:00]

The teacher explains the backstory:

- Before the war, Bhishma assessed each warrior as _rathī_, _atirathī_, or _mahā-rathī_. When Karna's turn came, Bhishma pointed out that his armour and earrings were gone, given away in charity, and rated him accordingly at half strength.
- Karna took this as a grave insult and vowed not to enter the battlefield as long as Bhishma remained commander.
- His stated reason was credit: if he won, the commander would receive it, the way a winning cricket team's captain is remembered.
- A quarrel followed, and Duryodhana had to pacify both. He reasoned that appointing Karna as commander would keep Bhishma away permanently, and Bhishma was the most experienced and powerful; whereas if Bhishma fell, Karna could be persuaded to return.
- Hence the teacher's formulation: Karna is not on the battlefield, but Karna is in Duryodhana's mind — which is why the name appears.

#### Why Vikarna is included [60:00]

- Vikarna is an ordinary warrior, not comparable to the others named. The teacher illustrates the mismatch with a forest sign warning of tigers and leopards — one does not add a warning about dogs to that list.
- Vikarna is included because he had objected during the attempted disrobing of Draupadi, and Duryodhana wants him pacified rather than speaking up again.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Every name in Duryodhana's own list is placed for effect — seniority managed, an absent ally invoked, a potential dissenter flattered. Calculation of this kind is the mark of a mind that is not steady. [52:00 – 60:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.9 [60:00 – 64:00]

<!-- verse:1.9 -->

**Context.** Having named only a few, Duryodhana adds a general verse so that the rest of his army is not discouraged. [60:00 – 62:00]

#### Sarasvati speaks again [62:00 – 64:00]

- The acharyas note that Sarasvati has settled on Duryodhana's tongue as well: in saying they have _given up_ their lives for his sake, he states more than he means — none of them will escape.
- Srila Prabhupada's purport makes the same point, that it is a foregone conclusion they will be killed, having joined the side of sinful Duryodhana.
- The teacher notes that Duryodhana has managed to cover Bhishma, Drona, Kripa and the rest with great calculation in two verses — and that all of it will be corrected in the Eleventh Chapter, when the universal form shows these same warriors already destroyed. - His closing remark on the chapter: technically there is not much philosophy yet; Chapter One is largely description, and the argument develops gradually from here.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Duryodhana's encouraging verse is also his unintended epitaph for his own army. The narrative lets the speaker's own words pronounce the verdict twice — once through Dhritarashtra, once through his son. [60:00 – 64:00]

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications

### 1. Devotees also try to please everyone — how is that different? [64:00 – 66:00]

- **Question:** If one who tries to please all pleases none, how should this be understood when devotees also try to please everyone?
- **Answer:** The difference is the centre. A materialist wanting to please everyone is essentially trying to please himself and protect an image; attachment to anyone in this world rests on attachment to oneself.
- A devotee tries to please others by keeping Krishna at the centre, seeing each person in connection with Krishna.
- Because of that understanding, such effort actually works to some degree. Where Krishna is pleased, the result manifests; otherwise people cannot really be satisfied however hard one tries.

### 2. Was Bhima's desire for retribution a fault? [66:00 – 72:00]

- **Question:** Bhima carried his vows of revenge through the entire war, yet he prospered and is said to have attained the heavenly realm. Where was that corrected?
- **Answer:** The teacher makes two points.
- First, on the honesty of scripture: it does not hide things. Perfection belongs to Krishna; others are not perfect, each has a different nature, and weaknesses may appear at different times. What matters is the prominent characteristic — the Pandavas were surrendered to Krishna and acted under His direction, wanting only to please Him.
- Second, on the legality of the response: scripture lists six kinds of aggressor who may be met with force — among them one who sets fire to your house, one who attacks you with weapons, one who seizes your property, one who molests your wife, and one who drives you from your kingdom. The teacher notes the Kauravas committed all six against the Pandavas. Anger used in this way, in the Lord's service and against those who oppose devotees, is the proper use of anger; he compares it to Hanuman. [List and verse reference flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- A participant asked whether BG 9.30 — on the devotee of bad conduct still being considered saintly — applies here. The teacher's answer was that it does not: _durācāra_ means abominable action, whereas fighting and becoming angry in battle is natural and proper for a kshatriya, not misconduct.

### 3. How should Dronacharya have responded to the insult? [72:00 – 79:24]

- **Question:** If revenge only perpetuates itself, what should Dronacharya have done when publicly humiliated?
- **Answer:** The teacher first grants the difficulty. A sense of injustice goes very deep in every living being and cannot simply be negated; escaping it requires serious internal work.
- Without positive work, the feeling consumes the person. The mind keeps up a constant negative commentary, which he compares to a damaged cassette looping the same phrase endlessly.
- He notes that psychiatrists who absorb so much negativity themselves need support, as an illustration of how draining constant negative input is.
- An unhealthy reaction draws an unhealthy reaction in return, and the wrong cycle continues.
- As the contrasting example he relates the incident of Ramachandra Puri criticizing Chaitanya Mahaprabhu — claiming that ants outside the room proved sweets were being eaten inside. Other devotees became angry; Chaitanya Mahaprabhu took it positively, described him as a well-wisher, and reduced His own diet. Two things were achieved: further negativity did not spread, and He improved Himself. [Account flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- The teacher anticipates the objection that Chaitanya Mahaprabhu is the Lord, and answers that the point stands: acting positively in such a situation requires very high internal strength.
- Hence the practical conclusion — regular _sādhana_ of reading, hearing, and chanting is what builds the capacity. Without spiritual capacity, acting on that level is simply not practical, and the grievance keeps running in the mind.
- He closes with scriptural examples of people who took injustice positively — Dhruva Maharaja, Vidura — noting that as capacity grows, one minds such things less.
