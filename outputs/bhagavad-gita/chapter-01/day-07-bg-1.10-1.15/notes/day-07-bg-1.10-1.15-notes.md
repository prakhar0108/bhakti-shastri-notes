## Continuity & Method: How to Study a Section [04:00 – 14:00]

#### Why the Gita carries the most weight of the four course books [04:00 – 06:00]

- Of the four books Srila Prabhupada gave for this course, the teacher singles out the Gita. _The Nectar of Instruction_ has eleven verses and _Śrī Īśopaniṣad_ around twenty, so both are short; _The Nectar of Devotion_ is a summary study rather than a verse-by-verse treatment.
- The Gita is therefore the one book that, studied properly, helps at three levels: one's own practical application, clearing up doctrinal confusion for others, and refining the guidance one gives.

#### The study sequence [06:00 – 08:00]

The teacher repeats his method and asks the class to implement it now rather than later:

1. Read alongside whatever has been covered in class; do not run ahead.
2. Afterwards, listen to Srila Prabhupada's lectures on that portion, or read the transcriptions, which he notes are available on Vedabase.
3. Write your own reflection on each section, in your own words, in paragraph form, and keep it. When you later study or teach that section, the reflection will help.

#### Working the verses [06:00 – 08:00]

- Read the verses aloud.
- Underline any word you do not follow, then look up its word-for-word meaning.
- Repeat the section three times a day. Ten or eleven verses take about two minutes to recite, so three repetitions cost roughly six minutes daily.
- Since there are usually only two classes a week, each section gets five or six days of this treatment, after which the verses settle without much effort at remembering.
- When moving on, simply listen to a recording of the previous section; having already repeated it five or six times, that is enough to keep it fresh.

#### Two kinds of looking [08:00 – 10:00]

- _Vihaṅgāvalokana_ — the bird's-eye overview. For Chapter One: 46 verses in four sections — 1–11 preparation for war, 12–20 the signs of victory, 21–27 Krishna as _bhakta-vatsala_, 28–46 Arjuna's five reasons for not fighting.
- _Sīmāvalokana_ — the teacher's image is a lion that turns and looks back after making its kill, checking that nothing has been left. Revision works the same way: looking back over what has been covered clarifies where the present passage connects from.

#### Recap of BG 1.1–1.9 [10:00 – 14:00]

- Dhritarashtra asks his one question; Sanjaya begins narrating, zooming his camera from Hastinapura to Kurukshetra.
- Duryodhana, shaken by the Pandava formation, approaches Drona and speaks deliberately chosen words, crediting the formation to Drona's own disciple so that his teacher will fight without holding back.
- Verses 4–6 are almost entirely names of powerful warriors on the Pandava side.
- Because Drona faces the Pandava army while the Kaurava army stands behind him, Duryodhana — worried that he has glorified the enemy too well — turns to his own commanders from verse 7.

## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 1.10 [18:00 – 26:00]

<!-- verse:1.10 -->

**Context.** After nine verses of description, Duryodhana states his conclusion: a comparative estimate of the two forces. The teacher treats this verse as the most instructive in the section, because the acharyas read its key word in two opposite ways. [18:00 – 20:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Aparyāptam_: Srila Prabhupada renders it as immeasurable. [18:00 – 20:00]
- _Paryāptam_: Srila Prabhupada renders it as limited. [18:00 – 20:00]
- _Bhīṣmābhirakṣitam_ / _bhīmābhirakṣitam_: Protected by Bhishma; protected by Bhima. [18:00 – 20:00]

#### Duryodhana's reading [18:00 – 20:00]

- Our strength is unlimited because Bhishma is commander-in-chief; theirs is limited because it rests on Bhima.
- The teacher notes Bhishma's standing: to that day his record held no defeat and no draw.
- Srila Prabhupada's purport opens by identifying exactly this — an estimation of comparative strength.

#### The opposite reading [20:00 – 22:00]

- Ramanujacharya, Vishvanatha Chakravarti Thakura, and Sridhara Swami take _aparyāptam_ in its other sense: insufficient.
- The teacher connects this to the pattern already seen in verse 9 — Sarasvati seated on Duryodhana's tongue, making him state the future rather than his intention.
- The reason the Kaurava force is insufficient is that Krishna is on the other side.
- He notes that Srila Prabhupada and Baladeva Vidyabhushana both take the word as immeasurable, so the two readings coexist in the tradition.

#### One word encouraging two people [22:00]

Some acharyas, the teacher says, credit Duryodhana with enough diplomatic skill to intend both senses at once:

- "Immeasurable" encourages Bhishma — it expresses full confidence that his command will win the war.
- "Insufficient" encourages Drona — a warning not to be casual, since Bhima stands on the other side and Drona may have to carry the fighting himself.

He draws the governing principle from this: the calibre of speech lies not only in the expertise of the speaking but also in the motive behind it. Duryodhana speaks warmly and cleverly, but his purpose is impure.

#### Why the stronger army is the weaker one [22:00 – 26:00]

- Bhishma is supremely powerful, but he is a devotee of Krishna and loves the Pandavas. Before the war he had already blessed Yudhishthira with victory.
- The Pandava army, by contrast, is united, and Bhima's anger is at its peak. Yudhishthira had repeatedly brought peace proposals and philosophy where Bhima wanted action, so Bhima had been given little opportunity until now and will not hold back a single per cent.
- Krishna will not take up a weapon, but He is present on the field as Arjuna's charioteer, and so will play some role.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The same word carries opposite meanings in the commentaries, and both are instructive: Duryodhana measures what can be measured, while the tradition hears the verdict he is unknowingly pronouncing. Eloquence is judged by motive, not by skill. [18:00 – 26:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.11 [26:00 – 36:00]

<!-- verse:1.11 -->

**Context.** Still uneasy, Duryodhana instructs every commander to hold his assigned position and give Bhishma support from the flanks. [26:00 – 28:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Anīkeṣu_: In the strategic positions — the Hindi edition's rendering is the fronts or posts. [26:00]
- _Sarveṣu yathā-bhāgam avasthitāḥ_: All of you, stationed in your respective allotted places. [26:00 – 28:00]
- _Bhīṣmam evābhirakṣantu_: Give support to Bhishma. [28:00]

#### Why position matters [26:00 – 28:00]

- The teacher stresses that in warfare the strategic position is decisive, and suggests anyone with military experience would confirm it.
- His examples are the Kargil conflict, where the opposing force had captured commanding heights, and the forts of earlier times, which existed precisely to supply a strategic position to fight from.
- Duryodhana's instruction is therefore that nobody drift from his post — not one man slack, nobody wandering to another's sector on the assumption that Bhishma will handle everything.

#### The purport, and a calculation about silence [28:00 – 32:00]

- Srila Prabhupada writes that Duryodhana wanted everyone alert so that if Bhishma became engaged with one opponent, the enemy could not take advantage of the opening.
- The teacher adds Duryodhana's grounds for confidence that Bhishma and Drona will support him: both were silent during the attempted disrobing of Draupadi, and neither opposed him then.
- He recounts the sabha episode — Yudhishthira losing the kingdom, the wealth, his brothers, and himself, and then staking Draupadi; Draupadi's question to the assembly as to how a man who has already lost himself can stake another; the silence; and finally Bhishma's answer that both propositions are true and that he is confused on the point of dharma.
- The teacher flags the difficulty himself: Bhishma is among the twelve _mahājanas_ said to know the truth of dharma, so he could not genuinely be confused. He treats the episode as part of the Lord's arrangement. [Reference flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Two things Duryodhana is doing [32:00 – 34:00]

- Convincing every warrior that he is valued. The teacher generalizes: team members need to know they have a role, rather than feeling they are present only to applaud.
- Simultaneously telling Bhishma that he holds the central leading position, and keeping Drona managed as well.

He characterizes the technique as a net of words, and contrasts it with Srila Prabhupada's writing, which he describes as direct and practical rather than elaborate.

#### The missing factor [32:00 – 36:00]

This is the teacher's principal point in the class:

- Duryodhana has counted everyone — Bhishma, Karna, Vikarna, the son of Somadatta, and on the other side Arjuna and Bhima.
- He never mentions Krishna. The Krishna factor is left entirely outside the calculation.
- The reason is that he is assessing the situation on purely material terms — who is strong, who has what capacity — and since Krishna will not take up a weapon, Duryodhana treats Him as a non-entity whose presence or absence is irrelevant.
- The teacher's generalization: this is the life of a materialist, who manipulates a great deal but misses the one who matters most. His illustrations are negotiating over a fare or over the price of vegetables — enormous attention to small calculations, while the Lord is never counted as a factor at all, and neither one's time nor one's wealth nor one's energy is directed toward Him.
- He also reads the whole speech as an indication of fear: a warrior who talks this much on the battlefield is betraying that he is afraid.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Meticulous calculation is not the same as accurate calculation. Duryodhana accounts for every measurable asset and omits the only decisive one, which is exactly how material planning tends to proceed. [26:00 – 36:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.12 [36:00 – 42:00]

<!-- verse:1.12 -->

**Context.** Bhishma responds — not with words, but by blowing his conchshell like a roaring lion, gladdening Duryodhana. [36:00 – 38:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Kuru-vṛddhaḥ_: The senior-most among the Kurus. The teacher notes _vṛddha_ carries two senses — advanced in years, and advanced in knowledge, so Bhishma is _jñāna-vṛddha_ as well. [38:00]
- _Pratāpavān_: Powerful, glorious, and famous. [38:00]
- _Pitāmahaḥ_: The grandsire. [38:00]
- _Siṁha-nādam_: A sound like a lion's roar. [36:00 – 38:00]

#### Why Bhishma is described this way [38:00 – 40:00]

The teacher lists the grounds for Bhishma's fame, all of which the assembled warriors would have known:

- He is the son of Ganga.
- His encounter with Parashurama lasted twenty-seven days, and Parashurama had rid the earth of the warrior class twenty-one times. Bhishma was not defeated.
- He had defeated an assembly of kings at the svayamvara on his family's behalf. [Names as captioned; see Verification Flags.]

On _jñāna-vṛddha_ he points forward to the Bhagavatam's First Canto, ninth chapter: after Bhishma fell on the tenth day and the war ended, Krishna took the lamenting Yudhishthira to him, telling him to ask whatever he wished about the principles of dharma, because the sun of scriptural knowledge was about to set.

#### What the conchshell said [40:00 – 42:00]

- The literal sense of the verse is that Bhishma gladdened Duryodhana.
- Baladeva Vidyabhushana's reading, as the teacher presents it, is that Bhishma answered the whole speech without a single word. He blew the conch and nothing else.
- What that silence conveyed: I will give my life according to the duty of a kshatriya, but victory and defeat rest entirely with the Lord, and He is standing on the other side. Your calculations are beside the point.
- The teacher notes that kshatriyas speak little and act more, which is itself part of the answer.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Bhishma's one sound is a complete reply to Duryodhana's long calculation: duty is mine to perform, the result is not mine to arrange, and the arranger is on the other side. [36:00 – 42:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.13 [42:00 – 46:00]

<!-- verse:1.13 -->

**Context.** Bhishma's conch is answered at once by every instrument in the Kaurava army, producing an uproar. [42:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Sahasā eva_: All of a sudden. [42:00 – 44:00]
- _Abhyahanyanta_: Simultaneously sounded. [42:00 – 44:00]
- _Tumulaḥ_: The teacher points out the Sanskrit is almost the English word tumultuous. [44:00]
- The instruments named include conchshells, large drums, smaller drums, and horns. **Homework set in class:** count how many instruments this verse actually describes. [42:00 – 44:00]

#### Creating the right atmosphere [44:00 – 46:00]

- In earlier times every activity had its prescribed procedure, and war was conducted with something of the character of a celebration, since it was fought for the establishment of dharma.
- The teacher compares the ceremony preceding a cricket World Cup — a different kind of celebration, but the same underlying principle.
- That principle is to create the atmosphere appropriate to the event, because an activity undertaken in a fitting atmosphere goes well.
- He extends it to kirtana, where different tunes suit different occasions, and tells the story of a brahmachari with little musical background sent to lead kirtana at a funeral, who used a joyful dancing tune he happened to know. The tune should match the occasion.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The sounding of the instruments is not scene-setting for its own sake. Deliberately establishing the atmosphere an activity requires is treated as part of doing the activity properly. [42:00 – 46:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.14 [46:00 – 56:00]

<!-- verse:1.14 -->

**Context.** The camera turns. Sanjaya has been describing the Kaurava side for Dhritarashtra; now he moves to the other army, and Krishna enters the Bhagavad-gita. [46:00 – 48:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Śvetair hayaiḥ_: With white horses. [48:00]
- _Mahati syandane_: On a great chariot. [48:00 – 50:00]
- _Mādhavaḥ_: _mā_ is Lakshmi and _dhava_ is husband, so the husband of the goddess of fortune. [48:00 – 50:00]
- _Pāṇḍavaḥ_: Here a name of Arjuna, not the five brothers collectively. [48:00 – 50:00]
- _Divyau śaṅkhau_: Transcendental conchshells. [48:00 – 50:00]

#### The manner of the entry [46:00 – 48:00]

- The teacher compares it to the entrance of a film hero, where the camera does not go straight to the face: first the vehicle arrives, then the camera moves up from the shoes.
- He connects this to how one should take darshan — beginning at the Lord's feet and moving upward with prayer, rather than looking directly at the face, which he presents as both a mark of respect and something one can verify in one's own experience.
- Sanjaya moves the same way: the horses first, then the chariot, then the personalities seated on it.

#### What the description establishes [50:00 – 52:00]

Each element is extraordinary, which is why it is described at all:

- **The horses** were among a hundred divine horses given to Arjuna by a Gandharva named Chitraratha. Their peculiarity was that the count never fell below a hundred however many were killed, and they could travel anywhere, including the heavenly regions. Five were selected from among them. The teacher contrasts them with ordinary hill-station horses that sometimes stumble and bring down their rider.
- **The chariot** was given by Agnideva, was overlaid with gold, carried a flag, and could store as much weaponry as nine bullock carts. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- **The personalities** are the husband of the goddess of fortune, and Arjuna.

#### The point of the reversal [52:00 – 54:00]

The teacher marks the difference between this and the film-hero image he has just used:

- A hero steps out of a vehicle that someone else is driving. Here the Lord is the driver, and Arjuna is the principal figure.
- The principle he draws: real heroism is being the servant of the servant of the servant, and this is how Krishna chooses to appear.
- He notes that whatever the Lord instructs in the Gita, He has Himself done. Having taught that serving His devotees is greater than serving Him directly, He demonstrates it.
- The visual he draws out: Krishna as Shyamasundara, like a dark monsoon cloud, with Arjuna's shining complexion beside Him like lightning within the cloud.

#### The chariot in our own case [54:00]

Referring to the Upanishadic comparison of the body to a chariot, the teacher applies it:

- The Lord is present in this body as well and never leaves us, however the outer world and the inner mind behave, and whether we win or lose.
- He has a plan for us. The difficulty is that we snatch the reins back and do not let Him drive.

#### The purport: sure success [54:00 – 56:00]

The teacher gives this purport the heading "sure success" and treats it as important:

- Krishna's and Arjuna's conchshells are specifically called transcendental, in contrast with the ones Bhishma and his side sounded.
- That indicated there was no hope of victory for the other side, because Krishna was with the Pandavas.
- He cites the statement that victory belongs to the sons of Pandu, on whose side is Janardana.
- Where Vishnu is, the goddess of fortune is, since she does not remain apart from her husband. So the sound of that conch announced that both victory and fortune awaited Arjuna.
- Following Baladeva Vidyabhushana, the teacher notes that of all the warriors standing on chariots, only these two are mentioned — because the charioteer is the driver of the entire universe, the warrior is extraordinary, the chariot is extraordinary, and the horses are extraordinary. Everything about this one chariot is special, which is why it alone receives the detailed description.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The outcome is announced before a weapon is raised. The Lord enters His own book in the position of a driver, establishing that real greatness lies in service rather than in the lead role. [46:00 – 56:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.15 [56:00 – 60:00]

<!-- verse:1.15 -->

**Context.** The conches of Krishna, Arjuna, and Bhima are named individually. [56:00 – 58:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Hṛṣīkeśaḥ_: _hṛṣīka_ means the senses and _īśa_ means master, so the master of the senses. [58:00]
- _Dhanañjayaḥ_: Conqueror of wealth. [58:00 – 60:00]
- _Bhīma-karmā_ and _vṛkodara_: Epithets of Bhima, explained in the purport. [60:00]

#### Hrishikesha and the ownership of the senses [58:00 – 60:00]

- Since the Lord is the master of the senses, the purpose of our senses is His service. We are not their owners.
- The teacher's illustration: a company car is given for the company's work, and the person who drives it is a driver, not the owner — he is not entitled to take it wherever he likes.
- Srila Prabhupada's purport notes that the Lord is in everyone's heart, but that in the case of a pure devotee He directly controls the senses, because such a devotee keeps them always engaged in His service.
- The teacher's extension: the Lord wants to drive everyone's chariot, but He does not force; Arjuna handed Him the reins, and we do not. As the senses are engaged in service they become purified, then attracted to the Lord, and devotional practice becomes relishable; impure senses make the same practice a struggle.

#### Why Arjuna is called conqueror of wealth [60:00]

- The first sense is material: Arjuna gathered enormous wealth for Yudhishthira's Rajasuya sacrifice.
- The teacher adds a second sense — conqueror of spiritual wealth — pointing to the blessings and weapons Arjuna accumulated from Shiva, from Indra, and from others. He was rich in both.

The teacher notes that Srila Prabhupada uses two modes in the purports — sometimes explaining a word from the verse, sometimes developing a theme — and recommends writing the word being explained above the relevant paragraph.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The name Hrishikesha states the whole relationship in one word: the senses belong to their master, and a devotee's practice is the process of handing them back. [56:00 – 60:00]
</Callout>

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications

### 1. How could Bhishma, one of the twelve mahajanas, fail to decide rightly in Draupadi's case? [60:00 – 64:00]

- **Question:** It was an obviously wrong situation. Was it Krishna's will that the war should happen, or was there some other reason he could not take the right decision?
- **Answer:** The internal reason is that it was Krishna's will — events had to unfold in a particular way, and but for that arrangement Bhishma would not have been confused.
- The external reasons Bhishma himself gives — being bound to those whose wealth he has accepted — the teacher treats partly as scriptural argument pitched at a particular level and partly as Bhishma's humility, which is also why he says he must fight on that side.
- He adds that the Draupadi episode was a principal instance through which the Lord intended to teach something important.

### 2. Is a desire for retribution ever right for us, and how should injustice be handled? [64:00 – 70:00]

- **Question:** We cannot compare ourselves to Bhima, but similar feelings arise in our own lives. Is that right, and how should one avoid it?
- **Answer:** Bhima used his anger constructively, against those who were acting irreligiously. For ourselves the teacher gives a sequence:
- **Accept the situation first.** Most people go on fighting a war they have already lost — an old abuse, a death in the family, a relationship that ended — and simply live inside that situation because they cannot accept it.
- **Remember that life never stops.** The Lord closes one door and opens a hundred.
- **Take whatever practical action is available** — this is the level of _vyavahāra_.
- **Protect yourself from being internally affected.** A situation affects one inwardly as well as outwardly, so dealing with the negativity requires being strongly connected to something positive: reading biographies of positive personalities, prayer, staying connected with devotees. Endless discussion and analysis of the wrong done yields very little benefit.
- **Use the situation to take shelter.** Any circumstance whatever can be used as an occasion to take shelter of the Lord, and that is the question worth asking.
- **Do not let revenge grow.** His practical instrument is to pray for the person one finds difficult, and to chant a round for them.
- He illustrates acceptance with Rama, who was told of the exile on the day the coronation was announced — a punishment he describes as one level below execution — and who accepted immediately, not mounting his chariot or wearing his royal footwear, and told his mother that his father had given him the kingdom of the forest.
- He illustrates praying for a difficult person with the hunter and the rabbit: the rabbit, instead of running, approaches the hunter and licks his feet, and the hunter cannot kill it. Praying for someone in the background leaves them similarly unable to attack.

### 3. How does Bhishma's conchshell symbolically indicate that there was no hope of victory? [70:00 – 72:00]

- **Question:** The point about the conch indicating defeat was not clear.
- **Answer:** Three things are happening at once.
- Externally, it encourages Duryodhana.
- Kshatriyas speak little and act more, and Bhishma felt the fighting should simply begin.
- Internally, per Baladeva Vidyabhushana, Bhishma's thought was that he would blow the conch and fight and give his life, but victory and defeat rest with the Lord, who is standing on the other side.
- The teacher notes this is exactly why the acharyas' commentaries exist: Srila Prabhupada did not record every such internal mood in the purports, but the commentaries preserve them.

### 4. Did Bhishma know the outcome? [72:00 – 74:00]

- **Question:** Krishna's presence guaranteed the Pandavas' victory — and Bhishma must have known who would win, yet gave his life for dharma anyway.
- **Answer:** Yes, in the sense that Bhishma was confident the Lord is affectionate to His devotees, that the Pandavas are devotees, and that He would therefore give them victory.

### 5. If different schools read the same word in opposite ways, how does one know which is right? [74:00 – 78:00]

- **Question:** _Aparyāptam_ has been given two contradictory meanings. When schools of thought differ like this, doubt arises about which is correct.
- **Answer:** The teacher cites the description of word-jugglery as a vast forest that causes the mind to wander. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- He recommends a lecture of Srila Prabhupada's titled _Hearing the Gita as Arjuna Did_.
- His decisive criterion is the conclusion and the result. Arjuna's result was that his illusion was destroyed, his memory regained, and he accepted the Lord's words and His position as the Supreme, and surrendered fully.
- Applied as a test: the distinction of Srila Prabhupada's Gita is that millions surrendered their lives to Krishna through it. A thing is known by its fruit.

### 6. The names fit both the metre and the context [78:00]

- **Comment:** One would assume from a poetic standpoint that a name like Hrishikesha is chosen to fit the metre, yet the context of the verse also draws on its meaning.
- **Answer:** The teacher agrees and returns to Sridhara Swami's benedictory verse, saying again that he will look it up and discuss it. ### 7. Does Bhishma's life not look like a lifetime of supporting injustice? [78:00 – 84:00]

- **Question:** The teacher has explained how to face injustice, but Bhishma's life seems to show how to support it.
- **Answer:** The teacher's opening principle is that to make sense of the present one has to understand the past, and that injustice often appears to us precisely because we do not know the background.
- He sketches Bhishma's vows as they occurred at the external level — renouncing the kingdom, then renouncing marriage, then undertaking to serve whoever occupied the throne — noting that at the time he could not have known what kind of rulers would follow. He raises and rejects the objection that Bhishma could simply have reconsidered later or changed sides.
- The deeper reason he gives: Krishna wanted to establish for the entire world, for all time, that one who stands on the side of irreligion cannot win. For that lesson no one serves better than Bhishma, who held an upper hand through twenty-seven days against Parashurama. When such a person is on the losing side, the message is unmistakable.
- Pure devotees are willing instruments who will do anything for the Lord, including appearing as the villain, knowing that people will question it for all time to come.
- Finally, before Bhishma departed the Lord showed the world what was in his heart — his prayers, in the ninth chapter of the First Canto.

### 8. We pray for the person, but they do not change and we are affected again [82:00 – 88:00]

- **Question:** Praying and chanting for someone removes our own ill feeling, but the other person repeats the behaviour. Should one forgive and forget, or forgive while remembering that people's natures do not change, and keep a distance?
- **Answer:** The teacher reframes it: this is not a question of the right method but of capacity. People are told to forget and forgive, and cannot.
- His illustration of what full capacity looks like is Kamsa, who killed six of Devaki's newborn children in front of her, and who — after the goddess appeared and told him the one destined to kill him had already taken birth elsewhere — fell at Vasudeva and Devaki's feet, argued that he was only an instrument of karma, and was forgiven by them. The teacher's comment on reading that pastime was that these are the people who can become the father and mother of God.
- Our own capacity is proportionate to how Krishna conscious we are and how much positivity is in our life; without that, bringing the principle into practice is not easy.
- On the observed result: our ill feelings do go down, which is the expected outcome. The other person changes less because our prayer is not intense — we are doing it as a formality, a round or two, not as we would pray for someone in our own household in serious difficulty.
- His figure for the effect: it moves the situation from minus fifty to minus forty-eight, and we expect minus five, so we conclude nothing happened.

### 9. How does one avoid being internally disturbed when it happens in one's own family? [88:00 – 94:00]

- **Question:** We are not disturbed when something happens to others or elsewhere, but we are when it happens to us. Arjuna wept when Abhimanyu was killed.
- **Answer:** The teacher corrects the premise — he did not say one should not be disturbed. Emotions are part of us, and being connected to someone means being affected; this is natural and should not be made artificial.
- **But duty continues.** Arjuna wept and returned to the fighting. Devotees are affected, but they do not give up their duty.
- **Zero emotion is not the goal.** If a person feels nothing for someone he is connected to, how would he feel anything for the Lord? A devotee is by nature grateful for what others have done for him.
- What distinguishes a devotee is that the emotion does not overwhelm him; he stays grounded in reality, knowing the nature of this world, and asks what is next, what best he can do, and how he can connect with Krishna and fulfil His mission.
- **Being disturbed beyond the point of functioning is a signal** that one needs to go deeper in Krishna consciousness.
- He closes with an incident from his own service: discussing a junior devotee's shortcomings with another devotee on the ashram balcony, unaware that the devotee was in the room and overheard. When the teacher later apologized, the reply was that he was indeed disturbed — not by the correction itself, but at how shallow his Krishna consciousness was, that he could not take such a small correction from his closest superiors wholeheartedly. The teacher presents this as the right attitude.

### 10. Where does Arjuna address Krishna as the Supreme Personality of Godhead? [94:00 – 96:00]

- **Question:** A participant offers her worked answer to an earlier exercise, citing Vyasadeva, Narada, and Brahma as accepting Krishna as the Supreme.
- **Answer:** The teacher clarifies what he had actually asked — how many places _Arjuna himself_ addresses Krishna as the Supreme. He points to the Tenth Chapter, where Arjuna states this and then cites Narada, Asita, Devala, and Vyasa as also accepting it, and notes some further instances in the Eleventh Chapter, with not many in the earlier chapters. He acknowledges the participant's own analysis as worthwhile. [Verse references as spoken; see Verification Flags.]

### 11. We are told to hear as Arjuna did, but our level is nothing like his [96:00 – 99:41]

- **Question:** Arjuna was a pure devotee; our faith and study are not on that level.
- **Answer:** The principle is following in the footsteps, not imitating.
- His analogy: a candidate preparing for a competitive examination studies the way the topper studied, even though his calibre, intelligence, and capacity are not the same. That is simply how one proceeds.
- What is to be followed is Arjuna's mood — particularly the gratitude in his heart. The teacher notes that the Gita contains emotions and not only words, which is precisely why the acharyas' commentaries are needed at points; the word Hrishikesha in this very verse carries Arjuna's appeal that the Lord take control of his senses.
- The practical form of this is reading with a grateful heart, and reading in order to serve Krishna rather than to become a scholar. Read with that attitude and faith grows, devotion grows, and the result manifests.
- His closing remark: the Lord finished the Gita in forty to forty-five minutes, and ours takes rather longer.
