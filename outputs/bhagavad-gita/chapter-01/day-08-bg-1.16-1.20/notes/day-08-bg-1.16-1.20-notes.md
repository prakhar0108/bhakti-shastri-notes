## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 1.15 (continued) [12:00 – 22:00]

<!-- verse:1.15 -->

**Context.** Carrying on from the previous class, the teacher fills in the histories behind the three conchshells named in this verse. His framing point is that every name in these verses has a story behind it. [12:00 – 14:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Pāñcajanya_: The Lord's conchshell. The teacher recounts that it came from a demon named Panchajana whom the Lord killed and whose form He then took as this conch. [12:00 – 14:00]
- _Devadatta_: Arjuna's conchshell, given to him by Indra. [12:00 – 14:00]
- _Pauṇḍra_: Bhima's conchshell, which the teacher describes as enormous, in proportion to Bhima himself. [12:00]
- _Vṛkodara_: Bhima's epithet, referring to the fire in his stomach that digested whatever he ate. [12:00 – 14:00]
- _Bhīma-karmā_: One who performs herculean tasks — the teacher notes this is how Srila Prabhupada renders it. [14:00]

#### How Arjuna received Devadatta [12:00 – 14:00]

The teacher relates that certain demons were troubling Indra in the heavenly realm and the demigods could not defeat them. When Arjuna went there he assisted the demigods and destroyed those demons, and a pleased Indra gave him the conchshell Devadatta. [Names flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Why Bhima is called Vrikodara and Bhima-karma [14:00 – 20:00]

- Whatever Bhima ate was digested immediately. Through the years in exile the alms collected were divided in two, with half going to Bhima and the other half shared among everyone else — and even then he felt he was fasting.
- The teacher stresses that although Bhima ate enormously, there is no description anywhere of him sleeping it off; he worked extremely hard.
- **The source of his strength.** As a child Bhima was poisoned by Duryodhana and thrown into the Ganga. He sank to the subterranean regions, where the serpents there bit him, and their venom neutralized the poison he had been given. He then woke and began fighting them, and was eventually brought to Vasuki. There he drank from jars of a nectar that ordinarily no one could take more than a glass of; Bhima drank seven, and gained the strength of ten thousand elephants. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- **Herculean tasks before the war.** The teacher notes Bhima had already killed Kichaka and other demons, and that when the house of lac was set ablaze it was Bhima who carried all the Pandavas out.
- **His conduct in battle.** He swung his club so fast that soldiers were thrown aside; sometimes he merely laughed in a way that frightened an approaching opponent; he could pull an attacker's arms out; and when a warrior came at him on an elephant, a single blow felled the elephant and its rider. His club was itself so heavy that a thousand men were needed to lift it. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Why Duryodhana named Bhima first [16:00 – 18:00]

Returning to verse 4, the teacher observes that although Arjuna is the principal commander, Duryodhana named Bhima first — because of Bhima's anger and the vows he had taken but had never yet had the opportunity to fulfil:

- When Duhshasana dragged Draupadi into the assembly by her hair, Bhima vowed to tear out the arm that had done it.
- When Duryodhana gestured at his thigh, Bhima vowed to break it.

#### Anger with a proper object [18:00 – 20:00]

- The teacher treats Bhima's anger as the constructive and justified use of anger, because the Kauravas had committed grave aggression and the war was for the establishment of dharma.
- He recalls the classification of six kinds of aggressor mentioned in a purport — among them one who sets fire to your house, one who administers poison, one who molests your wife, and one who seizes your property — and notes the Kauravas committed every one of them. On air he recalls only some of the six. [List flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- He contrasts this with staged fighting that draws large audiences while serving no noble cause.
- Crucially, he notes that Bhima never crossed Yudhishthira. His anger was tempered by his superiors, and was therefore controlled anger.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Every epithet in the verse is earned. Bhima's strength, appetite, and fury are described at length precisely because his anger had a legitimate object and remained under a superior's restraint. [12:00 – 22:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.16 – 1.18 [22:00 – 24:00]

<!-- verse:1.16 -->

**Context.** The remaining Pandava warriors sound their conchshells in turn — Yudhishthira, Nakula and Sahadeva, and then the assembled kings and princes of that side. [22:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Ananta-vijaya_: Yudhishthira's conchshell. [22:00]
- _Sughoṣa_ and _maṇipuṣpaka_: The conchshells of Nakula and Sahadeva. [22:00]
- _Rājā_: King — used here of Yudhishthira. [22:00 – 24:00]

#### Why Yudhishthira is already called king [22:00 – 24:00]

The teacher raises the difficulty himself: the war is not over and Yudhishthira has not yet taken the throne. Following Baladeva Vidyabhushana, he explains that the title is used because Yudhishthira had earlier performed the Rajasuya sacrifice.

#### What Sanjaya is doing by listing them [22:00 – 24:00]

- Srila Prabhupada's purport observes that Sanjaya is indicating to Dhritarashtra, indirectly, that his sons cannot win.
- The teacher notes that people of that time understood such indications, so the accumulation of signs was itself the message: the chariot is divine, the horses are divine, the personalities seated on it are divine, and now the conchshells too are called transcendental.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The roll-call of conchshells is Sanjaya's way of delivering a verdict without stating it. The signs are laid out one after another for a listener expected to draw the conclusion himself. [22:00 – 24:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.19 [24:00 – 28:00]

<!-- verse:1.19 -->

**Context.** The verse states the result of everything that has sounded so far: the tumult shattered the hearts of Dhritarashtra's sons, and the sound reverberated through both sky and earth. [24:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Dhārtarāṣṭrāṇām_: Of the sons of Dhritarashtra — the teacher extends it to those who follow Dhritarashtra's policy, blind in matters of dharma as he was. [24:00 – 26:00]
- _Hṛdayāni vyadārayat_: Shattered their hearts. [24:00]
- _Nabhaś ca pṛthivīṁ caiva_: Sky and earth alike. [24:00]

#### Why only one side's hearts were shattered [24:00 – 26:00]

- When the Kaurava conches sounded, nothing comparable happened to the Pandavas. When the Pandava conches sounded, the Kauravas' hearts were shattered.
- The acharyas' reason, as the teacher gives it: irreligion weakens the heart. His illustrations are travelling without a ticket and feeling anxious at the sight of any inspector, or a thief becoming uneasy at the sight of any policeman.

#### Two rounds already lost [26:00 – 28:00]

The teacher reads the narrative as a sequence of contests decided before the fighting:

- **First round — the seeing.** Duryodhana had not expected what he saw. The Kaurava army was almost double in size, but the Pandava force was arranged in such a way, and was united in heart, that the smaller army looked larger. Duryodhana was frightened, and nothing comparable is described of the Pandavas.
- **Second round — the sound.** The Kauravas sounded first and nothing followed; the Pandavas sounded and the Kauravas' hearts gave way.

#### How Duryodhana came by eleven divisions [26:00 – 28:00]

The teacher notes that little of that force was to Duryodhana's credit:

- Nine divisions came from the kingdom Pandu had ruled well before Dhritarashtra.
- One division came from Krishna.
- The eleventh came from Shalya, who loved the Pandavas but was received so lavishly on the road that he assumed the Pandavas had arranged it and promised to support whoever had; Duryodhana then revealed himself. The teacher treats this as another act of cheating. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]

He adds a saying he attributes to military experience — that wars are won in the general's tent — making the point that fighting is not merely physical conflict and that fighting spirit is decisive.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Irreligion weakens the heart, and the weakening shows before any blow is struck. Two contests — the seeing and the sounding — are settled while both armies are still standing still. [24:00 – 28:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.20 [28:00 – 36:00]

<!-- verse:1.20 -->

**Context.** Arjuna, seated beneath the flag bearing Hanuman, takes up his bow as the weapons are about to be released, and prepares to speak to Krishna. [28:00 – 30:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Kapi-dhvajaḥ_: One whose flag bears Hanuman. [28:00 – 30:00]
- _Pāṇḍavaḥ_: Again a name of Arjuna specifically. [28:00 – 30:00]
- _Hṛṣīkeśam tadā vākyam_: The words he then addressed to Hrishikesha. [28:00 – 30:00]

#### The flag itself [30:00]

The teacher describes it as two miles long, never collecting dust, and never catching in any obstruction — a special flag, not an ordinary one. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Two accounts of how Hanuman came to be there [30:00 – 36:00]

**The first,** which he attributes to Baladeva Vidyabhushana, is set at Rameshvaram:

- Arjuna remarked that Rama need not have taken the monkeys' help to build the bridge, since as an archer He could simply have built one.
- A small monkey seated there replied that a bridge made of arrows would not hold — it would not bear even the weight of a small monkey like himself.
- Arjuna, challenged, built one; it broke as soon as the monkey stepped on it. He built a second; it broke again. Before the third attempt he vowed to end his life if it failed.
- At that point it was pointed out that a contest requires a judge, and someone offered to oversee it. This time Arjuna's bridge held, even as the monkey — who was Hanuman — applied his full strength at full size.
- Looking beneath, they found the one who had been supporting it, bleeding, and recognized the Lord, who asked why the two of them were quarrelling when the work of bridge-building was already done and other work remained.
- Hanuman then said he would remain on Arjuna's flag. The teacher notes that the accounts of the intervening figure vary between versions.

**The second** concerns the golden lotus:

- Arjuna had brought Draupadi a fragrant golden lotus of a thousand petals from beyond the Alakananda in the Himalayas. Delighted, she asked for more, and Bhima set off confidently to fetch them.
- On the way an aged monkey lay with its tail across the path. Asked to move it aside, the monkey said it was too old and invited Bhima to move it himself.
- Bhima applied the strength of ten thousand elephants and could not shift it. He then offered obeisances, understanding that this was some great personality, and Hanuman revealed himself.
- Invited to join the coming war, Hanuman replied that his time for action was past, but that he would be present in their war cries, seated on Arjuna's flag.

#### Srila Prabhupada's point in the purport [34:00 – 36:00]

Hanuman was with Rama, and Rama was victorious. In this war the same Lord is present as Krishna on Arjuna's chariot, and the same Hanuman is present on the flag. The Pandavas' victory is therefore settled.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The flag is the last of the signs. The same Lord and the same servant who won an earlier war are present again, so the outcome is established before Arjuna has spoken a word. [28:00 – 36:00]
</Callout>

## The Nine Signs of Victory [36:00 – 38:00]

The teacher gathers the section's argument into a list, noting that different authors count these slightly differently:

1. **The Lord is seated on the chariot** — the statement that victory belongs to the sons of Pandu, on whose side is Janardana. [BG 1.14]
2. **The name _Mādhava_** — the Lord is not alone; the goddess of fortune is with Him. [BG 1.14]
3. **_Dharma-kṣetra_** — the field itself. Those who follow dharma are the Pandavas; the father of dharma is Krishna; and Yudhishthira is Dharmaraja. The teacher compares it to playing on home ground. [BG 1.1]
4. **_Kapi-dhvaja_** — Hanuman on the flag. [BG 1.20]
5. **The transcendental conchshells** of Krishna and Arjuna. [BG 1.14]
6. **Bhishma's conchshell**, which the acharyas read as his acknowledgement that victory and defeat rest with the Lord, who stands on the other side. [BG 1.12]
7. **The divine chariot**, given by Agnideva. [BG 1.14]
8. **The divine horses.** [BG 1.14]
9. **The shattering of the Kauravas' hearts** at the sound. [BG 1.19]

#### Why none of it registered [38:00 – 40:00]

- The teacher's formulation: when the mind is clouded by material desires, we cannot make sense of things even when they are entirely obvious.
- The cause is attachment to one's own plans. A person clings to his scheme and will not concede.
- His illustration is the joke of two men arguing over whether a knife or a pair of scissors cuts better; the stronger throws the other into the river, and as he goes under, the drowning man's hand emerges above the water still making a scissoring motion.
- Applied: the Lord indicates a great deal to us through our lives, and we are unable to accept it. This is Duryodhana's mentality — everything had already been made clear, both throughout the Mahabharata and now immediately before the war.

## The Willing Instrument: Hanuman on the Flag [40:00 – 44:00]

The teacher treats this as the section's principal practical lesson.

- Devotees do not want to do something great or something small; they want to do whatever pleases Krishna, and to become willing instruments in His hands.
- **The contrast.** In the Ramayana Hanuman destroyed vast numbers of extremely powerful demons single-handed, and the teacher observes that the fighters of this later age would hardly compare with them. Yet here he is seated on a flag, calling out encouragement rather than fighting.
- **Why that is difficult.** His analogies are drawn from service: an accomplished singer listening to someone who cannot sing lead the arati, or an expert mridanga player listening to a beginner. Tolerating it is hard, and the urge is to take over.
- Hanuman knew what his service was. In the Ramayana that was his service; here his service is to support, and the man of the match is Arjuna.
- The teacher notes this was Hanuman's attitude throughout his life — powerful and intelligent as he was, he became Sugriva's servant, someone lesser in strength, devotion, and intelligence, because of an obligation to his own teacher. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The hardest service is often the smaller one, offered by someone capable of much more. Hanuman's place on the flag is the chapter's quiet instruction on what willing instrumentality looks like. [40:00 – 44:00]
</Callout>

## Purushottama Month [44:00 – 54:00]

The month began on the day of this class, and the teacher devotes the closing portion to it.

#### What the month is [46:00 – 48:00]

- It comes once in roughly three years, as an extra month, and is otherwise called _mala-māsa_ — the impure month — during which people avoid materially auspicious undertakings.
- The teacher relates that the rejected month lamented its lack of value, and that the Lord accepted it personally and declared it equal to Himself in glory, fame, and benediction — which is why it is called Purushottama month.
- He mentions the exchange in which Narada Muni asked Narayana at Badrika-ashrama which month is best and how it should be observed, with the resulting accounts given in the Padma Purana. [References flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- The benediction he cites is that one who observes this month properly will attain the Lord's abode.

#### Gratitude for the daily programme [44:00 – 46:00]

The teacher makes a point he says should accompany all such observances:

- Whatever is prescribed for Purushottama month, for Kartika, for Ekadashi, or for festivals — reading the Gita and the Bhagavatam, offering flowers and ghee lamps, kirtana, chanting — is already what the morning programme contains.
- Srila Prabhupada gave that programme, so these occasions should produce gratitude toward him rather than a sense of novelty.
- The reason special periods still matter is that the living entity tends to become mechanical and stops being conscious of what he is doing.

#### What to actually do [48:00 – 54:00]

- Focus more on spiritual life, and take on some extra spiritual commitment. Those are the two purposes of such months.
- Rise in _brahma-muhūrta_ and complete the morning programme properly.
- Recite the Fifteenth Chapter of the Gita daily — the teacher notes it is the shortest chapter, so it costs little time.
- Offer ghee lamps and flowers; increase reading, hearing, and chanting.
- He names songs associated with this observance, including the Jagannatha, Chaura, and Yugala ashtakams. [Titles flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- Avoid offences to Vaishnavas during this month particularly.

#### How to take the vow [52:00 – 54:00]

- Take a resolution you will not break, rather than a loose intention to try.
- Do not plan on the basis of an ordinary day and then abandon it when a programme runs late.
- Equally, do not push existing services aside in order to increase devotional activity — that is not the method.
- Fit the additions into the schedule deliberately: prayers and recitation immediately after the morning programme, extra rounds pegged to a fixed point such as not taking dinner until they are complete.

#### On our actual position [48:00 – 52:00]

- The teacher notes that in the present age the body lacks strength, the senses pull in the wrong direction, and the mind produces contrary thoughts — which makes concentrated effort more necessary, not less.
- He relates that Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati Thakura described one who does not chant sixty-four rounds as fallen, and that Srila Prabhupada, seeing our actual condition, gave sixteen.
- He compares the drift in practice to posture during chanting: attentive at the start of a japa session and gradually sagging. These months exist to revive and bounce back.

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications

### 1. How should philosophy be spoken to someone in acute distress? [54:00 – 58:00]

- **Question:** A person may answer outwardly while going through great inner turmoil. Telling someone who is starving that he is not the body could make a suicidal situation worse. How should this be handled step by step?
- **Answer:** The teacher separates the levels. At the level of principle the person is the soul, whether hungry or fed. At the level of dealing, the Gita itself gives the method.
- He points to BG 17.15 on the austerity of speech, and lists four filters to apply before speaking:
  - **Not agitating** — do not tell a person who is fasting that he is the soul; that filter has already been crossed.
  - **Truthful.**
  - **Pleasing.**
  - **Beneficial.**
- He remarks that applying all four would nearly amount to a vow of silence, but singles out the fourth as most important: we should be concerned with how this person can actually be benefited.
- When that is the concern, the question of _how_ to present what scripture says follows naturally — rather than simply downloading the scriptural statement onto the person.

### 2. Does Indra represent the mind? [58:00 – 60:00]

- **Question:** A participant relays an analogy encountered online: that Indra's insecure and disruptive behaviour represents the human mind, with the other demigods ruling other parts of the body and serving him, and that we should therefore control the mind through bhakti.
- **Answer:** The teacher accepts the conclusion — that one should practise bhakti — but corrects the premise: the presiding deity of the mind is the moon.
- His governing principle: where scripture itself supplies an analogy, such as the body compared to a chariot and the senses to horses in the Upanishads, it can be used. Where there is no scriptural basis, stretching an allegory creates problems.

### 3. What should we learn from Sanjaya's clarity? [60:00 – 62:00]

- **Question:** Sanjaya was entirely dependent on Dhritarashtra, yet reported the truth without being influenced — unlike Drona and Bhishma, who could not speak in Draupadi's assembly. What is the lesson?
- **Answer:** The teacher points to the end of the Gita, where Sanjaya attributes his position to Vyasadeva's mercy.
- So clarity in our own lives depends on the blessings of Hari, guru, and the Vaishnavas — and on our own receptivity to them.
- His counter-example is Dhritarashtra himself: Vidura wanted to give him mercy and he would not take it, so he had to absorb some shocks first; in the thirteenth chapter of the Bhagavatam's First Canto he finally receives it and departs. The more receptive a person is, the sooner clarity comes.
- He cites the Caitanya-caritamrta's comparison of Krishna to the sun, where maya cannot stand: attachment to what is temporary blurs clarity, and attachment to Krishna produces it. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]

### 4. Why does fear persist after coming to bhakti? [62:00 – 66:00]

- **Question:** Irreligion makes the heart weak, but in devotional life fear remains and only changes form — in this case a constant fear of falling down, even imagining situations that would make continuing impossible.
- **Answer:** The teacher notes that fear is prominent in the material world and arises from absorption in the second, the separate interest. He gives the reference as SB 11.2.37. [Quotation flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- The criterion he offers is whether a thing takes us toward Krishna or away from Him. Whatever takes us toward Him has use.
- On this basis fear of maya is a healthy fear. He relates that Srila Prabhupada said his only concern was that his disciples were not sufficiently fearful of maya.
- The complete position is that fear of maya and confidence in Krishna proceed together, and both are the Lord's mercy to someone who is alert.
- His generalization: anyone seeking success anywhere needs to know both what must be done and what must not — the one requires energy, the other requires fear of the consequence.
