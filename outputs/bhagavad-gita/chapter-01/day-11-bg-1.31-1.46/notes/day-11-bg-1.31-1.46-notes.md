## Continuity & Recap [02:00 – 16:00]

- The teacher restates the CDEFI acronym for Arjuna's five reasons — Compassion, Destruction of family tradition, Enjoyment, Fear of sin, Indecision — noting the previous class covered compassion.
- He revises BG 1.31–1.35, where Arjuna says he sees no good in killing his own kinsmen, wants neither victory nor kingdom nor happiness, and would not do it even for the three worlds.

#### Happiness comes from reciprocation, not from things [04:00 – 08:00]

- The teacher's point on _kiṁ no rājyena govinda_: in this world things themselves do not give us happiness. Happiness lies in the reciprocation with those for whom we do things — the intensification of reciprocation.
- Arjuna had won a great deal previously, but always for someone's pleasure: he would report a victory to Yudhishthira, and Yudhishthira's pleasure was Arjuna's pleasure. As a boy he would demonstrate a new skill to Drona, and Drona's delight was his reward.
- **Applied to devotional life:** whatever we do is not for external accomplishment but to please guru, sadhu, and the Lord. Accomplishment is not the goal.
- So Arjuna's argument is that the very people for whose sake a kingdom would be worth having are the ones standing in front of him.

#### Long-term, but materially long-term [04:00 – 06:00]

- An ordinary listener would find Arjuna's position admirable: in a world where people quarrel over money, position, and land, here is someone ready to renounce everything, and thinking beyond the immediate.
- The acharyas' correction, as the teacher gives it: Arjuna is thinking long-term, but materially long-term — and it is not long-term enough. The Lord is going to show him something considerably further out.

#### Preparing the patient before the operation [08:00 – 12:00]

- The Lord is constructing the Gita's setting. Before any operation the patient has to be prepared; a doctor will not operate on a patient who is not ready.
- The teacher's illustration is a health retreat where very little food is given, because the therapy only takes effect on a suitable diet. Spiritual practice works the same way: the same hearing, reading, and chanting goes much deeper when consciousness is prepared for it.
- He relates an account of Dhruva Maharaja: had the same conversation with his mother occurred after an ordinary afternoon of play rather than in that desperate condition after the insult, the effect might not have been the same. The mood in which we hear determines the effect. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### What Arjuna is actually seeing [12:00 – 14:00]

- Arjuna had only wanted to see the army; he had no plan of negotiating peace. On seeing so many of his own people he came into bodily consciousness.
- These are not merely faces. Bhishma taught him to walk holding his finger; Drona taught him archery; so many of them had nourished the Pandavas' lives with affection, taught them how to live, played with them, blessed them, and helped them out of difficulties. The whole film is running in Arjuna's mind.
- The teacher's observation: often one only discovers what a situation is after entering it, and whenever a difficult situation arrives, the depth of one's Krishna consciousness is what gets tested.
- **The one good thing:** Krishna was right there. Precisely when Arjuna needed Him most, He was standing beside him — and the teacher applies this to everyone. As long as Krishna is there, there is hope even in an impossible situation.

#### Good people are not thereby free of the problem [14:00 – 16:00]

- The teacher notes that Arjuna's views sound broad-minded, but are formed with himself at the centre.
- He distinguishes two kinds of people in this world: those doing wrong and troubling others, and good people who trouble nobody, cheat nobody — but who still want to enjoy at the material level. The second is also a problem.
- His conclusion: for real enjoyment, material enjoyment has to be given up, and that is what the Lord will explain.

## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 1.36 [16:00 – 28:00]

<!-- verse:1.36 -->

**Context.** Arjuna's third reason: killing these men, even though they are aggressors, will only bring sin. [16:00 – 18:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Pāpam evāśrayed asmān_: Sin alone will take hold of us. [16:00]
- _Ātatāyinaḥ_: Aggressors. [16:00 – 18:00]
- _Svajanam_: Again "our own people" — the teacher notes how often the word recurs. [16:00 – 18:00]
- _Lobhopahata-cetasaḥ_: Those whose consciousness is overpowered by greed. [18:00 – 20:00]

#### Consciousness zooms, and then fixes [16:00 – 18:00]

The teacher returns to a point from the previous class: wherever our consciousness zooms in, it fixes. A great deal is present, but we see only what consciousness has zoomed to. His illustration is a child who wants a video game — everything positive or negative that anyone tells him is irrelevant, because his object is clear and nothing else registers. Arjuna keeps returning to _svajanam_, and nothing else is visible to him.

#### The conflict Arjuna cannot resolve [18:00]

- According to family duty, one protects one's own blood relations.
- According to the duty of a kshatriya, aggressors are to be killed.
- Caught between the two, Arjuna cannot determine what he should actually do. The teacher notes that this is a special case of aggression, since it includes Bhishma and Drona.

#### Greed covering consciousness [18:00 – 24:00]

Arjuna says the other side cannot see because greed has covered their consciousness. The teacher develops this into the class's central diagnostic principle:

- Outer eyes alone are not sufficient to analyse any situation. A correct consciousness is required.
- If consciousness is impure, perception will be distorted.
- **His scriptural comparison:** three personalities encounter a broadly similar sight — Ajamila, who was completely disturbed by it; Shukadeva Gosvami, who passed bathing women without registering any difference; and Narada Muni, who was unaffected and responded by giving a curse intended to purify. The same kind of scene, read entirely differently according to consciousness. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- His everyday illustration: a brother and sister of similar age walking down the street, whom a person of contaminated consciousness assumes to be a couple.
- **The sharpest application:** this is why we see others' faults so readily — because there are so many faults within, seeing anything good becomes difficult.
- **What greed does:** one who is covered by greed sees only the object of desire, and fails to recognize the price that must be paid for it.

#### Arjuna's logic is impeccable, and still wrong [24:00 – 26:00]

- Arjuna's argument is that the Kauravas' failure to see is explicable — greed has covered them — but _we_ should be able to see, since Yudhishthira is on our side and so is Krishna.
- The teacher notes how logical this all sounds, so much so that one might think the Gita were unnecessary.
- **The application he draws:** we all feel we are thinking correctly and that others are failing to understand — colleagues, superiors, even one's spiritual authority, who "just needs a personal meeting so I can give him some clarity." Attachment to one's own opinion makes this very difficult.

#### The purport on fighting [26:00 – 28:00]

- Srila Prabhupada's position is that a kshatriya should fight; religious warfare is not wrong.
- The teacher's analogy for why both sides can look alike from outside: a violent madman has to be subdued by police, who must use comparable force. An outsider might conclude both are mad. They are not — one is mad, the other is correcting the madness.
- So the Lord wanted this war, because those whose consciousness was destroyed by greed had to be corrected, and fighting was the remaining means.
- Arjuna's own reasoning, per Baladeva Vidyabhushana and Vishvanatha Chakravarti Thakura, is result-based: if the final result will be bad, why perform the action at all?

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Perception follows consciousness. Arjuna correctly diagnoses greed in his opponents and cannot see that his own view is being shaped the same way — which is exactly how we habitually assess situations. [16:00 – 28:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.39 – 1.42 [28:00 – 60:00]

<!-- verse:1.39 -->

**Context.** Arjuna's fourth reason, developed at length: the destruction of the dynasty destroys family tradition, irreligion follows, women become degraded, unwanted progeny appear, the ancestors fall, and community duties collapse. [28:00 – 30:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Kula-kṣaye praṇaśyanti kula-dharmāḥ sanātanāḥ_: With the destruction of the dynasty the eternal family tradition is destroyed. [28:00]
- _Adharmo 'bhibhavaty uta_: Irreligion becomes prominent. [34:00]
- _Pradūṣyanti kula-striyaḥ_ / _strīṣu duṣṭāsu_: The women of the family become polluted. [34:00 – 36:00]
- _Varṇa-saṅkaraḥ_: The teacher's working definition is progeny with poor consciousness and degraded mindsets. [36:00]
- _Lupta-piṇḍodaka-kriyāḥ_: The offerings to the ancestors cease. [44:00 – 46:00]
- _Utsādyante jāti-dharmāḥ_: Community duties are devastated — the teacher notes Srila Prabhupada's purport opens with "community projects." [56:00 – 60:00]

#### The chain Arjuna constructs [28:00 – 30:00; 58:00 – 60:00]

The teacher lays out the sequence Arjuna builds:

1. The dynasty is destroyed, because those fighting are the senior leaders of every family across the world — no women, no children, no infirm.
2. Since the elders are the ones who understand and protect the family tradition, that tradition lapses.
3. With religious principles lost, irreligion becomes prominent.
4. Women are left unprotected and become degraded.
5. Unwanted progeny appear.
6. Offerings to the ancestors cease, so the ancestors also suffer.
7. Community duties and projects collapse.

#### Family tradition changing in practice [30:00 – 32:00]

The teacher's illustration of the drift is a family shop or dharmashala with photographs across five generations: the great-great-grandfather in traditional dress, the next in a safari suit, the next in a tie and suit, the current proprietor in a t-shirt and jeans, and his son in shorts. His remark: in a sense the destruction of family tradition is already underway.

#### Is family tradition actually eternal? [32:00 – 34:00]

The teacher raises the difficulty himself and answers it:

- Vishvanatha Chakravarti Thakura's definition of _sanātana kula-dharma_ is right conduct maintained by the family and handed down over many generations.
- But if something is eternal it cannot be destroyed, so the two statements contradict each other. Family tradition is therefore not actually eternal.
- **The reason Arjuna says it:** when illusion sets in, a person does not register what he is saying. The teacher's illustration is the exaggerations people make at weddings and in romantic declarations.
- The distinction that resolves it: the supreme occupation (_paro dharma_) is devotion to the Lord, which is eternal. Duties to family — chastity between spouses, children respecting elders, caring for relatives — are secondary, and are not the eternal nature of the soul.

#### Why irreligion follows automatically [34:00]

- There is a continuous conflict between dharma and adharma, and dharma is always under attack. As soon as dharma weakens, irreligion takes over.
- **The teacher's personal application:** this operates in our own hearts as well. Fifteen days without the morning programme, fifteen days without reading — and wrong things arrive in the mind by themselves, the way dust settles in a room that is not cleaned daily.
- It operates identically at the level of the heart, of society, and of the universe.

#### Why unwanted progeny is the gravest link [34:00 – 40:00]

- The teacher states the principle that the primary purpose of marriage is to produce good progeny.
- When society's protectors are killed, women lose protection — he cites the principle that a woman is protected by parents, then by husband, then by children — and unscrupulous men exploit them.
- The resulting progeny have degraded consciousness, and additionally cannot be regulated.
- **His vivid formulation:** the inner software is corrupt, so whatever training is given goes wrong. Give such a person one profession and he misuses it; give him another and he misuses that. Education does not fix the underlying consciousness.
- Srila Prabhupada's purport, as he reads it: good population in human society is the basic principle of peace, prosperity, and spiritual progress, and such population depends on the chastity and faithfulness of its womanhood.
- He relates Srila Prabhupada's response to population-control arguments — that the problem is not the number but the quality, and that children should not be produced indiscriminately.
- He notes that much of consciousness is prepared in the womb according to scripture, which is why so much emphasis is placed on the parents' condition.

#### On the purport's statements about women [40:00 – 44:00]

The teacher addresses this directly, acknowledging that it is not comfortable material:

- He notes the purport cites Chanakya on women, and that Western audiences in particular found such statements difficult to accept.
- **Srila Prabhupada's own resolution,** as he relates it: asked whether women are less intelligent than men, Srila Prabhupada replied that one is neither a man nor a woman but a soul, and the soul is neither male nor female.
- He relates a second exchange in which a disciple asked about a research claim that women's brains are smaller, and Srila Prabhupada laughed and said that when she chanted, her brain would increase.
- **His own framing:** one should not conclude from this that men are superior to women. In reality the person is the soul, and after entering spiritual life these distinctions operate differently — he points to the many exalted women in the tradition and in the present movement.
- The point being made, he says, is at the level of dealings in society: that protecting women is important from the standpoint of the community. [Statements flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### The ancestors [44:00 – 48:00]

- Those with average pious credit go to the region of the ancestors; those with great piety go higher. The teacher notes the descriptions in the Fifth Canto's cosmology.
- The offerings made here reach them there — his comparison is depositing money at one place and its being withdrawn at another. Time runs differently, so six months here corresponds to a day there.
- Srila Prabhupada's purport notes that ancestors may be suffering for their own past actions, and that offerings made on their behalf bring them relief.
- Arjuna's fear is that unwanted progeny will not perform these at all. The teacher observes that this is already visible — he mentions a modern business that performs last rites on behalf of families who delegate them.

#### The six debts, and the one that clears them all [48:00 – 56:00]

The teacher gives this section substantial time, built around a Bhagavatam verse he cites from the Eleventh Canto about one who has taken shelter of Mukunda being freed of all obligations. [Reference flagged; see Verification Flags.]

He lists the debts as:

- **To the demigods** — for sunlight, rain, and the arrangements that sustain life. His illustration of what their absence means is the effect on the mind of days without sun in winter, and he relates Srila Prabhupada's remark in London, when asked how one could understand hell without living there, that London in that condition was itself a fair indication. [Account flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- **To the sages** — for scriptural knowledge, for the Puranas and Vedas, for knowing how and why to live. This debt is repaid by actually reading, which he notes we often neglect.
- **To other living entities** — who maintain the ecological balance. His illustration is an account of a plague attributed first to cats, which were then killed, after which the plague worsened because the actual carriers were rats. [Account flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- **To relatives** — from whom we receive so much affection.
- **To human society at large** — for schools, hospitals, and public facilities.
- **To the ancestors** — discharged through the offerings described above.

His summary: a debt means what has been taken must be given back. We take a great deal, and most people are not even aware of these obligations — he jokes that these are larger than the housing loan people already find so burdensome. But one who fully takes the Lord's shelter is freed of all of them, because the debt begins from the first moment of nursing, and even the milk in a mother's body came by the Lord's arrangement. Everything comes through Him.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Arjuna's fourth reason is his most carefully constructed, and it is not foolish — the social consequences he traces are real. What is missing is that devotional service to the Lord is the one obligation whose discharge covers all the rest. [28:00 – 60:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.43 – 1.44 [60:00 – 72:00]

<!-- verse:1.43 -->

**Context.** Arjuna concludes that those who destroy family traditions dwell in hell, and expresses astonishment that they were prepared to commit such a sin out of a desire for royal happiness. [60:00 – 66:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Narake niyataṁ vāso_: Dwelling in hell — the teacher reads _niyatam_ as "for a long time" rather than perpetually. [60:00 – 64:00]
- _Anuśuśruma_: I have heard so — through disciplic succession. [64:00 – 66:00]
- _Aho bata_: An expression of astonishment. [66:00 – 68:00]
- _Kartuṁ vyavasitā vayam_: We were determined to do it — the teacher notes _vayam_ is plural, so Arjuna includes everyone, even the Lord. [68:00]

#### No concept of eternal hell [60:00 – 64:00]

- The teacher notes Arjuna has again overstated, as he did with "eternal" family tradition.
- The concept of permanent hell does not exist in this tradition. He contrasts it with traditions in which the alternatives are permanent, and relates an exchange he attributes to a devotee with a Christian priest: asked where an unborn child who dies in the womb would go, the priest said the kingdom of God, because the child is innocent — at which point the follow-up question, whether abortion is therefore good, produced confusion. The teacher's point is about what follows from a two-option framework without a concept of rebirth. [Account flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- His explanation of the usage: the material world itself is not eternal, and karma is not eternal. Terms like _nitya-baddha_ are used for a prolonged condition, which feels eternal. The same idiom appears elsewhere in scripture.

#### "I have heard" — the method of knowledge [64:00 – 66:00]

- Srila Prabhupada highlights _anuśuśruma_: Arjuna is not speaking from personal experience but citing authority.
- The teacher fills in how: during their forest years the Pandavas constantly received sages — he names Markandeya and Narada among them — and before that, kings would invite sages for discourses. So Arjuna's argument rests on systematic hearing from reliable sources, which the purport identifies as the way knowledge is actually received in this world. [Names flagged; see Verification Flags.]

#### Arjuna takes the blame himself [70:00 – 72:00]

The teacher treats this as the section's most instructive feature:

- Baladeva Vidyabhushana's comment, as he gives it, is that Arjuna considers even the thought of killing such people to be sinful.
- **What makes Arjuna the right candidate:** the teacher relays that Vishvanatha Chakravarti Thakura, Madhusudana Sarasvati, and others comment that this first chapter shows Arjuna to be sensitive, thoughtful, considerate, and refined — a proper receiver for the knowledge to come.
- **The contrast he draws:** our habit is to place the mistake on others. Arjuna's mindset is the reverse — he takes the entire fault on himself. Self-reflection is difficult; everyone looks outward, and looking inward requires effort.
- His formulation: seeing one's own fault clearly is the result of good sadhana. Where sadhana is weak, the opposite happens.
- He illustrates with an account from his own service of two initiation candidates — one who attended nothing and claimed to be well connected and ready, and one who attended regularly, maintained his practice, and said he was not yet qualified and wished to wait. The mentality is the point. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The chapter's portrait of Arjuna is deliberate. He is sensitive, he reasons from authority rather than opinion, and he blames himself rather than others — which is precisely why he is the one who receives the Gita. [60:00 – 72:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 1.45 – 1.46 [76:00 – 90:00]

<!-- verse:1.45 -->

<!-- verse:1.46 -->

**Context.** Arjuna says it would be better for him to be killed unresisting and unarmed, and then, casting aside his bow and arrows, sits down on the chariot, his mind overwhelmed with grief. [76:00 – 84:00]

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Apratīkāram_: Without resisting. [76:00 – 78:00]
- _Aśastram_: Unarmed. [76:00 – 78:00]
- _Kṣema-taram_: The teacher parses this as welfare in a superlative degree — "better for me." He cross-references the sense of _kṣema_ in BG 9.22. [78:00]
- _Visṛjya sa-śaraṁ cāpam_: Casting aside his bow along with the arrows. [84:00]
- _Śoka-saṁvigna-mānasaḥ_: His mind overwhelmed with grief. [84:00]

#### What the offer costs him [78:00 – 80:00]

- On a battlefield there are only two options, since fleeing was not something kshatriyas of that time did: kill or be killed.
- Arjuna is choosing the second. For a kshatriya, saying he will neither resist nor take up a weapon is an enormous statement — and he makes it because he believes it will benefit everyone.
- The teacher is explicit that the decision is nonetheless not correct, and that the Lord will explain this later.

#### Why guidance matters [80:00 – 84:00]

He draws the practical lesson at length:

- Our perception, however logical, may not be the only perception available. People in this world do not have the habit of taking guidance, which is one problem.
- A second problem is that when people are confused, they take guidance from someone who is already confused.
- A third is that some do not feel guidance is needed at all — he relates a devotee saying he would seek guidance when confused, and was clear at present.
- **The medical comparison:** a doctor does not operate without a clear diagnosis simply because many patients are waiting. Where the diagnosis is unclear, a second opinion from a senior, experienced doctor is sought, and sometimes symptoms point in contradictory directions and the process has to pause.
- **His general observation:** we enter many phases of life — a business, a marriage, an ashram — and only after entering do we realize the complexity involved. A plan sounds complete beforehand; the Lord's own potency also operates.
- Arjuna came to fight and saw something else, so the best thing he did was to take shelter and take guidance.

#### The significance of putting down the bow [84:00 – 88:00]

- Until now Arjuna had been speaking; here he acts, and the teacher invokes the saying that actions speak louder than words.
- Setting the Gandiva aside is not a small matter. He had vowed to take the life of anyone who told him to relinquish it — and now he puts it down himself. It represented his determination.
- **Why the narrative records all this:** two reasons. To convey the gravity of the situation Arjuna was in; and to convey how much potency the Gita's message carries.
- **His closing analogy:** a team reaches a World Cup final and its star player sets down his bat and refuses to play. Everyone's attention is drawn. Then his coach speaks to him, and he not only picks the bat up but gives the finest performance of his life. Everyone would want to know what was said. The Lord arranged exactly that setting, so that it would be understood how special this message is and how much power it has to lift a person from anywhere.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The chapter ends with the most determined warrior of the age seated and unarmed. The setting exists to establish the magnitude of what the next chapter's answer must accomplish. [76:00 – 90:00]
</Callout>

## Audience Q&A & Clarifications

### 1. How should scripture broaden our perception? [90:00 – 96:00]

- **Question:** Reading the Bhagavatam's accounts of creation and of the hellish regions, one has no corresponding perception — and on reading the descriptions, one feels qualified only for the latter. How should this be approached?
- **Answer:** Scripture exists precisely to broaden perception, because we otherwise think only about the short material life in front of us.
- He relates a talk of Radhanath Swami on what trees teach: the oldest trees in a Californian forest, one over two thousand years old, with trunks so wide that dozens of people would be needed to encircle them. Asked what message such a tree would give humanity, the answer offered was that it would speak of how foolish human beings are, how short their lives are, and for what trivial things they fight and surrender their lives — having watched whole civilizations pass. [Details flagged; see Verification Flags.]
- **The second function of scripture** he identifies is responsibility. In life we receive facilities, and facilities come with responsibility. Vedic culture emphasizes responsibility given whatever facility one has; modern culture emphasizes increasing facility.
- **His conclusion:** when we read, we should be thinking about how we can render the best service. This will not be easy — it was not easy for Arjuna even after hearing the whole Gita, since fighting Bhishma and Drona remained difficult. His formulation: it is a tradition of accepting pain in order to serve others, and it continues. One should reflect deeply on what one can do to uplift the people around one, since everything is interconnected; where the thought process is selfish, one cannot help.

### 2. Does the sun-god's influence extend to the higher planets? [96:00 – 98:00]

- **Question:** Does the sun's influence extend to the higher regions, or only to this earth?
- **Answer:** This planet is the field of action; elsewhere one reaps the results of action. The teacher points to the Fifth Canto, where after describing the cosmology, Shukadeva Gosvami addresses why beings move up and down — one goes up to exhaust pious credit, and in the hellish regions receives a token of the punishment. Fresh karma is created here, in the human realm.
- Asked a further question of cosmological detail, the teacher says plainly that he has not studied the cosmology at that level.

### 3. Does intelligence indicate anything about a child's future devotion? [96:00 – 100:00]

- **Question:** If unwanted progeny has nothing to do with intelligence, then a child being sharp-minded is no indication that he will become a devotee. How should parents assess this?
- **Answer:** Correct — it has no connection with intelligence. Parents try to train children intellectually and simultaneously try to give them devotional impressions.
- Commenting on a child at a very young age is not easy; what manifests later reflects the whole cumulative background.

### 5. Why does dedication come so readily in material life and not for Krishna? [100:00 – 106:00]

- **Question:** We jump when a superior at work asks for something, while the Lord's instruction, seen everywhere and with our own eyes, does not produce the same response.
- **Answer:** The teacher says it is actually simple, and insists on looking at the root. His comparison is treating a headache, then body ache, then a bitter taste one by one — when the actual cause is a fever, and treating the fever resolves all three.
- **The root here:** we are not attached to the boss. We are attached to the salary. If the same person stopped paying, the response would stop.
- **His practical prescription** has two parts. Personal sadhana — reading, hearing, chanting — for which there is no alternative. And dedication: where one's time, energy, and resources are actually going, because that is where attachment follows. If everything goes only to the family, attachment naturally settles there.
- **His guarantee:** do not wait for conviction before acting. Begin directing time, energy, and resources toward the Lord's service and conviction will follow — he states this explicitly as a guarantee. Increase it gradually without measuring how much it has grown, and the Lord's reciprocation will be experienced.
- **The diagnostic he offers:** we constantly upgrade material life — the vehicle, the possessions, the salary — but the contribution to the Lord never gets upgraded. He suggests someone try it for a month or six months and observe what happens.
- He adds, regarding the extreme examples of dedication in scripture, that one should not be alarmed by them; the point is not to do everything at once, but to keep increasing wherever one can.

### 6. Should I keep a waterless Ekadashi fast if I cannot manage it? [106:00 – 108:00]

- **Question:** A participant describes attempting a waterless fast, sleeping four hours during the day as a result, and completing her rounds but finding the whole day compromised — whereas with some liquid she can fast comfortably and complete all her duties.
- **Answer:** The teacher treats this as the body's own indication.
- **The principle:** _upavāsa_ means dwelling near the Lord, so in any practice the main goal must be achieved — here, absorption in the Lord. Fasting is prescribed because heavy eating diverts energy into digestion and spiritual activity then lacks focus.
- **Therefore:** fast in the manner that suits your body. If the stricter fast forces sleep during the day and obstructs chanting, it is not good. Taking water or lemon juice — whatever the body basically requires — and chanting more is far better.
