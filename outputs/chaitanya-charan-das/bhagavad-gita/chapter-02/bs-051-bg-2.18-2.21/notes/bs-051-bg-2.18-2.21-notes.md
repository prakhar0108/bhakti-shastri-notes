## Shloka-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Bhagavad-gita 2.18 [00:00 – 08:00]

<!-- vedabase:2.18 -->

**Context.** The body is bound to end, but the embodied soul within is indestructible and immeasurable — therefore, Kṛṣṇa says, fight `00:00`.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Aprameya_ — immeasurable: not subject to material measurement, both because the soul is so small and, more importantly, because it is spiritual `02:00 – 04:00`.

#### How the preceding leads to "therefore fight" [00:00 – 02:00]

- The body is bound to be destroyed and the soul is indestructible.
- So Arjuna will not be able to save the body even if he avoids fighting, and will not be able to destroy the soul even if he fights.
- Therefore: don't work yourself into a frenzy of anxiety thinking you can save the body or destroy the soul — you can do neither.

#### Born with a death sentence [02:00]

- Prabhupāda explains: the body may perish today or after a hundred years — it is only a question of time.
- Material existence is a prison in which all of us carry a death sentence; we just don't know when it will be carried out.
- Like a product with an expiry date, the body has one — we just don't know the date.

#### Immeasurable, not dimensionless [02:00 – 04:00]

- Right after describing the soul's tiny size in the previous purport, Kṛṣṇa calls it immeasurable.
- The soul does have dimensions, but they are spiritual, not material.
- Connection with indestructibility: Bhīṣma lay on a bed of so many arrows that one could hardly put a finger between them, yet none touched the soul — the soul is too subtle for any weapon `04:00`.

#### Instructional and informational statements [04:00 – 08:00]

- Scriptural statements have different tones:
  - **Informational / educational** — e.g. "there is a soul in the body and the wise do not lament"; this carries only an indirect suggestion.
  - **Instructional** — direct calls to action: 2.14 ("tolerate") and 2.18 ("fight").
- For practitioners (_sādhakas_), instructional statements are the most important practically; informational ones help us understand Kṛṣṇa and the philosophy.
- **Doctor analogy:** a doctor may explain a lung infection — its cause, course and effects — but what the patient most needs is "take this medicine; do this."

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Since no one can save the perishable body or destroy the immeasurable soul, anxiety is pointless; and among scripture's statements, the direct instructions — like "therefore fight" — are the most practically important for a practitioner. [00:00 – 08:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.19 [08:00 – 12:00]

<!-- vedabase:2.19 -->

**Context.** One who thinks the soul is the slayer, or thinks it is slain, does not understand: the soul neither kills nor is killed `08:00`.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Kartā_ — the doer; the "I am the doer" mentality, to be discussed at 3.27 and in the fifth chapter `10:00`.

#### How can Arjuna not be the killer? [08:00 – 10:00]

- That the soul is not killed is clear. But Arjuna will shoot the arrows — isn't he the killer?
- The statement must be understood in context: the previous verse said the body is perishable and its destruction by nature's course cannot be avoided.
- The separation of soul and body at death was unavoidable; thinking "I am the killer" reflects the mentality "I am the doer."

#### Not a licence for violence [10:00]

- This does not mean anyone can kill and say "I'm not the killer, no one is killed."
- Scripture tells us not to commit violence; Prabhupāda will quote this in a later verse.
- Kṛṣṇa is speaking of the overall pattern of nature and of Arjuna's specific duty as a _kṣatriya_: flinching from duty would not change nature's course, in which bodies suffer and die.
- Prabhupāda says killing anyone's body without authority is abominable and punishable both by the laws of the state and by the law of the Lord.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The soul neither kills nor is killed because the body's end is inevitable — but this applies to duty performed under authority, never as a licence to kill indiscriminately. [08:00 – 10:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.20 [12:00 – 56:00]

<!-- vedabase:2.20 -->

**Context.** A frequently quoted verse: the soul is never born and never dies; it has not come into being and will not come into being; it is unborn, eternal and not slain when the body is slain `12:00`, `52:00 – 54:00`.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Aja_ — unborn: not "non-existent," but never having entered existence at a particular time `12:00`.
- _Neti neti_ — "not this, not this": the Upaniṣadic method of philosophical negation `14:00`.
- _Ontological_ — pertaining to the nature of existence, to reality as it is `16:00`.
- _Anātmā_ — the Buddhist "no-soul" idea `24:00`, `28:00`.
- _Pre-existence_ and _afterlife_ — existing before birth and after death; together they make up reincarnation `34:00`.

#### Unborn means beyond time [12:00 – 14:00]

- When something comes into existence at a particular time, it must also exit at a particular time.
- The soul never enters the time domain; it is transcendental, always eternal.
- Prabhupāda's translation — the soul has not come into being and will not come into being — means the soul exists beyond the dimension of time `52:00 – 54:00`.

#### The method of negation [14:00 – 16:00]

- Philosophical seekers introspect: "Am I this body? No. Am I the air? No…" — by negating the material, one paves the way for affirming the spiritual.
- Kṛṣṇa uses this method: whatever characterises the body — birth, death, measurability, divisibility — does not characterise the soul.
- Arjuna has conflated (mixed up) body and soul; Kṛṣṇa unmixes them.

#### Metaphorical versus ontological soul [16:00 – 20:00]

- Most traditions have a concept of the soul, but often a hazy one.
- In the Abrahamic religions (Judaism, Christianity, Islam), as the teacher presents them, the soul is mostly a metaphorical reference to our non-material essence, not a literal entity.
- The Gītā and Vedic literature treat the soul as an ontological, higher-dimensional entity — an actual conscious being.
- "Love God with all thy soul" uses soul metaphorically — put your whole essential being into it.
- Devotees also use such language: "put your heart and soul into service" — the heart here means the seat of emotions (not the biological heart), and the soul means the seat of consciousness, one's whole being; the teacher links this to Kṛṣṇa's call to serve with all one's emotions (10.8, 15.19) `20:00`.
- Such conventional usage — "my soul," "I put my soul into that project" — is not wrong, even though philosophically "I am the soul."

#### Abrahamic and dharmic traditions [20:00 – 22:00]

- **Abrahamic religions** are history-centred: specific events such as Moses leading the people to the Promised Land, Jesus' appearance and resurrection, or Muhammad's coming.
- **Dharmic religions** (Buddhism, Jainism, Hinduism, Sikhism) also have historical events, but focus on understanding the universal principles of _dharma_ and harmonising with them.

#### Buddhism: reincarnation without a soul [22:00 – 30:00]

- As the teacher presents it, Christianity accepts a soul but not reincarnation; Buddhism accepts reincarnation without a soul.
- The Buddhist view: the sense of "I" is a residue of a flowing stream of consciousness that continues into the next life — but then what reincarnates?
- Why the Buddha rejected the Vedas: people were justifying meat-eating with the Vedas, reducing their broad programme to material gratification; he said "forget the Vedas."
- Without the Vedas it is hard to know the Absolute Truth clearly, so his teaching was eminently practical, avoiding metaphysics:
  - Asked whether he was a god, an angel or the Supreme, he said "I am awake."
  - Asked whether the soul is the same as or different from the body, he stayed silent; he later told a disciple the question was asked on a platform of ignorance, so any answer would reinforce it `24:00 – 26:00`.
- His focus was morality — right thought, right words, right action — bringing people to goodness so they could later receive Vedic wisdom; in that sense a pre-theistic path, not explicitly atheistic `26:00`.
- Later Buddhist thinkers imported atheism and the rejection of the soul, partly to establish philosophical autonomy from the Vedic tradition `26:00 – 28:00`.

#### The candle flame and the continuous observer [28:00 – 32:00]

- Buddhist example: a candle flame looks the same but is constantly changing, and passes to another candle — so consciousness only appears continuous.
- The teacher calls this a misapplied example. The _Bhāgavatam_ (Eleventh Canto) uses it to show that the **material world** is constantly changing — matter is always in flux.
- A torch whirled quickly looks like a circle of fire because of persistence of vision; similarly the material world appears continuous.
- But there must be a continuous observer who sees things as continuous.
- The soul is the locus — the root — of consciousness. In the candle example the candle is the source of the flame, but the body is not the source of consciousness; being material, it only expresses the soul's consciousness.
- Moods and thoughts change, but I, the observer and source of consciousness, remain the same `32:00`.

#### Reincarnation and the Christian tradition [32:00 – 36:00]

- The teacher says reincarnation was originally accepted in the Christian tradition and is implicitly referred to in the Bible — e.g. prophets considered reincarnations of earlier prophets.
- **The man born blind:** the disciples asked Jesus whether the man's blindness was due to his own misdeeds or his parents'. Jesus said neither — it was to show God's glory — and healed him.
- What Jesus did *not* say matters: he did not reject the idea that the man could have done wrong. Since a person cannot do anything wrong in the womb to be born blind, that possibility implies pre-existence.
- So reincarnation is not incompatible with Christian wisdom, though it is not accepted today `36:00`.

#### How reincarnation was banished [36:00 – 40:00]

- The teacher refers to a book, _The Reincarnation Controversy_, by a scholar devotee, describing how the idea was removed from Christian belief through a web of "theopolitics" (politics connected with religion).
- His account:
  - Early Christians were persecuted by the Romans for the first few centuries.
  - Around the fourth century, amid internal decay, the Roman Emperor adopted Christianity as the official religion to restore basic morality.
  - Tragically, Christians then persecuted others — heathens, Jews and those deemed heretics — through inquisitions; a thinker named Bruno, the teacher says, was burned alive for teaching the immortality of the soul.
  - The emperor's focus was political expediency: telling people they had many chances would make them lax in following the law (seen as God's law, with the emperor as God's representative), so "one life, or eternal hell" was preferred.
  - Through church councils, passages supporting reincarnation were purged or downplayed; Origen — second only to Augustine among early Christian thinkers — was declared anathema and his teachings on reincarnation rejected `40:00`.

#### The cricket scoreboard [40:00 – 48:00]

- Without reincarnation, the world's inequities cannot be explained: why is one born blind, another sighted; one rich, another poor?
- **Analogy:** a match begins with one team on 200 and the other on 0. Two explanations:
  - **The scoreboard works by chance** — then a batsman hits a six and his score becomes minus six; play would be pointless. This is a "doctrine of helplessness."
  - **The organisers fixed the match** — then God is biased and arbitrary; it is hard to love such a God, and many prefer atheism to an arbitrary God.
- **The third alternative:** it is the second innings, and the lead was earned in the first. Likewise, one is born wealthy because of good deeds in a previous life `46:00`.
- There is a controller, but He oversees laws, not arbitrary choices; we get results according to how we act.
- This understanding is **sanity-restoring** (there is order, not chaos or caprice) and **hope-engendering** (by acting well now I can create a brighter future) `48:00`.
- Thinking ourselves mere lumps of matter to be destroyed reduces life to meaninglessness.

#### The semi-eternal soul [48:00 – 52:00]

- Many Christian denominations, the teacher says, hold that the soul is created at conception, has one lifetime, and then goes either to God or to eternal hell.
- This raises questions about God's kindness: what of those born where they never hear of Jesus, or those who lived before him?
- **Train conversation:** a Christian fellow-traveller insisted Jesus is the only way. The teacher asked:
  - Why kill animals despite "Thou shalt not kill"? — He said it refers to humans and condemned abortion as murder.
  - What happens to aborted souls who never heard of Jesus? — "They are innocent, so they go back to God."
  - Then, if the goal of life is to go back to God, wouldn't aborting everyone guarantee it, while letting them be born risks hell? — exposing the logical flaws of the one-life idea.
- Even an ordinary parent gives children more than one chance; what to speak of the supremely compassionate Supreme Parent `52:00`.
- Many theologians hold eternity to mean "no end" but with a beginning; the Gītā's "unborn" refutes this `52:00`.

#### Repetition and the Upaniṣadic parallel [54:00 – 56:00]

- Prabhupāda explains that repetition indicates importance: the soul's indestructibility is repeated so it is clearly understood.
- He quotes a remarkably similar verse from the _Kaṭha Upaniṣad_: the last two lines are the same, and only one line differs — where it says the soul is conscious, has the capacity to discriminate — a word Kṛṣṇa will use later in this chapter in the section on the steady-minded person.
- The soul does not undergo the six changes of the body; it is eternally youthful.
- **Two birds analogy:** on one tree, one bird pecks at the fruits — happy with good fruit, miserable with bad; the other calmly observes. The eating bird is the soul trying to enjoy through the body; the observing bird is the Supersoul. If the soul turns to the Supersoul and surrenders, it becomes free from all miseries and is reinstated in its eternal life `54:00 – 56:00`.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The soul is unborn — beyond time, a real conscious being, not a metaphor or a passing stream; with reincarnation, life's inequities make sense under a just controller, which restores sanity and gives hope. [12:00 – 48:00]
</Callout>

### Bhagavad-gita 2.21 [56:00 – 58:20]

<!-- vedabase:2.21 -->

**Context.** Kṛṣṇa again calls the soul imperishable and asks, rhetorically, who could be its killer `56:00`.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Rhetorical question_ — a way of continuing and repeating the point of 2.19 `56:00`.

#### Who kills whom? [56:00]

- Who is the killer and who is killed? The _ātmā_ is eternal and the body destructible, so no one can kill the indestructible soul — there is no killer.
- In this way Kṛṣṇa emphatically drives home that the soul is eternal.

#### Where the argument goes next [56:00 – 58:00]

- 2.13 introduced the soul as a specific being; the following verses establish it as indestructible.
- From the next verse Kṛṣṇa will further emphasise the soul's indestructibility and speak of its future — how the soul gets its next body `58:00`.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Through a rhetorical question Kṛṣṇa seals the point of 2.19: since the soul cannot be killed, there is no killer of the soul. [56:00]
</Callout>
