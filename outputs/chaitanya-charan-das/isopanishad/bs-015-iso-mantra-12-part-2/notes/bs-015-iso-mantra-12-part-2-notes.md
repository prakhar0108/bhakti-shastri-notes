### The Vedic master plan [00:00 – 06:00]

<!-- vedabase:iso/12 -->

**Context.** The teacher uses a chart to show how the different parts of scripture move people progressively upward `02:00`.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Karma-kāṇḍa_ — the Vedic section for people interested in material things and sense gratification, but ready to pursue them under scriptural guidance `02:00`.
- _Jñāna-kāṇḍa_ — the section of the Upaniṣads `02:00`.
- _Bhakti_ — the subject of the _Bhāgavatam_ `02:00`.

#### Three stages of scripture [02:00]

- **Vedas (_karma-kāṇḍa_):** people seek material things through religion — a step forward. They develop faith in God and scripture, and their consciousness slowly rises towards goodness.
- **Upaniṣads (_jñāna-kāṇḍa_):** then they ask what use these material forms are — attraction to heavens, to beautiful-looking people — all temporary; give them up. The Upaniṣads are transitional: they take people from material form towards spiritual form.
- **_Bhāgavatam_ (_bhakti_):** the conclusion.

#### The number line [04:00]

- Material forms are the negative axis, spiritual form the positive axis, and the Upaniṣads sit at the zero position — taking people from negative to positive.
- While focusing on negating material forms, some verses may sound impersonal, but the final teaching of the Upaniṣads leads towards personalism.
- That personal conclusion is emphasised most in the _Bhāgavatam_, which describes _bhakti_, spiritual form, spiritual activities, spiritual personality and spiritual relationships.
- _Māyāvāda_ is a subsection of impersonalism; the teacher will look at how it was produced, how it became twisted in modern times, and what disasters it causes `04:00`.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Scripture is a graded path — from material forms, through the Upaniṣads' negation of matter, to the Bhāgavatam's spiritual form and relationships; the negating stage is a transition, not the destination. [02:00 – 04:00]
</Callout>

### The basic idea of māyāvāda [06:00 – 08:00]

**Context.** The teacher explains how _māyāvāda_ divides reality and scripture into two levels `06:00`.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Vyāvahārika_ — practical reality; the realm where form and variety exist, according to _māyāvāda_ `06:00`.
- _Pāramārthika_ — transcendental reality; according to _māyāvāda_, actual reality has no variety, only consciousness `06:00`.

#### Two levels of scripture [06:00 – 08:00]

- In this view, wherever scripture speaks of form, personality and relationship, that is lower-level teaching; the confidential teaching of scripture is the higher level.
- So scripture is divided into two divisions — higher-level and lower-level reality — with most of scripture placed at the lower level and only a few verses at the higher.
- Śaṅkara is the proponent of _māyāvāda_.
- This creates a system of reasoning by which any scriptural words or quotations that contradict the idea are relegated to the lower level — a very self-serving system, in which contradictions are conveniently rejected `08:00`.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Māyāvāda keeps its conclusion by sorting scripture into "higher" and "lower" reality and assigning every statement about form, personality and relationship to the lower — a self-serving way of escaping contradiction. [06:00 – 08:00]
</Callout>

### The history: Buddha and Śaṅkarācārya [08:00 – 24:00]

**Context.** The teacher traces the historical flow of impersonalism as part of what he calls the Lord's historical master plan `08:00`.

#### Buddha's emergency program [08:00 – 12:00]

- At that time there was indiscriminate, unrestricted animal killing in the name of _yajña_.
- Buddha stopped it; when told the scriptures sanction it, he rejected scripture and taught about higher reality without reference to scripture — right thought, right speech and so on `10:00`.
- He created a platform by which people could come to proper understanding.
- Buddhism spread very rapidly. Historically, the _brāhmaṇas_ had also become very materially minded and were imposing their superiority on others, so many people from the lower castes turned to it; at one time large parts of India were Buddhist `10:00 – 12:00`.
- Over time, to establish their own intellectual autonomy, Buddhists produced arguments and books explicitly criticising the Vedas; a temporary, emergency program to check animal slaughter became a wholesale program against the Vedas `12:00`.

#### Śaṅkarācārya's transitional conclusion [12:00 – 16:00]

- Lord Śiva appeared as Śaṅkarācārya, refuted Buddhism and brought India back into the Vedic fold.
- He could not give the complete conclusion of the Vedas at that time, so he gave a transitional conclusion.
- Conceptually, Buddha's _śūnya_ and Śaṅkara's Brahman are intellectual cousins: Śaṅkara essentially changed the terms within the Vedic framework.
- By teaching the impersonal level, he gained widespread acceptance of the Vedas and Vedic culture; people were not then ready to hear about the higher level of reality.
- **Historical purpose:** to check the spread of Buddhism and bring people back into the Vedic fold `14:00`.
- **Scriptural basis:** the _Padma Purāṇa_ describes Lord Viṣṇu telling Lord Śiva to come down and preach impersonalism, and Lord Śiva telling Pārvatī about this teaching `14:00`.
- Impersonalism, brought by Śaṅkarācārya, is within the Vedic fold, but it is transitional; made final, it becomes a blunder `14:00`.
- It also serves as a convenient cover for those who want to defy God in a "cleaner," religious way: they can say "I am God," because ultimately the Absolute is formless `14:00 – 16:00`.
- Its conclusion: material form is false and we must go beyond it — but what lies beyond is not clearly described `16:00`.

#### The movie analogy [16:00]

- In the first half of a movie, the villain keeps beating the hero; at the end, the hero wins.
- If someone takes one scene from the middle, where the villain is beating the hero, and says "this is the teaching of the movie," that is wrong — we must watch the movie through to its conclusion.
- Similarly, the Lord's historical master plan spans multiple centuries: starting from Buddha, who got people to follow basic morality and stop animal slaughter, then teaching about Brahman (similar to _śūnya_), and onward `16:00 – 18:00`.

#### The later ācāryas [18:00 – 22:00]

- **The soul–body example:** the soul and body are one and different — the body cannot exist without the soul and comes from it, yet they are different. Likewise we have a relationship of oneness with the Supreme Lord, but there is also difference. The teacher calls this simple yet very profound, and leaves it for later `18:00`.
- **Madhvācārya** countered Śaṅkarācārya: there is actually _bheda_ — dualism; it is not that everything is one `20:00`.
- **_Viśiṣṭādvaita_** brought things back towards the centre and balance `20:00`.
- **The highest understanding:** the relationship between the Lord, Brahman and the _ātmā_ is _acintya-bhedābheda_ — inconceivable oneness and difference `20:00`.

#### Who decides what is "lower"? [20:00 – 22:00]

- The problem the teacher highlights (citing Rāmānuja): if we resolve things this way, we are calling the majority of scripture unreal — imposing our own philosophy on scripture `20:00`.
- On what basis do we divide scripture like this? Scripture nowhere says "these verses are higher-level reality, those are lower" `22:00`.
- We have to find out how the truth of every scriptural statement can be seen; saying "this is real and this is unreal" imposes our philosophy, rather than hearing what scripture wants to say `22:00`.
- Buddha taught that everything is zero; Śaṅkarācārya pointed to everything being one — the problem lies in stopping there `22:00`.

#### Progressive revelation [24:00]

- What is in scripture is revealed progressively by the Lord through the great ācāryas — a progressive evolution in understanding.
- With Caitanya Mahāprabhu and the Gosvāmīs, the highest understanding is revealed.
- The problem comes when a transitional understanding is taken as the conclusion.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Buddha's and Śaṅkarācārya's teachings were scenes in a longer movie — the Lord's plan to bring people from animal slaughter back to the Vedas and onward; judging the whole by one middle scene mistakes a transition for the conclusion. [08:00 – 24:00]
</Callout>

### Philosophical problems of māyāvāda [30:00 – 38:00]

**Context.** After a stretch of the class that is not legible in the captions (`26:00 – 28:00`), the teacher examines the logical difficulties of dividing scripture into real and unreal `30:00`.

#### The epistemological problem [30:00 – 32:00]

- Do we understand everything about God? We cannot — for example, how we are simultaneously one with and different from God.
- The basic principle: God is revealed through scripture, and we accept it as true even when we cannot fully understand it right now.
- Interpretive methods may be required to understand scripture, but ultimately saying that parts of scripture are false is a different matter.
- A simple example: the _jīva_ and Brahman are the same in quality, different in quantity `30:00`.
- At the philosophical level, the exact relationship is not easy to grasp; scriptural statements may seem contradictory. The attributes of the Absolute Truth are not easy for the finite human mind to understand — that is the glory of the Absolute: it is _acintya_, inconceivable `32:00`.

#### "Indescribable" as an escape [32:00]

- _Māyāvādīs_ first say everything is Brahman; then logical problems arise, and the response is that it is _anirvacanīya_, indescribable.
- For example: if everyone is Brahman and you become liberated, what happens to me? What does liberation mean? There are many such logical problems.

#### Right about matter, wrong about form [34:00 – 36:00]

- Recalling the master plan: _karma-kāṇḍa_ is the negative axis, _jñāna-kāṇḍa_ the intermediate zero point, spiritual form the positive.
- Impersonalists are right in saying material form and material variety are unreal; they are wrong in saying the rejection of form is the ultimate truth.
- Simply by removing form one does not become unlimited; matter is always limited, with or without form.
- To become unlimited, we have to go beyond matter, not remove form: spirit is unlimited, and spirit can have form and still be unlimited `36:00`.
- Kṛṣṇa has a form so small He can sit on His mother's lap, yet when He opens His mouth there, the whole universe is contained in it — limited and unlimited at the same time `36:00`.

#### Where the reasoning goes wrong [36:00 – 38:00]

- **The shared starting point:** material forms and varieties lead to material desires — so many good-looking objects; enjoy one, then want another; desires keep multiplying, and desire is the cause of bondage.
- **The _māyāvāda_ conclusion:** "I have material desires because there are forms, so to be free from desire I must give up all forms and variety."
- **The correction:** to become free from the material, I must give up material desire and infatuation with material forms and variety — but there are spiritual forms and variety that attract the soul and help it become spiritually advanced `38:00`.
- When the transitional is considered final, it becomes very dangerous for future spiritual advancement `38:00`.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Impersonalism is right that material forms are unreal and bind us through desire, but wrong to conclude that all form is unreal — spirit can have form and still be unlimited, as Kṛṣṇa on His mother's lap shows. [34:00 – 38:00]
</Callout>

### Modern māyāvāda: a "khichri" [38:00 – 50:00]

**Context.** Prabhupāda is very strongly critical of modern _māyāvāda_: it is a deviation from the Vedic scriptures — a transitional teaching made into the conclusion `38:00`.

#### The ingredients [38:00]

- The teacher calls modern _māyāvāda_ a "delicious khichri":
  - **The grains** — impersonalism, which has a scriptural basis in the sense that the Upaniṣads' transitional teachings contain it, though distorted;
  - **The sugar** — social service, taken from Christianity;
  - **The spice** — nationalism, from the Indian independence movement.
- It became poisonous because of envy of Kṛṣṇa `38:00`.

#### Decline and colonisation [38:00 – 42:00]

- Historically, Rāmānujācārya, Madhvācārya, the Gosvāmīs and Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa refuted _māyāvāda_ very strongly `38:00`.
- Later, the _kṣatriya_ culture grew weak and the brahminical culture became corrupted; the economic and social health of the people declined — lower castes were exploited, there was weakness — and these were among the factors that let Europeans conquer India `40:00`.
- The teacher clarifies: India was not a poor country — the Europeans came because it was wealthy; poverty in an agricultural society was never perpetual. He means poverty of culture and philosophical understanding: the culture remained, but it was no longer pure devotion, mixed with ritualism without understanding `40:00`.

#### The sugar of social service [42:00 – 46:00]

- With colonisation came Christianity, and missionaries offered social service, material benefits and human rights, drawing people to convert `42:00`.
- The response: "Why should Hindus become Christians? Let us keep them Hindu by encouraging Hindus to do social service" `42:00`.
- This took shape during the pre-independence Bengali Renaissance: Bengal was where British rule first began, the first region strongly influenced by British culture, and the response of Bengali intellectuals led to what is called modern Hinduism `42:00`.
- Slogans arose such as "service to man is service to God" and service to the poor as service to Nārāyaṇa `42:00`.
- Serving the poor is good and part of Vedic culture — a king's duty when people are starving — but it is not exactly devotional service, though it can become devotional with proper consciousness `42:00 – 44:00`.
- An attack on devotional culture followed, with the idea that we must come to a humanitarian level. Humanitarian work is important, but not the ultimate goal; the teacher will discuss social service in a future class `44:00`.
- Impersonalism made social service not only equal to service of God — since Brahman is in the temple and Brahman is in the poor — but even higher: seeing Brahman in the heart of the poor was claimed to be higher than seeing Brahman in the temple `44:00 – 46:00`.

#### The spice of nationalism [46:00 – 48:00]

- Many spiritual teachers wanted a united India and to drive away the British.
- Religion was strong in India, so people could be united under its banner — but in the name of religion people were divided: some worshipped Kālī, others Gaṇeśa, Śiva, Viṣṇu, and so on.
- An impersonal idea offered a way to unite them, with the goal of driving away the British `46:00 – 48:00`.
- In this process, the spiritual conclusion of scripture — the personal God, Bhagavān Kṛṣṇa — was lost; commentaries on the Gītā did not glorify Kṛṣṇa, because Kṛṣṇa as its centre was not politically expedient `48:00`.

#### The envy of Kṛṣṇa [48:00 – 50:00]

- When such ideas are made to seem spiritual, what is truly spiritual becomes lost for most people.
- For the soul, surrender to Kṛṣṇa is glorious, because through it eternal love and eternal happiness are attained; but _māyāvāda_ says there is no need to surrender, because ultimately you are God.
- Modern _māyāvāda_ has become so cheap: "I have not come to tell you that I am God; I have come to tell you that you are God" — the ultimate flattery for conditioned souls `48:00 – 50:00`.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Modern māyāvāda blends a transitional impersonalism with social service and nationalism, and is made poisonous by envy of Kṛṣṇa — replacing surrender to Him with the flattering idea that "you are God." [38:00 – 50:00]
</Callout>

### The disastrous consequences of māyāvāda [50:00 – 60:00]

**Context.** The teacher concludes with what happens when _māyāvāda_ is taken as the conclusion `50:00`.

#### No God to surrender to [50:00]

- _Māyāvāda_ deprives the soul of the nature of _bhakti_: if I am God, how can I devote myself to God?
- Without devotion, I do not get the higher taste; "higher happiness" remains a concept, not an actual experience.
- I claim to understand the highest reality — "I am Brahman" — and think I am religious, but without spiritual happiness I am attracted to lower pleasures.

#### Spiritual reality made unattractive [50:00 – 52:00]

- What will I do when I go back? The only thing to do is nothing; the only thing to say is nothing; the only thing to think is nothing.
- Social leaders' contributions can be appreciated at their level; but when they are presented as spiritual — even as the conclusion — there is no higher taste, and one gravitates down to materialism `52:00`.
- So _māyāvāda_ makes material life more attractive than spiritual life: the goal is merging into Brahman, and what does one do after that? It is not exciting; out of frustration with material life people may be drawn to it temporarily, but with no positive vision they come back to material life `52:00`.

#### The sick patient [52:00 – 54:00]

- If I am very sick with a stomach problem and every moment brings pain, I want to become free from pain.
- But is that the perfection of my life? The goal is not just to be free from pain but to become joyful.
- _Māyāvāda_ is like that: it goes beyond material things, and then there is nothing; impersonal realization is not attractive, and knowing nothing positively attractive in the spiritual realm, one comes back `54:00`.

#### The Indian paradox [54:00]

- People in India are religious and yet materialistic at the same time — because _māyāvāda_ does not give them a higher spiritual taste, they are dragged down to material taste.

#### "Everything is in the mind" [54:00 – 56:00]

- At a public lecture in London, someone said that good and bad are ultimately only in the mind: if your mind is strong, nothing is bad; if weak, nothing is good.
- The teacher's response: when death comes, it will be painful for everyone attached to the body; merely thinking everything is good will not change my attachment to the world.
- We need a process of purification; without it, just imagining "I am God" does no good to anyone `56:00`.

#### The bhakti revival [56:00]

- Many Indian _māyāvādīs_ and others all over the world have become devotees.
- Prabhupāda's books strongly, logically and scripturally counter _māyāvāda_.
- A remarkable _bhakti_ revival is happening: people all over the world are building temples and worshipping deities, because of Prabhupāda's books.

#### Summary [58:00 – 60:00]

- Impersonalism is not final; it takes people from material form towards spiritual form, and in between it says "don't be attracted to form, personality and relationship" — to detach people from material conditions. It is transitional.
- Modern _māyāvāda_ dismissed the higher teachings of _bhakti_, and when logical problems arose, used the excuse of the indescribable.
- In the historical master plan — Buddha's _śūnya_, Śaṅkarācārya — making only one part the conclusion is like taking the scene where the hero is being beaten; we have to go through the whole progression for complete understanding.
- Modern _māyāvāda_: grains of impersonalism from the Vedic scriptures, spice of nationalism from the independence movement that wanted a united India, sugar of social service from Christianity, and envy of Kṛṣṇa `58:00`.
- It makes material life more attractive than spiritual life; people miss the highest taste of _bhakti_ — "I think I am Bhagavān, so why worship Bhagavān?" — and while claiming to be religious become more materialistic, worshipping themselves and imagining they are God `60:00`.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
By denying a God to surrender to and a spiritual world worth going to, māyāvāda leaves the soul without the higher taste of bhakti — so religious people slide back into materialism; only real purification and devotion fill that gap. [50:00 – 60:00]
</Callout>
