## Mantra-Wise Notes & Analytical Commentary

### Overview of Śrī Īśopaniṣad [00:00 – 04:00]

**Context.** Before Mantra 1, the teacher gives an overview of the whole book. As the name indicates, the Īśopaniṣad is centred on the _Īśa_ or _Īśvara_, the Supreme Lord `00:00`.

#### The five sections [00:00 – 02:00]

1. **Invocation – Mantra 3** — live in harmony with the Īśvara.
2. **Mantras 4 – 8** — to obey someone I must know Him; spiritual vision enables us to understand the Īśa.
3. **Mantras 9 – 11** — right and wrong _vidyā_ (knowledge) for understanding the Īśvara.
4. **Mantras 12 – 14** — right and wrong _upāsanā_ (worship) of the Īśvara.
5. **Mantras 15 – 18** — prayers to the Īśvara to reveal Himself.

#### Head, heart and mercy [00:00 – 02:00]

- Developing spiritual vision has two parts: cultivating proper knowledge, and developing the right disposition of the heart.
- Understanding the Lord is not just an intellectual exercise — we offer both head (section 3) and heart (section 4) to Him.
- Ultimately it is up to the Lord to reveal Himself, so we pray for His mercy (section 5).

#### The first section in detail [02:00 – 04:00]

- **Invocation** — everything is perfect, _pūrṇa_.
- **Mantra 1** — the actual beginning of the Īśopaniṣad: everything belongs to the Īśvara. The first step to seeing everything as perfect is seeing that everything belongs to Him; this is the _īśāvāsya_ principle, the underlying, unifying principle of the book (also called the Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad).
- **Mantra 2** — how living in harmony with it benefits us.
- **Mantra 3** — how not living in harmony with it harms us.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The Īśopaniṣad can be remembered in five steps — obey the Lord, know Him, offer Him our head through right knowledge, our heart through right worship, and pray for His mercy to reveal Himself. [00:00 – 04:00]
</Callout>

### Mantra 1: everything belongs to the Lord [04:00 – 08:00]

<!-- vedabase:iso/1 -->

**Context.** The teacher reads the mantra and explains its two halves `04:00`.

#### What the mantra says [04:00 – 06:00]

- The first line states that everything animate and inanimate within the universe is owned and controlled by the Lord; the same idea of the Lord owning the world appeared in the Invocation.
- The second line emphasises how to live in that universe: we may enjoy — but only what is given by Him, our allotted quota.
- We must not encroach on what is not ours, knowing to whom it belongs: it belongs to the Īśvara, and what is allocated as someone else's quota I cannot take and enjoy.

#### Scripture does not deprive us of happiness [04:00 – 06:00]

- The scriptures ultimately aim to give spiritual happiness, but even at the material level they do not ask us to give up all happiness.
- We can enjoy even materially — by accepting what the Lord gives.

#### Overview of the purport [06:00 – 08:00]

- Paragraphs 1–2: the glory of Vedic knowledge, a brief introduction to the Īśopaniṣad (the book proper begins here).
- Paragraphs 3–4: the Lord is the proprietor of everything — an analysis of the first two lines.
- Then examples of the Lord's allotted quotas:
  - quota in nature — animals take their quota and nothing more;
  - quota in human possession of the earth — how we should follow it, and how we presently do not;
  - paragraphs 9–10: quota in our diet.
- Conclusion: live according to the _īśāvāsya_ principle, and we will be able to live properly.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Mantra 1 does not forbid enjoyment; it places enjoyment within the Lord's ownership — enjoy what He allots, and never encroach on what belongs to others. [04:00 – 06:00]
</Callout>

### The Īśopaniṣad within the Vedic canon [08:00 – 16:00]

**Context.** The first purport paragraphs introduce the Īśopaniṣad as one of the prominent Upaniṣads `08:00`.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Upaniṣad_ — "to sit close by": the seer and seeker sit together, the seeker at the seer's feet, and through questions and answers the deepest truths are revealed `14:00`.
- _Brāhmaṇa_ (literature) — distinct from _brāhmaṇa_ (one _varṇa_) and from Brahman (the Absolute, generally used for the impersonal aspect); here it means a section of the Vedic literature `12:00`.
- _Āraṇyaka_ — from _araṇya_, forest: literature studied in the forest `14:00`.

#### The principal Upaniṣads [08:00]

- Eleven Upaniṣads are considered very important among the 108; each name has a significance related to its content.
- E.g. the Praśna is centred on a question; the Bṛhad-āraṇyaka is "big" — comparatively large.
- The Īśa Upaniṣad is very small: 18 mantras.

#### Commentaries and the course approach [08:00 – 12:00]

- Recalling the first class: a school establishes that its teachings are Vedic by commenting on the Upaniṣads (_śruti_), the Bhagavad-gītā (_smṛti_) and the _Vedānta-sūtra_ (_nyāya_); in the Gauḍīya line, Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa commented on all three.
- Being so important, the Īśopaniṣad has been commented on by many great ācāryas; Prabhupāda wrote elaborate purports on it even before founding ISKCON.
- In this course the focus is on Prabhupāda's purports — to understand his mood and the flow he develops "in a masterly way" — with only occasional references to previous ācāryas.
- Previous ācāryas' commentaries will be used extensively when studying the Bhagavad-gītā; learning goes step by step.

#### The four Vedas and their four parts [12:00 – 16:00]

- The four Vedas: Ṛg, Yajur, Sāma and Atharva.
- Each has four parts:
  - **_Saṁhitās_** — the primary parts.
  - **_Brāhmaṇas_** — e.g. the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa; meant to start directing the seeker from _karma-kāṇḍa_ to _jñāna-kāṇḍa_, from material religiosity toward transcendence.
  - **_Āraṇyakas_** — take the metaphysical quest further.
  - **_Upaniṣads_** — where the metaphysical quest becomes central; often abstruse, because they are meant for those who like to speculate.
- The Yajur Veda has two parts, the Kṛṣṇa (black) and the Śukla (white); the Īśopaniṣad is the last part of the Śukla Yajur Veda, preceded by the Bṛhad-āraṇyaka and, before that, the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa.

#### Prayers of Manu [16:00]

- The Īśopaniṣad is essentially prayers offered by Manu to Yajña; similar prayers, with a similar verse, appear in the Eighth Canto of the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam.
- The Upaniṣads are revelations from a higher reality received and spoken by sages, but they are not considered the sages' words: they are transcendental sound, and the sages are channels for receiving and transmitting it.
- The purport also recalls the four defects, which is why we need a transcendental source of knowledge coming from the Lord.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
The Īśopaniṣad is a small but principal Upaniṣad at the close of the Śukla Yajur Veda — transcendental sound received by sages — and the course studies it chiefly through Prabhupāda's purports to grasp his mood and flow. [08:00 – 16:00]
</Callout>

### The Lord is the proprietor of everything [16:00 – 24:00]

**Context.** Proprietorship fascinates people — conquerors like Alexander wanted to rule the whole known earth. How do we understand logically that God is the proprietor of everything? `16:00`

#### Scriptural logic: energy belongs to the energetic [18:00]

- Prabhupāda cross-references Bhagavad-gītā 7.4–5: the material elements — the teacher lists earth, water, fire and so on up to intelligence and ego — are one energy, and the soul is another, superior energy; both are the Lord's energies.
- Speech is the energy of the speaker, coming from and controlled by him — loud or soft as he wishes.
- Everything is matter, spirit or a combination; both belong to the Lord; therefore everything belongs to Him.

#### Experiential logic: our ownership is transitory [18:00 – 20:00]

- A house may be in my name — for a few years of life; then to descendants; then an invading country or natural devastation may take it all.
- Our proprietorship is illusory in the sense that it is transitory and can be arbitrarily taken away; we have limited, partial control.
- In a kingdom the king owns everything and farmers are subordinate owners whose land he can take back; similarly the Lord owns everything and we are secondary owners — tenants.
- We are not even owners of our bodies; as a tenant can be evicted from a house, we are evicted from the body at death.

#### Nature comes from somewhere [20:00 – 24:00]

- "I don't see God owning nature; nature is just there." But everything must have a source.
- "Everything came by chance" misuses the word _chance_:
  - If a bridge collapses, a structural engineer cannot say it collapsed "by chance" — he should have designed it to withstand natural forces.
  - _Chance_ applies where there is a set of possibilities, each with a probability — a die falling on six has a one-sixth probability.
  - Used as a causal principle without such a pattern, "chance" just covers up ignorance.
- Everything comes either from something or from nothing; we have no experience, in daily life or history, of something from nothing.
- What that "something" is lies beyond modern science, whose theories of origin are largely unproven speculations; scripture tells us it is God, Kṛṣṇa.
- Logic cannot prove that Kṛṣṇa is God, but it can establish that everything comes from something that still owns and controls everything.

#### "In the care of" [24:00]

- Devotees often say a thing is "in the care of" someone rather than owned: "It's not that I own this; Kṛṣṇa has entrusted it to my care, and I should keep it carefully."

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Since matter and spirit are both the Lord's energies and our hold on anything — even our body — is temporary, we are tenants and caretakers; the true proprietor is the source from whom everything comes. [16:00 – 24:00]
</Callout>

### Quota in nature: the cow [24:00 – 28:00]

**Context.** If everything belongs to God, may we take nothing? No — all living beings take, but each according to its quota `24:00`.

#### Every being has its quota [26:00]

- Prabhupāda's example: the cow gives milk but does not drink it; she eats grass and straw, and her milk is designated as food for humans.
- A lion will not eat grass even when starving; a cow will not eat flesh. When modern people distort nature by feeding flesh to cows, diseases such as mad cow disease can result.

#### "Cow's milk is meant only for calves" [26:00 – 28:00]

- The cow gives more milk than the calf can healthily drink.
- A cow is not a petrol tank holding a fixed quantity; she is a living, loving person. When warmly cared for and secure, her metabolism produces abundant milk — the teacher notes devotee communities seeing milk increase when previously neglected cows were cared for.
- The calf takes milk at first and is gradually weaned; the remaining milk is used by humans.
- Taking cows in fearful, unhealthy, artificial conditions as the norm, and concluding their milk is not for others, is wrong — our experience is limited.
- Prabhupāda concludes that such is the Lord's arrangement, and we should be satisfied with what He has kindly set aside for us `28:00`.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
In nature every creature lives within its quota; the cow, well cared for, gives abundant milk beyond her calf's need — showing an arrangement in which we should be satisfied with what the Lord sets aside for us. [26:00 – 28:00]
</Callout>

### Quota in human possession of the earth [28:00 – 44:00]

**Context.** Prabhupāda first gives the example of our own dwelling, then moves to the global level: the conflict between communists and capitalists `28:00`.

**Key Terms Explained by the Teacher**

- _Bhū-deva_ — "gods on earth", a name for _brāhmaṇas_ (_bhū_ = earth, _deva_ = godly being) `34:00`.
- _Nara-deva_ — _kṣatriyas_: human beings who are like gods, representatives of God among humans `36:00`.
- _Śāstra_ and _śastra_ — scripture, and weapons `36:00`.

#### The carpenter and the industrialist [28:00 – 30:00]

- All the ingredients of my house come from nature, which I do not own; so what is made from them also belongs to the Lord.
- If we give a carpenter the wood and nails and he makes a table, manipulating the ingredients does not make him the owner.
- Prabhupāda once asked an industrialist who produced glass where glass comes from — silica, from nature, from Kṛṣṇa: "Your factory is Kṛṣṇa's factory; calling it yours, you are a thief." When the embarrassed man said he also gave donations to Kṛṣṇa, Prabhupāda smiled: "Then you are not a big thief, you are a small thief."
- If we do not acknowledge God's authority, we are encroaching on His property and can be penalised.

#### Communists and capitalists [30:00 – 32:00]

- The Cold War was a prominent background fear after the Second World War, until communism collapsed in the USSR at the end of the 1980s.
- Both sides fought on the assumption that the world belonged to them. Prabhupāda sarcastically says they face each other and snarl like cats and dogs, each head of state announcing that he will annihilate the other.
- Both claimed to be right; both were wrong, because both were godless.

#### How the īśāvāsya principle is administered: varṇāśrama [32:00 – 38:00]

- God is at the top; then the four _varṇas_ — _brāhmaṇas_, _kṣatriyas_, _vaiśyas_ and _śūdras_ — and those outside regulations, called outcasts, by quality rather than birth.
- **_Brāhmaṇas_** — by quality, not birth; they study, teach and live _śāstra_ and so stand closest to Kṛṣṇa, the highest Brahman (as Arjuna acknowledges in the tenth chapter of the Gītā). They are so fixed on God that they are almost anomalies on earth. They give principles but do not get entangled in practical management.
- **_Kṣatriyas_** — the rulers and administrators; the _brāhmaṇas_ say what is right through _śāstra_; those who do not follow it are punished by the _kṣatriyas'_ _śastra_; they create policies to care for all of society.
- They get honour and power but publicly and privately remain devoted to God — e.g. at the Ratha-yātrā even today the King of Jagannātha Purī sweeps the road before Lord Jagannātha's cart, publicly showing his subordination to the King of kings.
- When the king says, "Nothing belongs to me; everything belongs to God; I am the caretaker," everyone follows his example.
- Thus Kṛṣṇa's will was taught by the _brāhmaṇas_ and implemented by the _kṣatriyas_. "Everything belongs to God" does not mean no one may hold property; it means an evolution of consciousness in which people are not possessive or greedy.

#### Comparing the three systems [38:00 – 44:00]

- **Communism** — "everything belongs to the people":
  - imposes artificial material equality, so the talented are not rewarded — like a classroom where everyone gets the same marks: the talented stop studying and the lazy stay lazy;
  - the world's resources, evenly distributed, would give adequate means but not prosperity, so most people live in scarcity;
  - an exploitative mentality arose — as George Orwell satirised, "all are equal but some are more equal than others" — with administrators prospering while others starved;
  - Prabhupāda would say spiritual communism, where everything belongs not to the state but to God, is the perfection of communism.
- **Capitalism** — wealth goes to whoever is intelligent and industrious enough, and wealth determines position and power:
  - the talented are rewarded, but the wealthy exploit the needy;
  - cut-throat competition breeds insecurity;
  - everyone, rich or poor, is greedy for money and so dissatisfied.
- **Varṇāśrama** — acknowledges God's supreme proprietorship and integrates absolute spiritual equality with relative material diversity:
  - as souls, all are equal children of God with a right to live — a principle the teacher compares to the American Declaration that all are born free with a right to life and the pursuit of happiness;
  - the Vedic culture implements it realistically, not idealistically: we are spiritually equal but materially different, so each contributes by his talents and nature, works diligently and happily, and society gets maximum benefit;
  - _vaiśyas_ hold property, _kṣatriyas_ take taxes, _śūdras_ labour, and all live cooperatively, knowing everything belongs to the Lord and working for their spiritual mission — God-realisation — while caring for bodily necessities.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Communism and capitalism fail because both are godless; varṇāśrama implements the īśāvāsya principle by uniting spiritual equality with material diversity, under rulers who see themselves as the Lord's caretakers. [30:00 – 44:00]
</Callout>

### Quota in diet: from vegetarian to prasādarian [44:00 – 58:00]

**Context.** Prabhupāda then discusses right dietary choices — natural resources in terms of what we eat `44:00`.

#### Human anatomy and the HELP argument [44:00 – 46:00]

- Non-vegetarian food is often thought standard, but human anatomy has no protruding jaws or sharp nails for killing and devouring animals — we need slaughterhouses and butchers — and our digestive system resembles that of vegetarians. The teacher mentions he has written about this in detail.
- "Help the world through your eating":
  - **H**ealth — our health is better on vegetarian food;
  - **E**nvironment — stays better;
  - **L**ivestock — animals are not killed;
  - **P**oor — land used to grow fodder could grow several times more grain; land that feeds one non-vegetarian can feed between 3 and 15 vegetarians.

#### "Plants have life too" [46:00 – 50:00]

- Plants do feel pain, but there are differences between killing plants and killing animals:
  - **Scientific** — plants have a less developed nervous system and feel much less pain; a sensitive person causes as little pain as possible.
  - **Natural** — crops left unharvested simply dry and die; fruits fall from the tree, meant for consumption. Animals are often raised in artificial conditions, artificially fattened, and brutally killed in their prime. Prabhupāda would say that if someone must eat meat, let him wait for the animal to die naturally.
  - **Intuitive** — harvest is jubilant and festive; a slaughterhouse is horrific, with screaming animals, blood everywhere and grave faces. No parent objects to a school trip to see a harvest; every parent objects to one to a slaughterhouse.
- It is all about degrees, and the difference is huge — hence vegetarian food is much preferable.

#### Canine teeth and regulated meat-eating [50:00 – 52:00]

- Prabhupāda's deeper point is not merely to be vegetarian but to eat according to our quota.
- "We have canine teeth, so we are omnivores": if so, our teeth would all be canine; ours are few and underdeveloped. At most meat would be a small part of the diet; historically most people lived on grains, fruits and vegetables, and mass industrial slaughter is modern.
- In Vedic culture, meat-eating was allowed for those at a lower level of religion, but regulated: e.g. one worshipping Goddess Kālī could sacrifice a goat only on the new-moon day, keep one of six parts and give the rest in charity, and while cutting say the word _māṁsa_ — "as I am now, so this one will become": in future the goat will slaughter me.
- At higher levels of religion meat-eating was discouraged and even rejected.

#### Prasādarian, not "Kṛṣṇatarian" [52:00]

- Our quota is what God has arranged for us through nature: we are neither vegetarian nor non-vegetarian but, in Prabhupāda's word, prasādarian.
- Some say "Kṛṣṇatarian", but the teacher notes Prabhupāda never used that word in his recorded writings, and it is slightly inappropriate: vegetarians eat vegetables, so "Kṛṣṇatarians" would eat Kṛṣṇa — whereas we eat Kṛṣṇa's prasādam.

#### Acknowledge and love the Lord [52:00 – 56:00]

- The perfection of life is not merely less karmic entanglement — vegetarian food still involves some — but to acknowledge and love the Lord.
- Many cultures offer thanks before eating, e.g. Christians say grace; in Vedic culture we go further and offer the food to the Lord, understanding He is the actual enjoyer.
- In the third chapter of the Gītā, Kṛṣṇa says that those who eat the remnants of sacrifice become free from sinful reactions: offering food with mantras to the Deities is a _yajña_, and the all-pure Lord purifies it of karmic reactions.
- Prasādam is not just karma-free but bhakti-filled — filled with the devotion of Rādhārāṇī and the Vaiṣṇava ācāryas through whom it comes down to us — so it purifies, elevates and helps us develop love for the Lord.
- The prasādam prayer is not a simple thanksgiving for survival: it says we are dragged here and there by our senses, and by giving prasādam the Lord helps us satisfy and purify our senses so we can devote ourselves to Him.

#### The teacher's summary [56:00 – 58:00]

- Overview: invocation–3 live in harmony with the Īśvara; 4–8 spiritual vision; 9–11 right _vidyā_; 12–14 right _upāsanā_; 15–18 prayer for revelation.
- Mantra 1 gives the _īśāvāsya_ principle; Mantra 2 the benefit of living by it; Mantra 3 the harm of not living by it.
- The Īśopaniṣad is the last part of the Yajur Veda; Kṛṣṇa owns everything; cows follow their quota; globally, varṇāśrama implemented the principle in society; in diet we follow the quota by becoming not only vegetarian but prasādarian — causing minimal pain to others, and acknowledging and loving the Lord.

<Callout type="idea" title="Takeaway">
Our dietary quota is not merely vegetarianism to reduce pain and karma, but prasādam — food offered to the Lord in acknowledgement and love, which purifies our senses and elevates us toward devotion. [44:00 – 58:00]
</Callout>
